Chapter 15

The Money in the Machine

“Every system has a customer. Ask who the customer is, and you will learn what the system is for.”a health economist

A kidney is not only an organ. It is also an economic event. When a patient receives one, a dialysis chair empties, a monthly Medicare payment stops, a clinic’s revenue shifts, and a stream of drug and follow-up spending begins somewhere else. None of the people involved is a villain. But when one asks why a pipeline that could grow has stayed small, the answer is not only medical. It is also arithmetic.

The 1972 bargain

In 1972 Congress extended Medicare coverage to nearly all Americans with end-stage renal disease. It was an act of moral clarity: no one in the country should die of kidney failure because they could not afford dialysis. It also created a single-disease entitlement with unusual features. Dialysis is paid per treatment. The industry that provides it consolidated over the following decades into a small number of large organizations that operate most outpatient dialysis facilities. The result was a system that does what it was designed to do, keeping patients alive on dialysis, extremely well, and that has, in structure, a financial interest in patients remaining on dialysis.

Transplant is, for most suitable patients, a better outcome than dialysis: longer life, better quality of life, and lower lifetime cost to the system. A rational system would push strongly toward transplant. For decades the incentives did not line up that way, and the share of dialysis patients who are placed on the transplant waiting list, or who receive a living donor transplant, remained far lower than medically justified, with persistent disparities. This is not a conspiracy theory. It is what financial structures produce when nobody is paid to change them.

The reforms of the last decade

The federal government has begun to change the incentives. In 2019 the Executive Order on Advancing American Kidney Health set goals for increasing transplant access and living donation, and directed new payment models. CMS tested payment models that reward dialysis facilities and nephrologists when patients receive transplants or home dialysis. In July 2025 the IOTA model began applying financial rewards and penalties to 103 kidney transplant hospitals based on transplant volume, matching efficiency and post-transplant outcomes, and requiring publicly posted living-donor selection criteria.1

The Numbers

IOTA: 6-year mandatory model · July 1, 2025 – June 30, 2031 · 103 kidney transplant hospitals · scored on achievement (up to 60 points), efficiency, and quality domains. Source: CMS; 2026 modifications finalized.

These reforms matter for living donors because transplant hospitals now have a direct financial reason to increase transplant volume, including living-donor transplants. That is a double-edged development. Done well, it means more programs investing in donor navigators, financial counseling, easier scheduling and better follow-up. Done badly, it can mean pressure to process donors faster and to loosen scrutiny. The difference will be determined by the independent safeguards of Chapter 6 and by whether the public pays attention.

Who profits from a living donor?

It is a fair question, and a guide committed to honesty should answer it. The donor is not paid. The recipient benefits. The transplant hospital is paid for the transplant, and now may be rewarded under IOTA. The surgeons are paid for their work. The recipient’s insurer, often Medicare, pays for the donor’s evaluation and surgery as part of the recipient’s costs. A dialysis provider loses a patient. Medicare saves money over time, since a successful transplant costs less per year than dialysis. In short, many institutions gain from the donor’s act, and the donor is the only party that is both unpaid and at risk. That is not an argument against donation. It is an argument for acknowledging whose sacrifice the structure rests on.

Independence and why it matters

This is a place where the Cold Ischemia Foundation’s independence is not decoration. When an organization advising donors and patients is funded by dialysis companies, pharmaceutical manufacturers or hospital systems, its advice is shaped, consciously or not, by those funders’ interests. We accept no money from any of them, and we encourage readers to ask the same question of any advocacy organization, including ours: who pays for this, and what do they want?

Counterpoint

“Industry partnerships fund research and support services.” They do, and many do good work. The concern is not that every industry-funded organization is corrupt, but that the structure creates pressure, and that patients are entitled to know about it. The best test is simple and applies to everyone: disclose funding, and be willing to criticize your funders.

Say It Plainly

When a system pays for dialysis and not for the harder work of getting people transplanted, it gets dialysis. Change the payment, and you change the system. That is happening. Watch it closely.
