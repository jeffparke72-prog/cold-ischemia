Chapter 8

Who Pays: The Economics of Giving

“Follow the money, and you will find the donor standing at the end of the line, holding a receipt.”a hospital finance officer, off the record

A hospital billing system does what billing systems do. It sorts costs into columns: the donor’s evaluation, the donor’s surgery, the recipient’s surgery, the recipient’s drugs. The donor’s costs are billed to the recipient’s insurer. The line labeled “donor lost wages” does not exist. The system is not malicious. It is doing exactly what it was built to do, and it was not built for her.

The medical bills: who pays what

In the United States, the donor’s evaluation, surgery and hospital stay are generally billed to the recipient’s insurance. For Medicare-covered recipients, that means Medicare, which has special rules treating the donor’s costs as part of the recipient’s transplant cost. For privately insured recipients, it means the recipient’s plan. The donor does not usually receive a bill for the surgery, and this works well in the typical case. The common failures happen at the edges: a test done before the recipient is formally approved, a follow-up visit after the standard window, a complication that arises long after the transplant, a recipient whose insurance changes. A donor who is billed erroneously should not pay; they should call the program’s financial coordinator and, if necessary, the recipient’s plan, and keep a written record.

The non-medical costs: the real gap

The costs that fall on donors are mostly non-medical. Lost wages during recovery, which for many people is four to twelve weeks depending on the job. Travel to the transplant center for the evaluation, which can take two days and multiple trips, and for surgery and follow-up. Lodging. Meals. A caregiver’s time off. Child care and elder care. These costs can run from a few hundred dollars for a local donor with generous leave to many thousands for a distant donor with none.

Federal policy has long recognized the problem. The National Living Donor Assistance Center, funded by HRSA, reimburses eligible donors for travel, lodging and meals associated with evaluation, surgery and follow-up, and, since the 2020 rule, can also reimburse lost wages and child- and elder-care expenses for donors who lack other financial support. Reimbursement is generally prospective: an approved applicant receives a controlled-value card to pay covered expenses, for themselves and up to two accompanying persons, up to a set maximum. Eligibility rules prioritize donors and recipients with lower incomes and have changed over time, so verify current criteria on the NLDAC website before relying on them.1

The Numbers

NLDAC has described reimbursement of up to roughly $6,000 in prospective expenses per approved applicant, covering hotel, travel and meals for evaluation, surgery and follow-up, with preference historically given to households at or below 300 percent of the federal poverty guidelines. These figures and criteria change. Confirm on livingdonorassistance.org.

What the hospital earns, and what it doesn’t

Transplant programs are economically significant for hospitals. A transplant center earns revenue from the recipient’s transplant, from a continuing stream of follow-up care and from associated services. The donor generates costs, absorbed by the recipient’s insurance, and, from the program’s point of view, a quality metric. Programs are judged by transplant volume and by recipient outcomes. Donor outcomes beyond the first two years are, in practice, nobody’s performance measure. That asymmetry does not make programs unethical. It does explain why donor-friendly features such as donor navigators, lodging, pre-approved leave letters and long-term follow-up depend so heavily on the individual program’s priorities.

The new incentive structure: IOTA

In July 2025 the Centers for Medicare & Medicaid Services launched the Increasing Organ Transplant Access (IOTA) Model, a mandatory six-year payment model for 103 kidney transplant hospitals, running through June 30, 2031. Participating hospitals are scored on three things: the number of kidney transplants they perform, how efficiently they match and use organs, and how well their patients do after transplant. High performers earn payments; low performers pay penalties. Hospitals must also post publicly their criteria for selecting living donors.2 In 2026 CMS finalized adjustments to the model.

IOTA is significant because it places a financial thumb on the scale for transplantation, including living-donor transplantation, for the first time at scale. Whether it will also improve donors’ experience depends on how hospitals respond. A hospital can raise its numbers by building a better program for donors, or by recruiting and screening more aggressively. A watchful public, and an alert potential donor, can tell the difference.

Counterpoint

The worry: payment tied to transplant counts may pressure programs to push donors through. The reply: OPTN consent rules and the independent advocate exist to guard against that, and transparency requirements make selection criteria visible. The honest answer: both are true. Incentives change behavior, usually in intended ways and sometimes in unintended ones, and the independent monitoring of donor experience is the necessary counterweight.

A donor’s budget

Before you commit, build a one-page budget. List every cost you can foresee: lost wages in weeks, the daily rate of any partial pay, the number of trips and miles to the center, nights of lodging, meals, child care, elder care, and the costs for the person who will care for you. List every source of coverage: employer leave, short-term disability if any, state programs, NLDAC, the recipient’s insurance, support from family and community. Subtract. If the gap is more than you can bear, you have a financial problem to solve before your evaluation, not after. Programs have social workers and financial coordinators whose job includes helping you do this; use them.

Say It Plainly

No donor should leave the hospital poorer for having saved a life. Until that is true in law, it is your job to plan as if it is not.
