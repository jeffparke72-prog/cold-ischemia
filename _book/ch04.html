Chapter 4

The Federal Architecture

“To understand a rule, ask who wrote it, who enforces it, and who can be hurt by it.”a regulator’s rule of thumb

You are sitting across from a transplant coordinator. She says, in a kind voice, “We’re required to do this,” and slides a form across the table. You sign. You do not ask who required it. That small silence, repeated millions of times in American health care, is where people lose their grip on how the system actually works. This chapter gives it back to you.

Living donation is governed by a stack of authorities, each with its own author and its own enforcer. Knowing which layer a rule belongs to tells you how firm it is, who can change it, and where to complain.

Layer one: the statute

The foundation is the National Organ Transplant Act, as amended, in Title 42 of the U.S. Code. NOTA created the Organ Procurement and Transplantation Network and the Scientific Registry of Transplant Recipients, forbade the sale of organs, and authorized federal support for living-donor-related expenses. A later provision, Section 377 of the Public Health Service Act, authorizes the reimbursement of living donors’ travel and subsistence expenses, and that authority is the basis of the National Living Donor Assistance Center.1

Congress also passed the Organ Donor Leave Act in 1999, raising the paid leave available to federal employees who donate an organ from seven days to thirty days a year, separate from annual and sick leave.2 That is the only federal guarantee of paid leave for living donors in the United States, and it covers federal employees alone.

Layer two: the regulations

Statutes are general; regulations supply the detail. The OPTN operates under 42 CFR Part 121, which sets out how the network is organized and how its policies bind members. Transplant programs that take Medicare must also meet the Conditions of Participation in 42 CFR Part 482, including specific requirements for transplant centers covering patient and living donor selection and patient and living donor rights (§§482.90 and 482.102 among others).3 These are federal rules enforced through CMS and state survey agencies, and a program that violates them risks its Medicare certification.

In the Statute

Who enforces what. The OPTN writes and enforces allocation and membership policy (through HRSA oversight). CMS certifies transplant programs and enforces Conditions of Participation. States license hospitals and regulate insurance. The Department of Labor enforces the Family and Medical Leave Act. HHS’s Office for Civil Rights enforces HIPAA and non-discrimination laws. A single living donor can touch all of them.

Layer three: OPTN policy

Between statute and daily practice sits the thick layer of OPTN policy. These are rules adopted through the network’s committee process and binding on its members. For living donors, the key material is Policy 14, Living Donation, which sets requirements for informing and evaluating potential donors, for the independent advocate, for the psychosocial evaluation, and for reporting. Related requirements on data submission and follow-up appear elsewhere in the policy manual.

OPTN policies matter because they reach into the hospital in ways the statute never could. They tell a program what it must tell you, who must meet you, and what it must report to the government after you leave. They are also where much of the practical protection of living donors lives. And they can change faster than statutes. In 2016 the OPTN modified the informed consent requirements for potential living donors; in 2021 it extended living-donor policy to include donors of other organs and vascularized composite tissue.4 It pays to look up the current policy rather than rely on a pamphlet.

Layer four: the program’s own protocols

Below the policies are the individual programs’ protocols: the specific tests they require, the thresholds they apply, the committees that decide. This layer varies. One program may accept a donor with well-controlled hypertension and another may decline. Neither is necessarily wrong. But a donor deserves to know the criteria, and under the IOTA model participating hospitals will have to post their living-donor selection criteria publicly, a change that moves this layer into the open.

The oversight layer, and its current upheaval

For nearly forty years a single private contractor operated the OPTN under HRSA oversight. A 2023 federal law ended that arrangement, and during 2025 HRSA moved to implement it: establishing an independent, elected OPTN Board of Directors, separating functions among multiple vendors, and taking over direct collection of patient registration fees.5 Whatever one thinks of the reforms, the consequence for patients is that the rule-making body and its processes are in transition. When you read an OPTN policy, check its effective date and look for pending changes.

Where the cracks are

The stack looks orderly. In practice, a living donor falls through the gaps between layers. The OPTN regulates what a transplant program must do for you, but not what your employer or your life insurer may do. CMS covers the recipient’s transplant, and thereby much of the donor’s evaluation and surgery, but it does not guarantee care for a complication ten years later. The Department of Labor says donors may qualify for FMLA leave, but only if they work for a covered employer for long enough. NLDAC reimburses expenses, but it is an application program with eligibility rules, not an entitlement. Each layer does its job. No layer owns the donor.

Counterpoint

“Isn’t it better to keep the system flexible?” Some flexibility is essential, because medicine moves fast and rules written in stone become obsolete. The objection is not to flexibility; it is to flexibility that always works in favor of the institution and rarely in favor of the person bearing the risk. Rules should be easy to improve and hard to evade.

Say It Plainly

When someone says “we’re required to,” ask: by whom? A good coordinator will tell you. A good donor learns to ask.
