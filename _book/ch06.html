Chapter 6

Consent, Coercion, and the Advocate

“Yes is only a gift if no was a real option.”a transplant ethicist

She says yes at the kitchen table, before anyone has asked a question. Her brother is on dialysis, her mother is watching, and the pause that follows is the longest she has ever heard. Later, in a quiet room at the hospital, a person she has never met asks whether anyone is pressuring her. She hesitates, then says no, because she cannot imagine saying anything else. The scene is composite, and it is the one the law is built to catch.

The central ethical problem of living donation

Almost every other medical procedure carries a risk that the patient accepts for their own benefit. Living organ donation is different. The donor accepts risk, small but real, with no medical benefit to themselves, for the sake of someone else. The ethical justification rests entirely on one thing: that the donor’s choice is informed, voluntary, and theirs. Everything in the regulatory architecture around donation is an attempt to protect that voluntariness against the forces that threaten it: family pressure, guilt, financial need, the wish to be good, and the institutional appetite of a transplant program that benefits from a transplant.

What the rules require

OPTN policy addresses this directly. Programs must give potential living donors specific information before they agree, a list that has grown over the years to include the medical, surgical, psychosocial and financial risks of donation, the possibility of long-term complications, the donor’s right to stop at any point, and the fact that the program’s decision to accept or decline them will be kept confidential. In 2016 the OPTN modified the informed consent requirements, and in the years since has issued guidance for programs on how to carry them out.1

The same policy framework requires each donor to be offered an Independent Living Donor Advocate (ILDA), a person who is not involved with the recipient’s evaluation, is independent of the decision to transplant the recipient, and whose job is to ensure that the donor’s interests are represented. Under the requirement, for any living kidney donor undergoing evaluation, the recovery hospital must designate and provide an ILDA. The requirement extends to living donors of other organs. The ILDA assesses whether the donor meets the standards of informed consent and whether their decision is free from coercion.2

The psychosocial evaluation is a separate requirement. It must be performed by a clinical social worker, psychologist or psychiatrist and includes an assessment of mental health, the ability to make an informed decision, the ability to cope with stress, and, specifically, whether the decision to donate is free of inducement, coercion and other undue pressure.3

In the Statute

What you are entitled to. (1) Information about risks, benefits, alternatives and financial implications, before you commit. (2) An independent advocate who does not work for the recipient’s team. (3) A confidential decision: if you change your mind, or are found ineligible, the program must handle that without disclosing your reasons to the recipient. (4) The freedom to withdraw at any point, up to the moment of surgery. These are summaries of OPTN policy; check the current text of Policy 14 and your program’s implementation.

What happens when the process works

When the system works, it is a quiet marvel. A potential donor meets a nurse coordinator, then a nephrologist, a surgeon, a social worker, a dietitian, and an advocate who asks, in private, whether this is really what they want. The advocate is not trying to talk them in or out of it. The advocate is the one person in the building whose only client is the donor. If the donor wants out, the program offers a medical reason that need not be true, so that the recipient never learns it was a refusal. Many donors have said that this gentle exit is what made it possible to say yes.

Where it breaks

The failures are not usually dramatic. They are structural. The advocate is typically an employee of the same hospital, and in a busy program may be a social worker or nurse with other duties, trained on the job. The independence the policy requires is organizational, not financial, and independence inside an institution is always partial. Some advocates are excellent; some are an administrative checkbox. Programs have an incentive to keep donors moving and, in a context of volume-based incentives, increasingly strong reasons to count transplants. None of this implies bad faith. It does mean that the safeguards depend on the culture of the individual program, and donors should treat them that way.

There is also the problem of the soft coercion no policy can detect. A relative who says nothing but sighs. A spouse whose silence communicates what is expected. A family that treats a refusal as betrayal. The ILDA can catch some of this. The potential donor must catch the rest, by taking the time to find out what they actually want before the conversation reaches the hospital.

Counterpoint

“Is the advocate too protective?” Some argue that screening for coercion can slide into paternalism: treating adults as unable to decide, or imposing delays that discourage willing donors. The fair answer is that the screening must be rigorous about voluntariness and respectful about autonomy, and that its purpose is to ensure the choice is the donor’s, not to second-guess a choice that is.

A practical protocol for the potential donor

Before any appointment, write down, privately, why you are considering this. Notice whether the reasons are yours. Notice what you would feel if you said no, and whether the answer is relief or dread. Choose one person who is not the recipient, not a relative of the recipient, and not employed by the hospital, and tell them the truth. When you meet the advocate, ask what their role is, who they report to, and what happens to what you say. Ask whether the program has ever turned a donor away because of coercion. Ask for the “graceful exit” in advance, so you know what the program will say if you withdraw.

Say It Plainly

You may say no at any time, for any reason, and you do not have to explain. The system is designed to protect that right. Use it if you need it, and do not let anyone make you feel you have wasted their time.
