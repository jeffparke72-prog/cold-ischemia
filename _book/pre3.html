Prelude Three

The Mortality You Carry

“The fact of death is the great teacher, and the great terror.”A theme of existential psychology, after Irvin Yalom

Marcus has made the decision four times. He made it in the car after the phone call. He unmade it in the shower. He remade it when his niece held up a drawing of the whole family, with Uncle Ray in the middle and a small, careful kidney drawn on his side like a medal. He unmade it again at the pharmacy, reading the side-effects on an unrelated bottle. He tells no one that what frightens him is not the operation. It is how calm his body feels about a thing his mind cannot hold still.

Every decision about donation is, underneath, a decision about three old human subjects: death, meaning and belonging. Medicine rarely says so. The evaluation asks about your creatinine and your blood pressure. It does not ask what you believe about your own finitude. But the people who go through this process, and the people who regret it, tend to remember the existential questions long after they have forgotten the lab values.

Death, the quiet participant

A living donor is asked to take a small risk of dying and a larger risk of living differently. The statistics are reassuring: the perioperative risk is on the order of a few per ten thousand, and most donors live long, healthy lives. But the mind does not weigh three in ten thousand the way a spreadsheet does. It weighs it against the people who depend on you. It weighs it against the fact that you have never had a reason to think about it. Psychologists describe this as mortality salience: when we are reminded of death, we cling to our values and our tribes, and sometimes to our rescue fantasies. A decision made at the peak of mortality salience, in a hospital hallway or the week after a relative’s diagnosis, deserves a second look in daylight.

Meaning, the strongest and most dangerous motive

Many donors describe their gift as the most meaningful thing they have done. That is a real and, for most, an honest finding: surveys of donors generally report that a large majority are glad they did it and would do it again. But a meaning-driven decision has a shadow. If your sense of self depends on being the one who gives, then a rejection (a failed crossmatch, a medical exclusion, a recipient who declines) feels like an erasure. And if your life is already short on meaning, donation can become a project you are asking a body part to carry. A mature program will ask what happens to you if the answer is no. A mature donor asks it first.

Counterpoint

“Meaning is the whole point. Why are you suspicious of it?” We are not. We are suspicious of meaning used as anesthesia. If the reason you are going ahead is that stopping would cost you your story, you are not yet free. The goal is a yes that could have been a no.

Belonging, the pressure no one names

Families are the main source of living donors, and families are also where the quietest coercion lives. A request can arrive as a plea, as a joke, as a long silence at a holiday table, or as a relative who has simply started to look at you for a beat too long. The role of the independent advocate is to give you a way out that does not cost you your place in the family: the right to say no, to withdraw at any stage, and to have the program say, on your behalf, that you are not a match, without ever disclosing that you changed your mind. Use that right if you need it. It exists because the people who built the safeguards knew how this goes.

Four honest questions to take outside

First: if no one would ever know I did this, would I still do it? The answer is not a test, but it tells you whether the audience is part of the motive. Second: what am I most afraid of, and is that fear about the surgery or about something older? Third: how will I feel if the recipient does not do well, or does not thank me, or goes back to the habits that damaged their kidneys? Many donors say that this was the hardest, and least discussed, part. Fourth: who will take care of me, and am I willing to ask? Donors are usually the caretakers. The recovery asks them to switch roles.

Say It Plainly

You can love someone and still say no. You can say yes and still be afraid. You can be afraid and still be right. A good decision is not a brave one; it is a free one.

Meeting yourself in time

One more thing. Time shapes this decision in a way that is easy to miss. The person on the waiting list is living in urgent time: months, dialysis sessions, a clock they can hear. The donor is asked to decide in human time: the pace of a life that has to be reorganized, a body that has to be examined, a family that has to be consulted. These two clocks do not run at the same speed, and a good process refuses to let the first one bully the second. Take the time you need. If anyone, including us, tells you that you must decide this week, ask them who benefits from the rush.

If you need support now

This guide is not therapy and its authors are not clinicians. If thinking about this decision, or about anything else, is making you feel overwhelmed, hopeless or unsafe, please reach out to a mental-health professional or your primary-care clinician. In the United States, you can call or text 988, the Suicide & Crisis Lifeline, at any hour. If you are in immediate danger, or someone is, call 911. Transplant programs also include social workers and psychologists whose job is to talk with potential donors; ask to speak with one at any point.
