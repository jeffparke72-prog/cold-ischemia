Chapter 23

Before You Sign: A Field Guide

“Courage is easier when you know where the exits are.”a donor coordinator

The last chapter of a book like this is not a conclusion. It is a handoff. You have read about the history, the law, the money, the risks and the ethics. Now you have to sit in a room with a form. This chapter is the one to bring with you.

Phase one: before you volunteer

Talk to the person who needs the kidney, and to someone else. Decide whether you want this freely. Learn the basics of the evaluation and risks. Tell your closest person the truth. Read the program’s published criteria, if any. Check SRTR for the program’s outcomes. Understand that you may stop at any point.

Phase two: before your evaluation

Call the program and ask the questions in the box below. Apply to NLDAC if you may be eligible, before the evaluation. Ask your employer, in writing, about leave. Ask your insurers about the effect of donation on your coverage and consider adjusting coverage before you are evaluated. Build the donor budget from Chapter 8. Arrange a friend or relative to attend the evaluation with you.

The Program Questions

Selection and process. What are your written living-donor selection criteria? How long does an evaluation take? Who is my coordinator? Who is my independent advocate, and to whom do they report? Outcomes. How many living-donor transplants do you perform a year? What are your donors’ outcomes and complication rates? Money. What will I owe? What will be covered? Who pays for complications and for how long? Can you help me apply for NLDAC? Follow-up. How long will you follow me? Who pays for annual labs? What is your follow-up completion rate? Rights. What happens if I change my mind? How will my decision be kept confidential? What is the program’s complaint process? Exchange. If I am not a match, do you participate in paired exchange?

Phase three: before you sign the consent

Read the consent form at home, not in the office. Highlight what you do not understand and ask about it. Confirm that you have met your independent advocate alone. Ask for your personal risk estimate. Ask what happens if your recipient’s transplant fails, or if you develop a complication. Ask for the program’s plan, in writing, if costs not covered by the program arise. Confirm that you have time to think. Make sure that your yes is yours.

Phase four: surgery and recovery

Prepare a caregiver and a backup, transport, time off, and a plan for children and pets. Know the warning signs that mean you should call the program. Take the recovery seriously: do not return to heavy lifting early, avoid NSAIDs, keep follow-up appointments. Accept support from the social worker and, if available, a peer.

Phase five: the rest of your life

Choose a primary care doctor who knows your history. Arrange yearly follow-up: blood pressure, kidney function and urine protein. Keep a copy of your records. Tell every future doctor and every form. Keep your weight and blood pressure healthy. Avoid kidney-stressing drugs. Join a registry or research cohort if one is available. If you develop a complication, document it, notify the program, and ask who pays. If you are ever harmed, use the channels in this book. Tell your story, if you want to, to someone who can change the rules.

Say It Plainly

You are not obligated to do this. If you choose to, you are entitled to do it with your eyes open, your costs covered, your future protected, and your voice heard. If any of those is missing, you are entitled to say so, and to wait until it is not.

A last word

This book began with an arithmetic problem: a list that grows faster than the supply. It ends with something less arithmetical. The people who give the most to this system are, in the end, individuals who made a private decision in a quiet moment, and who then entered an enormous machine built by others. The machine can be improved. It has been improved before, because people who gave, who received and who watched demanded it. If you are one of those people, or are about to become one, the Foundation’s job is to make sure the machine knows your name.

— The Cold Ischemia Foundation
Ellenton, Florida
