Chapter 13

Follow-Up: The Promise That Ends at Two Years

“We are very good at the front door of donation and very bad at the long hallway after.”a transplant social worker

Fourteen years after her donation, a woman gets a letter from a research registry asking how she has been. It is the first time in more than a decade that anyone in the system has inquired. She is fine, she reports, though she has been told she has mild hypertension, and no one has ever told her what to do about her one kidney. She holds the letter for a long time. It is not a bad letter. It is a letter from a system that remembered her, finally, by accident.

What the rules require

OPTN policy requires transplant programs to follow living donors for a minimum of two years. Since 2005 programs have been required to submit Living Donor Follow-up forms at discharge (or six weeks, whichever is earlier), at six months, at one year and at two years after donation, with thresholds for the proportion of complete, timely reports. Recovery hospitals must report donor deaths within two years, and policy calls for reporting within 72 hours of the hospital learning of one.1 Over time the OPTN has expanded the data collected, including data on later outcomes, and has issued guidance on procedures to collect post-donation follow-up data.2

That is the whole formal commitment. After two years, the system’s duty to the donor is, with minor exceptions, over.

The gap and its consequences

The significance of that boundary is hard to overstate. The risks of donation do not end at two years. The risk of kidney failure, hypertension and other complications accumulates over decades. Kidney disease, in particular, is silent for years. A donor who stops being tracked at two years has no systematic way for a problem to be caught. Programs have historically struggled even to meet the two-year standard: many donors, healthy and busy, do not return for visits, and programs have limited tools to bring them back.

Research suggests that donors who receive guideline-concordant follow-up care early do better in the long term, a finding that supports regular monitoring.3 But there is no national system ensuring that happens, no funding stream guaranteeing it, and no standard describing who pays for annual visits after the program’s obligations lapse.

In the Statute

What the minimum looks like. OPTN requires follow-up data submission and death reporting for at least two years after donation. Nothing in federal statute guarantees lifetime follow-up, lifetime coverage of donation-related complications, or a lifelong donor registry. Proposals to establish them exist in policy discussions and advocacy agendas. Verify current OPTN policy and pending changes.

Building your own follow-up

Because the system will not do it for you, you must. Arrange a primary-care physician who understands that you donated a kidney. Ask for an annual visit with a blood pressure check, a measure of kidney function (creatinine and estimated GFR), and a urine test for protein. Keep a copy of your donation records, your pre-donation labs and your surgical summary. Tell every new doctor you donated, and put it on every medical form. Avoid nonsteroidal anti-inflammatory drugs when possible, stay hydrated, keep a healthy weight, do not smoke, and treat high blood pressure aggressively if it appears. Consider enrolling in any donor registry or research cohort your program offers; the data you contribute may help the next donor.

What To Ask Before You Donate

How long will you follow me, and how will you reach me? Who pays for annual labs after the required period? If I develop a complication, which insurer covers it? Do you track donors beyond two years in any form? What is your program’s rate of completed two-year follow-up?

What former donors are owed

Donors who later need a kidney have a safeguard that is worth knowing about: under current OPTN kidney allocation policy, prior living donors receive priority on the waiting list. That is a recognition that the system owes them something. It has been used rarely, which is good news, and it is the bare minimum of reciprocity. A just system would go further: it would cover donors’ health care related to donation for life, and it would treat their long-term health as a matter of public concern. A donor who has to chase a registry to find out how they are doing has been left behind by the system they helped.

Counterpoint

“Lifetime follow-up is too expensive and too intrusive.” The cost is small relative to the cost of a single kidney transplant or a year of dialysis, and the data it would generate would improve the safety of every future donation. As for intrusion: it can be voluntary and light, a yearly check-in with a few tests. The expense is not the obstacle. Attention is.

Say It Plainly

The system will thank you at the door. It will not walk you down the hall. Walk it yourself, and demand that someday it walk with you.
