Chapter 17

The Psychology of Yes

“People do not decide to donate. They discover they already have.”a transplant psychologist

She cannot say when she decided. It was not in the waiting room, or at the dinner where her cousin told the family about the diagnosis, or on the drive home when she said nothing. It was sometime in the silence afterward, a slow recognition that the question had been answered before it was asked. This is how many donors describe it: not a decision but a discovery. The rest of the process exists to find out whether that discovery is sound.

Why people give

Motivations are mixed, and honest donors admit as much. Love is the most common: a spouse, a child, a sibling, a friend. Duty is close behind, a sense of what one owes the people one belongs to. Some give out of faith, some out of gratitude for their own health, some from a belief that having been lucky obliges them to be useful. Some give because they were asked, and found it harder to refuse than to agree. Others give from a need to be needed, or to prove something to themselves or to a family that has underestimated them. Few of these motives are shameful. All of them deserve examination, because a donation driven by guilt or by a need for approval can feel different, afterward, from one driven by clear choice.

The ambivalence that is normal

Donors are often told, or tell themselves, that doubt means they should not donate. This is wrong. Ambivalence is the ordinary state of a person contemplating a major, irreversible act on behalf of someone they love. The question is not whether doubt exists but what it says. Fear of surgery, worry about children, concern about money are practical problems with practical solutions. A feeling of dread at the thought of the recipient, or a gut sense of being cornered, is information of another kind. Programs screen for this, but the person best placed to detect it is you.

The family system

Donation never happens to an individual alone. It happens inside families with histories, alliances and silences. In some families, a single willing donor is the family hero; in others, the family’s unwillingness to say “no” to the sick person turns into pressure on whoever is healthiest. Siblings may compete over who will give; a spouse may be both the best medical match and the person with the most to lose. A donor may feel resentment toward relatives who did not step forward. The recipient may feel guilt, or anger at the loss of control, or fear that the gift will be held against them. Families that talk about this beforehand do better than those that do not. A social worker or family therapist can help, and many programs offer one.

Questions To Ask Yourself

If I could give without anyone knowing, would I still want to? If the recipient were someone I did not love, would I still offer? What would I feel if the answer were no, and how would I explain it? What would I feel if the transplant failed? Who else has a stake in this, and what would they say if they were honest? What am I afraid of, and what am I afraid of admitting?

The relationship after

The most underestimated part of donation is the relationship that follows. A gift of this size creates asymmetry. The recipient may feel indebted, or may feel entitled to guide the donor’s choices; the donor may feel responsible for the recipient’s health, with every lapse in adherence felt as a betrayal of the gift. When the transplant succeeds, the relationship may deepen. When it fails, the donor can experience grief, guilt and sometimes anger that no one prepared them for. Some relationships have strained or broken after donation. They should be talked about before, not after. Many programs recommend a conversation about what each person will do if the transplant fails, and about how the donor’s privacy will be protected.

Counterpoint

“Isn’t all this psychology just a way to talk people out of it?” It can be misused that way, and a good program is vigilant about it. The purpose of psychosocial evaluation is not to prevent donation but to make sure the person who donates is doing so freely and is prepared for what follows. Most donors say they would do it again. The few who say they would not usually point to something that was left unsaid.

Say It Plainly

The decision to donate is rarely a calculation. It is a discovery about who you are. The responsible thing is to make sure it is the discovery you want to make.
