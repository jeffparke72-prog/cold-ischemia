Chapter 21

A Donor’s Bill of Rights

“Rights written down are harder to forget.”a patient advocate

Charters are strange documents. They claim to describe what already exists, and they are written because it does not. A person who has been harmed rarely reaches for a charter in the moment; they reach for it afterward, to see if what happened was allowed. This chapter is written for the moment before. Read it as the minimum a donor should expect, and as the standard to which every program, insurer, employer and policymaker can be held.

The charter

The Cold Ischemia Foundation proposes the following rights, drawing on existing law, OPTN policy and the best practices in transplant programs. Some already exist in law; others are aspirations. Each is stated plainly so that it can be tested against real programs.

1. The right to decide freely. A potential donor may decline or withdraw at any time, for any reason, without explanation and without disclosure of the reason to the recipient. Exists in OPTN policy and program practice.

2. The right to full information. Before consenting, a donor shall receive clear, written information about the medical, surgical, psychosocial and financial risks, including long-term risks and uncertainty, in language they understand. Required by OPTN informed consent policy.

3. The right to an independent advocate. Every donor shall be offered an advocate who is independent of the recipient’s care and has no stake in the transplant. Required for kidney and other living donors under OPTN policy; independence is organizational and varies in practice.

4. The right to be made whole. No donor shall bear financial loss as a consequence of donation: evaluation, surgery, travel, lodging, lost wages, dependent care. Partly met through NLDAC and recipient insurance; not guaranteed.

5. The right to protected work. Every donor shall have job-protected leave, and paid leave where feasible, for evaluation and recovery. Partly exists: FMLA by Department of Labor guidance; Organ Donor Leave Act for federal employees; some state laws. Federal legislation pending.

6. The right to insurability. No donor shall be denied or surcharged for life, disability or long-term care insurance because they donated. Not guaranteed; Living Donor Protection Act pending.

7. The right to lifelong care. Donors shall have access to follow-up care for life, with donation-related complications covered without cost. Not guaranteed; formal OPTN follow-up lasts two years.

8. The right to priority if needed. A donor who later requires an organ shall receive priority. Exists in OPTN kidney allocation policy; confirm current text.

9. The right to privacy. A donor’s medical information and decision shall be confidential from the recipient and the public. Protected by HIPAA and program policy.

10. The right to the truth. Programs shall publish their living-donor volumes, selection criteria, outcomes, drop-out rates and follow-up completion, and shall report honestly to donors what they know and do not know. Partly met; the IOTA model now requires participating hospitals to post selection criteria.

11. The right to be heard. A donor shall have access to a fair, timely, independent process for complaints, and a voice in how the system is governed. Complaint channels exist; donor representation in governance is limited.

12. The right to support. Donors shall have access to counseling and peer support before and after donation, whatever the outcome. Varies by program.

How to use it

Take the charter into your first conversation. Ask the program which of these rights it honors, and how. Ask your employer, your insurer, your elected representatives. A program that welcomes the questions is a program worth trusting. A program that bristles may be telling you something. And if a right is not yet in law, that is not a reason to abandon it. It is the agenda of the chapters that follow.

Counterpoint

“A charter without enforcement is rhetoric.” That is true, and the Foundation does not pretend otherwise. A charter is a standard, and standards become law when enough people measure institutions against them in public. Nearly every right on the list began as someone’s complaint.
