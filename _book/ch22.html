Chapter 22

The Reform Agenda

“Every reform is a promise someone has to keep.”a legislative aide

A bill sits in a committee. Behind it is a coalition of patients, donors and advocates who have visited legislators for years, with stories, spreadsheets and a patience that nobody who has not tried it can fully appreciate. The bill is unglamorous. It says that a person who gives a kidney should not lose their job or their insurance for doing so. If it passes, no one will notice. That is how you know the reform worked.

The agenda in brief

The following proposals are the Foundation’s own reading of the problems in this book. They are grouped by the kind of change required. Each is framed so that its supporters and critics can argue about specifics.

1. Make donors whole, automatically

Replace application-based reimbursement with automatic coverage of donation-related costs, including lost wages, travel, lodging, and dependent care, without income tests, funded at a level that meets demand. Pair it with a simple prospective process so a donor knows what will be covered before committing. This builds on the 2020 rule and the National Living Donor Assistance Center and removes the means-testing that makes reimbursement an uncertain gift in its own right.

2. Pass the Living Donor Protection Act

Enact the federal protections now moving through Congress: bar life, disability and long-term care insurers from discriminating against donors, and codify job-protected leave under FMLA for donors in private and federal employment. Extend leave protections to workers at smaller employers. Consider a federal paid-leave floor modeled on the existing thirty days for federal employees. The bill was reported out of the Senate committee in February 2026; the remaining step is a floor vote and the House.1

3. Guarantee lifetime follow-up and coverage for complications

Extend OPTN follow-up requirements beyond two years, create a national living donor registry with voluntary lifelong participation, and establish a federally backed guarantee that donation-related complications are covered for life without cost to the donor. Fund annual follow-up visits through the recipient’s coverage, a dedicated fund, or both. A donor should never have to prove that a complication arose from donation to receive care.

4. Strengthen the independent advocate

Require that independent living donor advocates report outside the transplant program’s administrative chain, receive standardized training and certification, and publish aggregate, anonymized reports of their activity. Give donors a right to a second, outside consultation at no cost. The advocate is the one protection built specifically for donors. It deserves real independence.

5. Publish donor-centered data

Require every program to publish annually: the number of donor inquiries, evaluations started and completed, donors approved and declined, withdrawals and the reasons, surgeries, complications at discharge, six months, one year and two years, longer-term follow-up completion, and out-of-pocket costs reported by donors, broken down by race, income and geography. Treat donor drop-out as a performance measure. IOTA’s publication of living-donor selection criteria is a start, and it should be extended to outcomes.

6. Add donors to governance

Ensure the new OPTN Board and its committees include living donors and former donors with voting power, and that patient and donor representatives are not outnumbered by the entities they oversee. The 2025 restructuring created an elected Board; the question now is who is on it.

7. Align payment with donor welfare

Under IOTA and any successor models, tie financial rewards not only to transplant volume but also to donor experience measures: completion of follow-up, reported financial burden, complications and donor-reported satisfaction. A hospital should not earn a bonus for transplants that left donors worse off.

8. Invest in prevention and in deceased donation

The living donor is an answer to a shortage that other measures should shrink. Invest in kidney disease prevention and early detection, in reducing organ discard, in improving deceased donor conversion, and in increasing referral to transplant. A living donor system must not be a substitute for a functional deceased donor system.

9. Keep the line on payment, and move the argument forward

The Foundation does not advocate legalizing payment for organs. It does advocate exhausting every measure that would remove the costs of donation first, and encouraging the research and pilot evaluation that would let the country settle the compensation debate with evidence, not slogans.

The Ask, In One Sentence

No donor should lose money, work, insurability or health care for the act of saving a life, and the system should be required to prove it.

How a reader can act

Read the bills. Write to your representatives, with specifics. Share your own story or someone else’s, with permission. Ask your transplant program whether it publishes its numbers. Volunteer with organizations that keep the pressure on. Document what happens to you or someone you know, with dates and names, in the Foundation’s tools or elsewhere; documented experience is the evidence that policy changes require.

Counterpoint

“This is too much, too fast, too expensive.” The program costs are small relative to the savings from each additional transplant, since a successful kidney transplant costs the health system substantially less than years of dialysis. Staging is reasonable. What is not reasonable is waiting another decade to start.

Say It Plainly

The agenda is not radical. It is the minimum consistent with calling the system a system of donation.
