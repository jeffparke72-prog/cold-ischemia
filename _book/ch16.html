Chapter 16

Who Watches the Watchers

“A system that grades itself will give itself good marks.”a congressional staffer, to the author

A federal investigator opens a file and finds what investigators often find: a rule, a deadline, and a gap between the two. The file is a composite; the pattern is not. A system built over forty years, run by a contractor, overseen by an agency, scrutinized by Congress in fits and starts, has always depended on the quality of the people inside it. The question of what happens when they falter is the question of oversight.

The organizations

The OPTN, the national network, is a public-private partnership: the government contracts for its operation and HRSA oversees it. The Scientific Registry of Transplant Recipients, maintained by a separate contractor, publishes outcome data on transplant programs and organ procurement organizations, and its public reports are the best tool a patient has for comparing programs.1 CMS certifies transplant programs and OPOs and evaluates them under federal performance standards. States license hospitals and regulate insurance. Congress oversees them all, mostly through hearings and letters.

Why the structure has been criticized

For years, critics from inside and outside the field raised the same concerns about the OPTN’s structure: that a single contractor both made policy and operated the system, that governance was dominated by the members the network was supposed to oversee, that data were not transparent enough, that patient safety reporting was slow, and that conflicts of interest were not adequately managed. Senate and House inquiries documented failures in organ procurement and allocation, and the federal government acknowledged problems in the system’s oversight. Reports by Congress’s research arm describe the administrative and oversight issues in detail.2

The modernization

The response, enacted in 2023 and carried forward since, breaks the single-contractor model. Through 2025 HRSA established an independent, elected OPTN Board of Directors, separated the operations contractor from the policy-making structure, moved toward a multi-vendor model for the network’s technology, and arranged for direct collection of patient registration fees. HRSA also reported interviewing more than ninety stakeholders, including patients, donors, families and clinicians, as part of the process.3 These are real structural changes, and their effect on living donors will depend on whether the new bodies prioritize donor protection, which has historically been a lesser theme than recipient outcomes.

In the Statute

The living donor in the oversight map. The OPTN sets living donor policy (Policy 14) and collects follow-up data; CMS enforces program Conditions of Participation, including patient and living donor rights; HRSA oversees the OPTN and funds NLDAC; SRTR publishes outcomes. No single agency is responsible for a donor’s long-term welfare.

Your tools as a potential donor

You can compare programs. The SRTR publishes program-specific reports, including outcomes and volumes, searchable at srtr.org. Ask a program for its living-donor numbers: how many it performs, what proportion of evaluated donors it accepts, and what its follow-up completion rate is. Under IOTA, participating hospitals must post their living-donor selection criteria. If something goes wrong, you have channels: the program’s patient advocate, the hospital’s grievance process, the OPTN’s patient services, the state department of health, CMS, and your elected representatives. The Foundation’s Situation Room and Hub tools walk through them.

Counterpoint

“The reform will disrupt a working system.” That is a serious concern. A national network that allocates organs in real time cannot afford outages. The counter is that a system whose governance cannot be questioned cannot improve, and that a transition managed carefully can protect continuity. Both camps agree on the objective; they disagree about speed and risk.

What independent oversight would look like

A living donor oversight function worth the name would have four features. It would be independent of the programs it monitors. It would publish donor-centered outcomes, including long-term follow-up, complications and out-of-pocket costs. It would receive and investigate donor complaints with real authority. And it would include donors on its governing board. Parts of this exist in fragments; the whole does not yet. The Foundation’s reform agenda in Chapter 22 asks for it.

Say It Plainly

You are entitled to compare programs, to ask hard questions and to complain. The system is changing, and you are part of how.
