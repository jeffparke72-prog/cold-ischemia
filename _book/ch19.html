Chapter 19

The Ethics Seminar

“We do not ask whether the donor is good. We ask whether the system is just.”a bioethicist

Imagine a seminar room with four chairs. In the first sits a transplant surgeon, who believes that the surest way to protect donors is to restrict who may donate. In the second sits a libertarian philosopher, who believes that competent adults may accept any risk for any reason. In the third sits a patient on dialysis, who wants the kidney. In the fourth sits a donor, who gave, and has questions no one else in the room can answer. The seminar cannot be resolved. It can be conducted honestly, which is better than the alternative.

The four principles, and where they pull apart

Medical ethics rests on four widely accepted principles: respect for autonomy, beneficence (doing good), non-maleficence (avoiding harm), and justice. In most medicine they point the same way. In living donation they collide. Autonomy says a competent adult may choose to donate. Non-maleficence says a physician should not subject a healthy person to a risky operation with no medical benefit to them. Beneficence points toward the recipient, who will benefit enormously. Justice asks who donates, who receives, and whether the system is fair.

The traditional resolution holds that the donor benefits psychologically from acting on their values, and that this, together with rigorous informed consent, justifies the risk. It is a reasonable answer and not a complete one, since it depends on the donor’s choice being truly free and fully informed, the very things the safeguards in Chapter 6 try to secure.

The question of paternalism

Professionals have traditionally acted as gatekeepers, declining donors whose risk seems too high. Some ethicists now question how far that should go. If a fully informed, competent adult wants to donate despite a modest increase in risk, is a refusal respectful protection or condescending paternalism? Programs vary in their answers, which is partly why a donor turned away by one center may be accepted by another. The principled middle ground is transparency: a program that declines donors should say why, in writing, and apply its criteria consistently.

The question of payment, again

Chapter 5 set out the law. The ethical debate beneath it is whether any form of compensation, beyond expense reimbursement, could be consistent with a just system. Arguments against: exploitation of the poor, undue inducement, the commodification of the body. Arguments for: the status quo already depends on a form of exploitation, namely the uncompensated sacrifice of those who happen to have willing relatives; the poor already bear a disproportionate burden of kidney disease and of dialysis; and a regulated system could be safer than the black market it aims to prevent. The 2008 Declaration of Istanbul, signed by transplant professionals worldwide, opposes organ trafficking and transplant commercialism and calls on countries to be self-sufficient in organs. Reasonable people, and thoughtful bioethicists, disagree about how to apply it, and this book does not pretend otherwise.

Counterpoint

The deontologist: “Persons are not things; their bodies cannot be priced.” The consequentialist: “Counting the dead on the waiting list, can we afford our scruples?” The reformer in the middle: “Before debating prices, remove every cost we currently impose on donors. We have not done that.” The third position is the most actionable, and the least controversial.

Transplant tourism and the global frame

The shortage of organs is global, and so are the responses to it. In countries with weak oversight, a market in organs has flourished, with sellers often drawn from the poorest communities and receiving little of the proceeds or the aftercare. Americans who travel abroad to buy a kidney, and the brokers who arrange it, expose themselves to legal risk and exploit others. A guide to living donation in the United States has to say: do not do this, and do not accept an offer to do it. The path for a person without a donor in America is the waiting list, a paired exchange, and honest advocacy for a better system.

Justice and the question nobody wants to ask

The deepest ethical question may be the one least discussed: is it just that so much depends on the generosity of individuals? Every other life-saving medical intervention is funded by society. The living donor kidney is funded, in part, by the donor. A system that relies on individual sacrifice to address a collective failure is entitled to ask what else it could do. The answer will involve more deceased donation, better organ use, more prevention of kidney disease, better support for donors, and, at the end of the list, the compensation question. It should not begin there.

Say It Plainly

Ethics is not what happens in the seminar room. It is what the system does to the person who walks out of it and goes to the hospital.
