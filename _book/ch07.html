Chapter 7

Your Job, Your Insurance, Your Taxes

“The surgery lasts four hours. The paperwork lasts four years.”a living donor, in a support-group meeting, paraphrased

Three weeks before her operation, a nurse manager asks her employer for leave. HR says she qualifies for twelve weeks unpaid under federal law, if she has worked there a year, if the company has fifty employees within seventy-five miles, and if the paperwork is correct. She has, it does, and it is. She still cannot afford twelve unpaid weeks. So she borrows against a retirement account, tells herself it is a loan to the future, and tries not to do the arithmetic. The scene is composite. The arithmetic is real.

Work: the law that mostly isn’t there

There is no federal law that requires private employers to give paid leave to a living organ donor. The Family and Medical Leave Act provides up to twelve weeks of unpaid, job-protected leave a year for a serious health condition, and the Department of Labor has long taken the position that recovery from organ donation qualifies. That guidance matters, but it is guidance, not statute. FMLA also reaches only employees of covered employers, those with fifty or more employees, who have worked at least twelve months and 1,250 hours. Many workers, including most part-time and gig workers and many employees of small businesses, are outside it.

The one federal exception is for federal employees. The Organ Donor Leave Act of 1999 gives them up to thirty days of paid leave a year for organ donation, separate from annual and sick leave.1 It is a model that shows the policy is practical.

Beyond that, the law is a patchwork of state statutes. Some states require paid or unpaid leave for organ donors; some provide paid leave for state employees only; some have nothing. Louisiana, for example, enacted a law effective August 1, 2026, requiring employers to provide paid leave to employees who undergo medical procedures to donate an organ.2 The American Kidney Fund’s State of the States tool and the National Kidney Foundation’s compendium of state laws are the best current maps; use them, since statutes change every year.3

In the Statute

The Living Donor Protection Act (S. 1552 and H.R. 4583/4582 in the 119th Congress) would codify Department of Labor guidance that living donors are covered by FMLA, extend that treatment to federal civil service employment, and bar life, disability and long-term care insurers from discriminating against people solely because they donated an organ. It was reintroduced in 2025 and, in February 2026, was reported out of the Senate committee with an amendment in the nature of a substitute.4 As of this writing it has not become law; check congress.gov for current status.

Insurance: the quiet penalty

Medically, donors are healthy. Actuarially, some insurers treat them as a risk. Studies of living donors have found that a meaningful minority reported difficulty obtaining life insurance after donation, being denied coverage, charged more, or told they had a “pre-existing condition,” with one study published in the American Journal of Transplantation reporting that about one in four donors had such trouble.5 Life, disability and long-term care policies are largely outside the Affordable Care Act’s protections, which is why this form of discrimination is legal in most places and why the Living Donor Protection Act targets it.

Health insurance is a different matter. Under the ACA, insurers generally cannot deny coverage or charge more because of a pre-existing condition, and being a donor does not change that. But donors can face trouble with complications: whose insurance pays for care for a condition that arises from the donation, years later? The recipient’s insurance, usually Medicare or a private plan, covers the donor’s evaluation and surgery. Beyond the immediate perioperative period, coverage for complications is inconsistent. Some programs and some states address it; others do not. Ask the program in writing, before surgery, who pays for complications and for how long, and keep the answer.

Taxes: a small and uneven benefit

The federal tax code gives living donors little. Unreimbursed medical and travel expenses connected with donation may qualify as itemized medical deductions if they exceed the usual threshold, but most households do not itemize at levels that make this useful. Some states offer their own deductions or credits for expenses related to living donation; many do not. The proposed federal Living Organ Donor Tax Credit Act would create a refundable credit of up to $5,000 for donation-related expenses, an idea with growing support, and a reminder that the burden of proof sits with those who would leave things as they are.6 Tax rules change; confirm with a tax professional what applies to you in the year you donate.

Other protections worth knowing

The Americans with Disabilities Act protects against discrimination on the basis of a disability, and a donor recovering from surgery may qualify for reasonable accommodations at work during that time. It does not make donation itself a disability. Genetic privacy rules (GINA) bar health insurers and employers from using genetic information, which may matter for some donor evaluations. The Uniformed Services Employment and Reemployment Rights Act and military and VA policies create separate paths for service members and veterans, and anyone in that category should ask the program’s social worker and their command or VA coordinator early. And HIPAA protects the confidentiality of a donor’s own medical information, including from the recipient’s team in many circumstances.

What To Ask Before You Sign

Your employer: What leave am I entitled to, in writing? Is any of it paid? Your life and disability insurer: How would donation affect my current policy and my ability to buy or increase coverage? Consider buying or increasing coverage before your evaluation if you need it. The program: Who pays for complications, and for how long? Your tax adviser: Which of my costs are deductible this year?

Counterpoint

“Employers can’t be forced to absorb this.” Small businesses do face real costs, and a serious proposal should account for them. But the number of living donors in any given workplace is tiny, the leave required is finite, and the societal benefit, a person freed from dialysis and a place opened on the waiting list, is large. Where states have enacted donor leave, the system has not collapsed. The question is who bears the cost, and the current answer, the donor, is the one least able to defend it.

Say It Plainly

In most of America today, donating a kidney can cost you wages, insurability and savings, and the law does not clearly protect you from any of the three. That is a choice the country has made. It can unmake it.
