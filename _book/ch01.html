Chapter 1

The Arithmetic of Waiting

“The most expensive thing in transplantation is not the surgery. It is the time.”an organ procurement coordinator, speaking to the author’s notebook, paraphrased

Picture a spreadsheet nobody wants to open. One column holds names. Another holds dates: the day each person was listed, the day each began dialysis, the day each last felt like a person with a long future. A third column, the one the system never prints, holds a single number per row: how many nights remain on a machine before something in the body gives out. The spreadsheet is a composite, invented for this book. The people it stands for are not.

Start with what we can count. As of July 2026, 109,817 people were on the national transplant waiting list, and about 95,492 of them were waiting for a kidney, with another 2,353 waiting for a kidney and pancreas together.1 Kidneys are roughly 85 percent of everything people are waiting for. For decades the headline estimate attached to the list has been that seventeen people die each day waiting for an organ.2 The number moves a little with the year and the method, but the shape never changes. The list is longer than the supply. People age, sicken, and die inside the gap.

Now count what the system produces. In 2025 the United States performed 49,064 organ transplants, the fifth consecutive record.3 It is a staggering achievement, and every one of those operations was a life changed. Inside that total sits a quieter number: 7,237 people became living organ donors in 2025, a three percent increase over the prior year. Living donors made possible 6,521 kidney transplants and 709 liver transplants, the latter up 17 percent.4 Roughly one in seven transplants in this country now begins with a person who walked into a hospital healthy and walked out with less of themselves, on purpose.

Why the living matter more than the arithmetic suggests

If the aim were only to add organs to the pile, a living donor would look like a rounding error beside a deceased donor, who can supply several organs. But the case for living donation is not about quantity. It is about quality, timing, and the structure of the queue.

A kidney from a living donor generally begins working sooner and, on average, lasts longer than a kidney from a deceased donor. The comparison is not perfect, since living donors are carefully selected and recipients are matched in ways that favor good outcomes. Nonetheless the pattern is consistent across decades of registry data. The surgery can also be scheduled. Planned transplantation allows a patient to avoid or shorten time on dialysis, which carries its own mortality and its own erosion of a life: the thrice-weekly chair, the fluid limits, the exhaustion that follows. In the language of the field, a preemptive living-donor transplant is the best outcome available.

There is a second, less discussed effect. Every person who receives a living-donor kidney leaves the deceased-donor waiting list, or never joins it. That advances everyone behind them by one place. A living donor is therefore a gift to the stranger as well as to the intended recipient, a point to which this book returns.

The decline nobody put on a poster

For all the progress, living donation has not been a story of steady growth. The number of living kidney donors in the United States peaked in the mid-2000s, fell for years, and only recently began to recover; by 2023 the Scientific Registry of Transplant Recipients reported 6,226 living kidney donors, still well below the earlier peak.5 The 2025 totals suggest a rebound, and that is genuinely good news. But it is worth asking why a gift that medicine has refined for seventy years was shrinking in the first place. The answers are not mostly medical. They are financial, legal, logistical, and cultural, and they are the subject of the chapters ahead.

Counterpoint

“Isn’t the real fix more deceased donors?” Partly. Deceased donation is the larger source of kidneys, and improving it matters enormously. But deceased donation depends on circumstances no one controls, and the organs available do not always match the people waiting. Living donation is the one source that individuals, families, and policy can expand deliberately. The two efforts are complements, not rivals. A health system serious about its waiting list pursues both, and measures itself against both.

Three ways to read the same list

As a patient, the list is a clock. Each month on dialysis changes your health, your finances, your plans. The time you wait is not neutral; it is spent.

As a potential donor, the list is a question that finds you, usually through someone you love. You are asked, directly or by the silence in the room, whether you would be willing to do something extraordinary. What nobody tells you is that almost every step of what follows is governed by federal law, national policy, and state statutes you have never read.

As a citizen, the list is a ledger. It records how a wealthy country decided to organize a scarce, life-saving resource, and whom that organization favors. It shows where the rules reward courage and where they quietly punish it.

This book is written for all three readers at once, because the same facts mean different things from each chair.

Say It Plainly

A living donor gives something they cannot get back, to a person who needs it to survive, within a system built by law and policy. The quality of that system is a legitimate public question, and the donor is entitled to ask it before agreeing, not after.

What this chapter has not told you

It has not told you whether you should do it. No chapter in this book will. The Cold Ischemia Foundation takes no side on any individual decision to donate. We take a firm side on how the decision should be made: freely, with full information, with an independent advocate, with protection from financial harm, and with a system that keeps its promises afterward. Everything that follows is an attempt to hold that standard against the facts.
