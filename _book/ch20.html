Chapter 20

Asking Without Shame

“Research shows that a surprising share of people would donate a kidney if they knew someone who needed one. The hard part is that no one asks.”widely repeated in transplant outreach

He has rehearsed the sentence in his head for six months and still cannot say it. He needs a kidney. His doctors say a living donor is the best option. He has a sister, two friends, a congregation, a workplace. He has pride, which is a form of fear, and a dread of becoming the person who asks. The sentence he cannot say is not complicated. The hardest part is the silence before it.

This chapter is for the patient. If you are the potential donor, read it anyway: it will tell you what the person who loves you is trying not to say.

Why asking is so hard

Most people who need a kidney do not ask. They do not want to burden anyone. They fear rejection, or fear acceptance, since a yes creates a debt. They fear the answer will change a relationship. They do not want to appear to be pressuring. They worry, reasonably, that someone will say yes out of obligation, and that the donor will be harmed. These are decent reasons, and they cost lives. Transplant programs, patient groups and hospitals now teach patients that asking, done well, is not begging.

How to ask well

Programs and patient educators converge on a similar set of practices. Start by sharing, not asking. Tell people how you are doing, that your doctors say a kidney from a living donor is the best option for you, and that you are letting the people close to you know. Make it easy to say no. Acknowledge that donation is not right for everyone, that it is entirely their decision, and that nothing between you will change if the answer is no. Do not mention your blood type in public requests: a person who is not a match could be deterred, and paired exchange can solve incompatibility. Give people a way to learn without telling you. The transplant program can speak to a potential donor privately, and you need not know their decision. Consider a donor champion: a friend or relative who learns about the process and speaks for you, a buffer that makes it easier for others to come forward. Use the right channels. Start with those closest to you, then widen the circle, to coworkers, your faith community, social media, your local news, as you feel comfortable.1

A Template, In Plain Language

“I wanted to tell you something personal. My kidneys are failing, and my doctors say a kidney from a living donor would give me the best chance at a healthy life. I’m telling the people close to me. I’m not asking for an answer, and I understand this isn’t right for everyone. If you’re curious, the transplant program can explain everything and talk with you privately, and I would never need to know what you decide. Either way, thank you for hearing me.” (The Hub’s message writer can draft this in your words.)

Using social media without losing yourself

Social media can reach people you would not otherwise find. It also exposes you. Create a separate page or account for your search, so that your private life remains your own. Use a photograph and a real story: what treatment is like, how you feel, what you hope for. Posts with images attract more attention than those without. Be honest and be specific. Do not offer or accept payment for an organ. If a stranger contacts you, route them to the transplant program, which will protect both of you, and be wary of anyone who talks about money. The program, not you, should handle the evaluation.

For the person who is asked

If someone asks you, or tells you they need a kidney, you do not have to respond immediately. Say that you are glad they told you. Ask what you can learn. Take your time. You are allowed to say no, and nothing in this book suggests you owe anyone a kidney. You are also allowed to say yes, after you have done what Chapters 6 through 10 describe. If you want to help without donating, you can serve as a donor champion, help with the search, raise awareness, assist with paperwork, or drive. Many such supporters turn out to be as essential as donors.

Counterpoint

“Isn’t public appeal tacky, or even coercive?” It can feel that way, and individual styles differ. But the evidence from programs is that patients who ask have more living donors than those who do not, and that the distinction between a respectful request and pressure is largely in the framing. The responsible practices above are designed to preserve the other person’s freedom.

Say It Plainly

Asking is not begging. It is giving the people who love you a chance to help, and to say no, without losing you.
