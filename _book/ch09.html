Chapter 9

The Evaluation

“The evaluation is not an obstacle. It is the part of the process that is working for you.”a living donor coordinator

The questionnaire arrives by email. It asks about her health, her family, her work, her relationships, whether she smokes, whether she drinks, whether anyone has ever told her what to do with her body. She completes it on a Sunday night with a glass of water and the strange feeling of being interviewed by an institution that has not yet decided whether she is worth the risk to the person she loves. This is how it begins. It will take months, and the odds of reaching surgery are not what she imagines.

The funnel

Evaluation is a funnel. Many people express interest. A smaller number complete a health history. Fewer still complete screening labs and an in-person evaluation. A smaller number again are approved. A minority donate. Some drop out for medical reasons: kidney function that falls short, hypertension, diabetes, obesity, stones, a family history of kidney disease, a finding on imaging. Some drop out for non-medical reasons: cold feet, a change in the recipient’s health, a new transplant option, the cost, the time, the family conflict. Both kinds of drop-out are legitimate. Both deserve attention, because some are preventable.

What it typically involves

Programs vary, but the pattern is consistent. It begins with a health history form for the specific transplant center. A first round of blood and urine tests, sometimes done locally, screens out obvious problems. Then an in-person evaluation, usually one to two days, in which you meet the living donor team: a nurse coordinator who will be your point person, a transplant nephrologist who evaluates your kidney function and overall health, a social worker or psychologist who assesses your support and your readiness, a surgeon who explains the operation, often a dietitian, and an independent living donor advocate.1 Tests include blood work for blood type, tissue typing and general health; measures of kidney function; urine studies; an assessment of blood pressure; and imaging, usually a CT scan, to map the kidneys and their blood vessels. Women of childbearing age receive pregnancy counseling. Cancer screening appropriate for your age is typically required. In total, an evaluation commonly stretches from a few months to a year.

A donor’s compatibility with a specific recipient is determined in stages: blood type, tissue (HLA) typing, and the crossmatch test, which checks whether the recipient’s immune system will attack the donor’s cells. Incompatibility is no longer a dead end, as Chapter 14 explains.

In the Statute

Standardization. OPTN guidance on new living kidney donor program requirements aims to standardize aspects of donor evaluation and care across programs, including medical and psychosocial evaluation and follow-up.2 Programs also set their own selection criteria for conditions such as age, body mass index, blood pressure and kidney function. Under the IOTA model, participating hospitals must publicly post those criteria. Ask any program you are considering for its written living-donor selection criteria.

Who is excluded, and why it varies

No single national checklist decides who can donate. Broadly, programs look for good overall health, normal or near-normal kidney function for age, controlled or absent hypertension, no diabetes, a healthy body weight or one that can be brought into a safe range, no history of significant kidney disease or recurrent stones, no active cancer, and no psychological or social circumstances that make donation unsafe or involuntary. Each program interprets the gray zones differently. A donor with mild, well-controlled hypertension may be accepted by one program and declined by another. Age limits differ. Weight thresholds differ. If a program says no, you may ask why, and you may ask for a second opinion at another center, subject to what the recipient’s team agrees.

The long game: a lifetime of risk estimates

Tools now exist to estimate an individual candidate’s lifetime risk of kidney failure with and without donation, and to compare it to the general population. The risk projection published in the New England Journal of Medicine in 2016 is one such effort.3 Such tools are not crystal balls, and they depend on assumptions. But they allow a conversation grounded in numbers specific to you, not in averages for everyone. Ask your program whether it uses one, and what your projected risk is, over what time horizon, and with what uncertainty.

If you are turned down

A refusal is not a verdict on your worth. It is a medical judgment, or a program’s risk tolerance, or a mismatch. Ask for the reason in writing. Ask whether another program would apply different criteria. Ask whether there are other ways to help: a paired exchange, a donor champion role for the recipient, a blood-drive, an advocacy contribution. Many people who cannot donate become the most effective advocates for those who can.

What To Ask Before You Start

What are your written selection criteria? How long does an evaluation take here, start to finish? Who is my coordinator, and how do I reach them? Who is the independent advocate? What would make you decline me? What costs should I expect, and what help is available? What follow-up will you provide, and for how long?

Say It Plainly

The evaluation exists to protect you before it protects anyone else. A donor who is rushed through it has been failed. A donor who uses it fully is making the best decision they can.
