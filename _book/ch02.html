Chapter 2

A Gift Older Than the Law

“Ronald Herrick lived more than fifty years after giving his kidney to his brother. Richard lived eight. Both are the argument.”from the standard history of the first transplant

On December 23, 1954, in a Boston operating room, a surgeon named Joseph Murray took a kidney from a twenty-three-year-old man and placed it in his identical twin. The brothers shared a genome; the recipient’s immune system had nothing to reject. Nobody had a consent form like the ones used today. Nobody had an independent advocate, or a federal registry, or an insurer to argue with. They had a brother in kidney failure, a brother with two kidneys, and a surgeon willing to try.1

Richard Herrick lived another eight years, long enough to marry and have two children. Ronald, the donor, lived another half-century. Murray would share the 1990 Nobel Prize in Physiology or Medicine. Transplantation’s first great success was a living-donor transplant. For the better part of a decade it was the only kind that worked.

The long decade of improvisation

Between that first operation and the modern system lies a messy, inspiring, and sometimes alarming history. Without drugs to suppress rejection, transplants worked reliably only between twins. As immunosuppression improved through the 1960s, transplants between non-identical relatives, then between strangers, became feasible. Donors were overwhelmingly family. Hospitals developed their own rules. Waiting lists were local and often informal. Whether a patient got a kidney could depend on which hospital they walked into and whom they knew.

In 1972 Congress made a decision that reshaped American kidney care: it extended Medicare entitlement to nearly everyone with end-stage renal disease, regardless of age. It remains one of the few instances in which the federal government covers a single disease category for the whole population. It made dialysis widely available and, by the same stroke, built a large, permanent financial interest in dialysis itself. Chapter 16 returns to that tension.

The Act that built the system

By the early 1980s the field faced a crisis of its own success. Transplants worked, demand exploded, and the arrangements for allocating organs were uneven and sometimes ethically troubling, including a short-lived, widely condemned scheme to broker the purchase of kidneys from living sellers. Congress responded with the National Organ Transplant Act of 1984.2

NOTA did several things at once. It created the Organ Procurement and Transplantation Network, the national system for matching and allocating organs, which the government contracts a private nonprofit to run, and the Scientific Registry of Transplant Recipients to collect outcomes data. It authorized assistance to organ procurement organizations. And, in a provision that shapes every living donor’s experience to this day, it made it a federal crime to knowingly acquire, receive or transfer a human organ for “valuable consideration” in interstate commerce. Chapter 5 is devoted to that sentence and the long argument it has produced.

In the Statute

42 U.S.C. §274e prohibits knowingly acquiring, receiving, or transferring a human organ for valuable consideration for use in human transplantation if the transfer affects interstate commerce. The statute excludes from “valuable consideration” reasonable payments for removal, transportation, implantation, processing, preservation, quality control and storage, and the expenses of travel, housing and lost wages incurred by the donor. The penalty can reach five years in prison and a $50,000 fine.3

From brokers to a national network

The OPTN began operating under a federal contract in the mid-1980s, and a 1998 federal regulation (42 CFR Part 121) formalized how it works. Over the next three decades the system grew into what it is today: a national matching network, a registry of outcomes, transplant programs certified by Medicare, and organ procurement organizations with exclusive service areas. In 2023, Congress passed legislation to break the single-contractor model of the OPTN, and in 2025 the Health Resources and Services Administration pressed ahead with a modernization effort that includes an independent, elected OPTN Board of Directors and a multi-vendor structure.4

The rise of the strangers

The most consequential change for living donors came late and from below. Two ideas transformed the field. The first was paired exchange: if your willing donor is not a match for you, perhaps they match someone else whose donor matches you, and the two pairs can swap. The second was the non-directed donor, a person who gives to someone they have never met, often starting a chain in which each recipient’s donor passes the gift forward. These practices turned a transaction between two people into a network. The National Kidney Registry, founded in 2007, is the largest of the organizations that built them, and the OPTN later developed its own kidney paired donation pilot.5

The effect was subtle and large. For the first time a donor who was not a match was not a dead end. A refusal by biology no longer had to end the story.

The recent turn: from permission to incentive

The last decade changed the policy conversation. In July 2019 Executive Order 13879, Advancing American Kidney Health, directed the Department of Health and Human Services to expand reimbursement for living donors’ costs. A 2020 rule did so, adding lost wages and child-care and elder-care expenses to the categories the federal government could reimburse, effective October 22, 2020.6 In July 2025 the Centers for Medicare & Medicaid Services began a mandatory six-year payment model, the Increasing Organ Transplant Access (IOTA) model, for 103 kidney transplant hospitals, tying payments to transplant volume, matching efficiency and outcomes, and requiring participating hospitals to post their living-donor selection criteria publicly.7 In Congress, the Living Donor Protection Act, which would bar certain insurance discrimination and clarify job-protected leave, was reintroduced in 2025 and, in February 2026, reported out of a Senate committee.8

That is a great deal of motion for a field that moved slowly for decades. It is worth noticing, however, that each reform acknowledges the same underlying fact: for seventy years the system relied on donors absorbing costs the system itself created.

Say It Plainly

The story of living donation is a story of individual courage repeatedly outrunning the rules designed to support it. The rules have improved. They are not finished.
