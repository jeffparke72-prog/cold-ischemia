Prelude Two

The Ecosystem, Mapped

“Show me the incentive and I will show you the outcome.”A saying attributed to Charlie Munger

A hospital administrator, a dialysis nurse, a congressional staffer and a kidney donor sit in the same room only once a year: the room is a conference hall, the occasion is a panel, and none of them has ever seen the whole machine. Each describes the part they touch. The audience, mostly patients, realizes slowly that no one on the stage can draw the entire thing on a whiteboard. Someone should. This chapter tries.

To an individual, the living-donor process feels like a series of appointments. To a policy analyst, it is a market-shaped system with a government-run center of gravity, no single owner, and a handful of well-resourced industries that depend on how it works. Understanding it does not require cynicism. It requires seeing who is paid, who is accountable, and who is absent.

The actors, plainly

The patient and the donor. They are the reason for the system and, by design, its least powerful members. They hold the rare asset (the organ and the consent) but little institutional information. The transplant program. A hospital-based team of surgeons, nephrologists, nurses, social workers and coordinators. Programs are certified by the federal government, are measured on outcomes, and must follow national living-donor policies. Each also operates inside a hospital with a budget. The independent living donor advocate. Required by federal policy to be separate from the recipient’s team, this person exists for one purpose: the donor’s interests. How well the role is staffed varies a great deal.

The OPTN and its contractors. The national network that develops the rules of allocation and living-donor safeguards under federal oversight, with contractors that have been reorganized in recent years. The SRTR. The registry that analyzes outcomes and publishes program-level performance. HRSA and CMS. The two federal agencies that sit above it all: one oversees the network, the other certifies hospitals and pays most of the bills. Congress and the states. Congress wrote the foundational statute and sets the money; states write the tax credits, the leave laws and the discrimination protections that decide whether a donor is financially safe.

Payers. Because the recipient’s insurance, usually Medicare, covers the donor’s evaluation and surgery, a donor is financially entangled with someone else’s coverage in ways that are rarely explained. The dialysis industry. A small number of large companies provide most U.S. dialysis; a successful transplant ends a patient’s time on the machine and, with it, a source of revenue. We do not allege bad faith. We note that the incentives are worth a question, and Chapters 15 and 16 ask it. Paired-exchange registries. Networks that let incompatible pairs trade, creating chains that can start with one stranger. Patient organizations and advocates. From national charities to small volunteer groups, the ones who hold hands in the waiting room and, occasionally, testify.

Where the flows go

Three flows define the ecosystem. Information moves from the program to you, mostly in the form of consent documents and a coordinator’s time. Money moves from payers to programs, with the donor often reimbursed late, partially, or not at all for the incidental costs (travel, lost wages, childcare). Accountability moves, in theory, from the programs up to the federal agencies and back down in the form of audits and rule changes; in practice it moves slowly and rarely reaches the individual donor. If you remember one sentence from this chapter, make it this: the person who bears the most risk has the least institutional voice.

The Numbers

6,521 living-donor kidney transplants and 7,237 living donors in 2025, out of 49,064 transplants overall. Living donors are a small, indispensable fraction of the system, and a sizable share of kidney transplants. Source: OPTN; see notes.

What a healthy ecosystem would look like

A system that served donors well would make the donor’s advocate truly independent, publish long-term donor outcomes by program, reimburse the full financial cost of donating up front, protect donors from insurance and employment penalties by federal law, and treat paired exchange as a default rather than a rescue. None of these is a fantasy; each exists somewhere in the world or in fragments here. The gap between the fragments and the whole is the subject of Part VI.

What To Ask

“Who is my independent living donor advocate, and how can I reach them without going through the recipient’s team?” — “What are this program’s own donor outcomes and long-term follow-up rates?” — “What costs will I be responsible for, and which can be reimbursed before I incur them?”
