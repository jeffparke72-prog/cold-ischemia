Appendix A

Glossary

ABO compatibility
Matching of blood types between donor and recipient. Incompatible pairs can often still participate in paired exchange or, in some programs, be transplanted with special protocols.
Cold ischemia time
The interval between the moment an organ’s blood supply is cut off and the moment blood flows through it again in the recipient, during which the organ is kept cold. It is the period of maximum vulnerability of an organ and the origin of this Foundation’s name.
CMS
The Centers for Medicare & Medicaid Services, which certifies transplant programs and OPOs and sets the Conditions of Participation.
Crossmatch
A laboratory test that checks whether a recipient’s antibodies will attack a particular donor’s cells.
Donor champion
A friend or relative who learns about living donation and speaks for a patient seeking a donor.
eGFR
Estimated glomerular filtration rate: a calculated measure of kidney function from blood tests.
ESRD
End-stage renal disease: kidney failure requiring dialysis or transplant.
FMLA
The Family and Medical Leave Act, which provides up to 12 weeks of unpaid, job-protected leave for eligible employees.
HLA typing
Tissue typing that assesses immune compatibility between donor and recipient.
HRSA
The Health Resources and Services Administration, the federal agency that oversees the OPTN and funds NLDAC.
ILDA
Independent Living Donor Advocate: a person independent of the recipient’s care who represents the donor’s interests.
IOTA
The Increasing Organ Transplant Access Model, a CMS payment model for 103 kidney transplant hospitals (July 2025 to June 2031).
Kidney paired donation
Exchange between incompatible donor-recipient pairs so that each recipient receives a compatible kidney.
NLDAC
National Living Donor Assistance Center: a federally funded program that reimburses eligible donors’ non-medical expenses.
Non-directed donor
A living donor who gives to someone they do not know; also called a Good Samaritan donor.
NOTA
The National Organ Transplant Act of 1984.
OPO
Organ procurement organization: a federally designated organization that coordinates deceased donation in a service area.
OPTN
The Organ Procurement and Transplantation Network: the national system that sets organ allocation and membership policy.
Preemptive transplant
A transplant performed before the patient needs dialysis.
SRTR
The Scientific Registry of Transplant Recipients: the national registry that publishes outcomes for programs and OPOs.
Valuable consideration
The legal term in NOTA for payment or other value given in exchange for an organ, which is prohibited; reimbursement of donor expenses is excluded from the definition.
Appendix B

Timeline

  1. 1954 First successful living-donor kidney transplant, between identical twins, Boston (Murray; Nobel Prize 1990).
  2. 1972 Congress extends Medicare coverage to nearly all Americans with end-stage renal disease.
  3. 1984 National Organ Transplant Act: creates the OPTN and the SRTR; bans the sale of organs.
  4. 1986 The OPTN begins operating under federal contract.
  5. 1998–2000 Final rule, 42 CFR Part 121, formalizes OPTN operations.
  6. 1999 Organ Donor Leave Act: 30 days of paid leave for federal employee donors.
  7. 2005 OPTN begins requiring living-donor follow-up forms.
  8. 2007 National Kidney Registry founded; paired donation clarified in federal law.
  9. 2008 Declaration of Istanbul against organ trafficking and transplant tourism.
  10. 2016 OPTN modifies informed consent requirements for potential living donors.
  11. 2019 Executive Order 13879, Advancing American Kidney Health.
  12. 2020 Rule effective Oct. 22 expands NLDAC reimbursement to lost wages and dependent care.
  13. 2023 Federal law to break the single-contractor OPTN model.
  14. 2025 HRSA OPTN modernization advances; IOTA begins July 1; Living Donor Protection Act reintroduced; 49,064 transplants and 7,237 living donors.
  15. 2026 Living Donor Protection Act reported out of Senate committee (Feb.); IOTA modifications finalized; Louisiana donor-leave law takes effect Aug. 1.

Appendix D

How This Guide Was Made, and Its Limits

Honesty about method is part of the argument of this book, so here it is.

Sources. The statistics, statutes and policies in this guide were gathered in October 2026 from public sources: federal agency pages, the Federal Register, congress.gov, peer-reviewed journals indexed in PubMed Central, and the websites of national patient and professional organizations. Some web pages were identified through search results and their key content was read in summary form; we could not open every cited page in full. Where a number or rule is uncertain, we say so, and we recommend verifying it at the primary source in Appendix C.

Review. This edition has not been reviewed by a physician or an attorney. Before it is distributed widely, it should be. Legal citations are stated in general terms and should be checked against the current text of each rule. Clinical statements are summaries of published research, not recommendations.

Composites. Every scene that opens a chapter is invented. No scene depicts a real person or program.

What it leaves out. This is a guide to living donation in the United States. It does not cover donation abroad, deceased donation in depth, tissue or bone marrow donation, or the detail of every state’s law. It does not replace the program’s own materials, which you should read in full.

Corrections. If you find an error, or if something has changed, tell us at jeffparke72@gmail.com. A corrected edition is a feature, not an embarrassment.
