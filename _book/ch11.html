Chapter 11

The Operation, the Recovery, the Aftermath

“Nobody tells you the surprising part. The surprising part is how ordinary the Tuesday after feels.”a living donor

The morning of the surgery she is calmer than she expected and more frightened than she admits. A nurse takes her blood pressure, a resident asks her name three times, an anesthesiologist explains what she will feel and what she won’t. She thinks of her brother, in another room, being prepared for the other half of the operation. For a few minutes, before the sedative, they are two people in two beds in the same building, the closest they have ever been to each other and the farthest apart.

The operation

Most living kidney donations in the United States are performed laparoscopically, through a few small incisions in the abdomen and a slightly larger one to remove the kidney, often hand-assisted. Robotic approaches are increasingly used. The surgeon chooses which kidney to remove, generally leaving the donor with the better-functioning one. The kidney is flushed with a cold preservation solution, which is where this foundation takes its name: the interval between the moment the kidney leaves the donor and the moment blood flows through it again in the recipient is called cold ischemia time, and in a living-donor transplant it can be measured in an hour or two. The operation takes roughly two to four hours. The recipient’s operation proceeds in the adjacent room.

The hospital stay and the first weeks

Most donors stay in the hospital two to four days. Pain is real and manageable: abdominal soreness, shoulder pain from the gas used in laparoscopy, fatigue, and sometimes nausea. Programs generally ask donors to walk early, avoid lifting heavy objects for several weeks, and abstain from nonsteroidal anti-inflammatory drugs such as ibuprofen, which can strain the remaining kidney. Most donors feel dramatically better by two weeks and close to normal by six, but this is an average, and recovery varies with age, fitness, the nature of the surgery and the job. Planning for twelve weeks of reduced capacity, even if you need less, is wiser than planning for four and needing more.

What To Arrange Before Surgery

A caregiver for the first week, and a backup. Transportation home and to follow-up visits. Time off work, in writing. A plan for children, pets and household tasks. Prescription pain medication arranged before discharge. A list of symptoms that mean call the program immediately: fever, worsening pain, wound changes, inability to urinate, leg swelling, shortness of breath.

What no one rehearses: the emotional aftermath

Donors speak, afterward, of several things clinical summaries leave out. There is often a letdown. The intensity of the preparation, the attention, and the sense of purpose gives way to a quiet recovery and to the realization that the world has gone back to normal. Some donors feel a loss they did not expect and have trouble naming. Some feel a pride that they hesitate to express. Some feel anxiety about the remaining kidney, a vigilance about every ache. And the relationship with the recipient is complicated by the fact that a gift of this kind creates obligations nobody asked for: the recipient may feel indebted, the donor may feel scrutinized for how they behave, and each may feel unable to be honest about the difficult days.

These feelings are common and not pathological. They are the ordinary human response to an extraordinary event. A good program has social-work support available after surgery, and donors should use it. Peer support, whether through a hospital group, the National Kidney Registry, or the American Society of Transplantation’s resources, is often more useful than any pamphlet.

Counterpoint

“Why dwell on the hard parts? Won’t it scare people away?” Hiding them does the damage. A donor who expected a three-week recovery and has an eight-week one feels deceived, and that sense of deception spreads through every community of potential donors. Honest preparation produces better decisions and better recoveries. The donors who regret donating are mostly the ones who were surprised.

Returning to life

Most donors return to normal activity, including exercise, travel, and eventually most sports, within a few months. Programs advise a healthy diet, regular exercise, blood-pressure control, avoidance of nephrotoxic drugs, and annual checkups with kidney function and blood-pressure tests. That list is not the tax of having donated; it is good advice for anyone. But for a donor it carries a specific weight: it is the maintenance that protects the one kidney left. Chapter 13 describes how that maintenance is supposed to be supported, and how often it is not.

Say It Plainly

You will probably recover well. You may not recover quickly. Plan for the second case, and be pleasantly surprised by the first.
