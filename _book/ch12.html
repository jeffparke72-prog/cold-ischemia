Chapter 12

Beyond the Kidney: The Liver and Rarer Gifts

“Kidney donation asks you to give up one of two. Liver donation asks you to give up a piece of one, and trust it to grow back.”a hepatobiliary surgeon

A father stands in a hospital corridor holding a coffee he has not touched. His daughter, nine years old, has a failing liver. Her best option is a piece of his. The surgeon has drawn a diagram on a napkin: a right lobe for an adult recipient, a smaller left lobe for a child. The numbers on the diagram are percentages, and the father reads them as odds. He asks the question out loud: “What are the chances something goes wrong with me?” The honest answer is not the one he wants.

Why the liver is different

The liver regenerates. A donated portion of a healthy liver regrows within weeks to months in both donor and recipient. That biological fact made living liver donation possible, and also hides its difficulty. The operation to remove a lobe is a major abdominal operation, longer and riskier than a kidney donation. Recovery is longer and more variable. Pain can be significant. Return to full activity commonly takes two to three months or more.

In 2025, living donors made possible 709 liver transplants in the United States, a 17 percent increase over the prior year, a small number beside kidneys but a growing one.1 Living liver donation is performed in only some transplant centers, since it requires a team experienced in both donor and recipient surgery.

The risks, plainly

The risk of death for a living liver donor is higher than for a kidney donor. Published estimates range from about 0.15 to 0.5 percent overall, with the highest risk in the first few months and the higher end of the range for right-lobe donors, whose operation removes more of the liver; left-lobe donation is associated with lower risk, nearer 0.1 percent.2 Complications are common: studies report a complication rate of about 15 to 25 percent on average, and as high as 40 percent after adult-to-adult right-lobe donation. Most are minor and resolve; a small proportion, around one percent, are near-miss, potentially life-threatening events. In the large Adult-to-Adult Living Donor Liver Transplantation Cohort Study (A2ALL), roughly six percent of donors first experienced a complication a year or more after surgery, most commonly hernia, bowel obstruction and psychological complications, and while 95 percent of complications resolved within a year, only about three-quarters of hernias and well under half of psychological complications did.3

The Numbers

Living liver donation: estimated mortality about 0.15–0.5%; complication rates about 15–25% overall and up to about 40% after right-lobe donation; about 1% near-miss events. These figures come from published cohort studies and vary by center and era. Ask the specific program for its own donor outcomes.

The psychological dimension

The A2ALL findings include a sobering note. Among the deaths in that cohort, two of three occurred more than a year after donation and were attributed to psychological causes. That finding is rare and is not a prediction. It is a reason to take the psychological side of donation seriously: screening before surgery, support after, and attention to donors who struggle. A donor who is depressed, isolated or ashamed of feeling regret needs a program that checks in, and a family that knows the signs.

The rarer gifts

Lung. Living-donor lobar lung transplantation, in which two donors each give a lobe to one recipient, is performed rarely in the United States and requires specialized centers. Intestine and pancreas. Segmental living donation exists but is uncommon. Uterus. Uterus transplantation, including from living donors, has moved from experiment to a small number of programs, with specific ethical and medical requirements. The OPTN extended its living donor policies in 2021 to cover donors of vascularized composite tissue and other organs, which means that the safeguards described in Chapter 6 apply across a wider range of gifts.4 A donor considering any of these should ask for the program’s own experience and outcomes, since sample sizes are small and differences between centers matter.

Counterpoint

“If the risks are higher, shouldn’t living liver donation be rarer or banned?” Many transplant professionals ask exactly this and answer that donation should proceed only with rigorous selection and informed consent, since the alternative for some recipients, especially children and people with few options, is death on the waiting list. Others argue that the line is a matter of what a competent adult is permitted to risk for a person they love. The disagreement is real and respectable, and a potential donor deserves to hear both sides.

Say It Plainly

The liver is a more dangerous gift than the kidney. If a program describes both in the same reassuring tone, ask more questions.
