Prelude One

Where You Are Standing

“Everyone is somebody’s waiting room.”Said by a dialysis social worker, paraphrased from memory

It is ten past two in the morning and Dana is not asleep. The house is the kind of quiet that only happens when everyone you love is breathing in the next room. Her brother has been on dialysis for fourteen months. Tonight, for the first time, she typed the words into a search bar that she had been circling for weeks: can a healthy person give a kidney. The screen answered in pamphlets and forums and one furious comment thread. She closed the laptop. She opened it again. She has not told anyone she is looking.

Before we give you a single statistic, we want to do something unusual for a guide like this one. We want to tell you where you are.

You are standing in a particular year

It is 2026. In the United States, roughly 95,500 people are waiting for a kidney, and the number has not meaningfully fallen in a generation. Each of them is a person with a Tuesday and a favorite song, and each is, in a very literal sense, on a clock. Kidney failure is the one organ failure that medicine has learned to postpone with a machine. Most people on in-center hemodialysis are connected to one for roughly four hours, three days a week, for as long as they wait. That is a life lived in the margins of an appointment schedule. It is survivable. It is not the same as living.

The year matters because the system is changing under you. The national network that matches organs was reorganized by Congress and the federal government in the early 2020s. Payment models have been rewritten to give hospitals and dialysis providers different incentives. Living-donor protections are being debated in state legislatures and in Washington. A decision made in 2026 sits in a different legal and financial landscape than the same decision made in 2006, and the pamphlet you were handed may be older than your coverage.

You are standing in a particular place

Geography is not neutral here. Which transplant center you live near, whether your state offers a tax benefit to donors, whether your employer is covered by a leave law, whether your insurance counts a donor’s recovery as a medical event or as a pre-existing condition: all of it varies by zip code and paycheck. Two people with the same kidney, the same generosity and the same blood type can face wildly different bills.

The place matters in a second way. You are standing inside a system, not merely a hospital: federal agencies, a national matching network, transplant programs, insurers, dialysis corporations, state legislatures, patient charities, and a rising ecosystem of paired-exchange registries. Each of them has a job, and each has incentives. The next chapter draws the map, because you cannot navigate a country you have never seen.

You are standing in a particular body

You have two kidneys, and you have probably never thought about them. That is the paradox of the organ: its health is invisible until it is the only thing that matters. A living donor makes a choice that almost nothing else in medicine asks of a healthy adult: to accept a real, permanent, small-but-not-zero risk, with no health benefit to themselves, for another person’s sake. Surgeons call it the exceptional case in which the principle of “do no harm” is knowingly bent, and the bending is justified only by the donor’s free and fully informed choice. Everything in this book is, in the end, about the word fully.

Say It Plainly

You are not a rescuer arriving at a rescue. You are a person with a life, standing at a door, deciding whether to open it. Everyone in this system, including us, should help you see clearly what is on the other side.

If any of this sounds like you

People arrive at this decision from many different directions, and the feelings are older than the facts. You may recognize yourself in one or several of these:

The one who always says yes. You are the person your family calls. Yes is automatic, and you are not sure you are choosing so much as obeying a reflex you have had since you were nine. The one who is terrified and ashamed of it. You want to help and you are afraid of the operation, of the scar, of dying, of living differently, and you think the fear makes you a coward. It does not. It makes you accurate. The one who feels nothing yet and is worried about that. The decision is intellectual, the stakes feel abstract, and you wonder what it says about you. The one who was asked, and cannot say no. A relative has made the ask, directly or by silence, and refusing feels like a verdict on love. The stranger. Nobody asked. You read something once, and you cannot put it down. You may be the most curious and the most scrutinized person in the whole system, and you deserve to be treated as neither a saint nor a suspect.

All of these are legitimate places to begin. None of them is a qualification. The rest of the book exists to help you move from where you are to what you actually want, with the evidence in front of you.

Counterpoint

“Isn’t it manipulative to talk about feelings in a policy guide?” It would be, if we told you which feeling to have. We do not. But a decision that ignores the psychology of the person making it is not an informed decision; it is a procedure with a signature. The best transplant programs know this, and so do their critics.
