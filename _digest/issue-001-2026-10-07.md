## This week in one sentence
Donation looks safe for most donors over the long run, but the people around a transplant (caregivers, patients facing a second transplant, Black candidates owed restored wait time) keep running into gaps the system does not measure.

---
## 1. Living donors: two big studies say kidney function holds. One small one disagrees.
**The finding.** A Swiss registry of 1,863 living kidney donors with 5+ years of follow-up (1993-2018) found kidney function stable, with an average annual eGFR change of +0.14 ml/min per 1.73 m². A Heidelberg cohort of 632 donors (1991-2020) found stable function after the expected early drop, across ages under 40, 40-60 and over 60. A small Turkish comparison (87 donors, 100 controls) found nephrectomy was an independent risk factor for hypertension and for a lasting GFR reduction above 20%.
**Why it matters.** Donors are told the long-term picture is reassuring. For most, the larger studies support that, and the older-donor result matters for people considering donating later in life. The small study is a reminder to ask a program for *your* numbers, not an average.
**What it does not show.** Observational registries cannot prove cause, and donors who stay in follow-up may be healthier than those who drop out. The Turkish study is single-center and small.
**Who paid / conflicts.** Not confirmed. Check the disclosure sections before posting.
**Source.** Transplant International 2026, DOI 10.3389/ti.2026.16266 (Heidelberg). Kidney International Reports 2026 (Swiss registry; locate by title on PubMed). International Urology and Nephrology 2026, "Long-term clinical outcomes in living kidney donors: a comparative study with matched controls", PMID 41175287.

## 2. The caregiver's side of graft loss
**The finding.** 23 caregivers (11 spouses, 10 parents, 2 siblings) described supporting a kidney transplant recipient through graft loss: managing diet, supporting home dialysis, navigating care, and holding multiple roles. They reported feeling undervalued, and the authors recommend better integration, support and education for caregivers.
**Why it matters.** Almost all transplant data is about the patient. This is rare evidence about the person at the kitchen table when a kidney fails, which is exactly the person the system forgets.
**What it does not show.** Interview studies show experience, not how common it is. The sample is small.
**Who paid / conflicts.** Not confirmed. Check the disclosure section.
**Source.** "Caregiver Perspectives on Supporting Kidney Transplant Recipients Through Graft Loss", 2026, PMID 42088030. Journal as listed on ScienceDirect (Kidney International Reports family; confirm).
**Tier.** 1  **Status.** VERIFY BEFORE POSTING: journal name and funding.

## 3. After a transplant fails: what blocks the second one
**The finding.** A qualitative study in CJASN (published July 22, 2026) asked patients, caregivers and providers about barriers to retransplantation after graft loss. It identified several medical and non-medical barriers, plus additional barriers specific to pursuing a living-donor retransplant.
**Why it matters.** Graft loss is common enough that many families face a second search for a donor, and living-donor retransplants carry their own obstacles. Naming them gives families questions to bring to the team.
**What it does not show.** Perceptions, not measured rates. Exact counts of barriers were not confirmed from the sources reached.
**Who paid / conflicts.** Not confirmed.
**Source.** "A Qualitative Study of Patients, Caregivers, and Providers on Barriers to Kidney Retransplantation following Graft Loss", Clinical Journal of the American Society of Nephrology, 2026, DOI prefix 10.2215/CJN.0000001162.
**Tier.** 1  **Status.** VERIFY BEFORE POSTING: full DOI, exact barrier list, funding.

## 4. Removing race from the kidney formula: a policy change was not enough
**The finding.** At one large U.S. center, 274 Black patients were eligible for modified wait time after the race-neutral eGFR policy (effective July 2022). 109 (39.8%) actually received modifications, averaging 2.7 additional years of wait time, and recovery was limited by the availability of old laboratory data.
**Why it matters.** The policy repaid a historical debt, but it did not reach everyone. Candidates may need to help locate old lab values. Mainstream outlets covered a separate national analysis in March 2026; this single-center study shows where it leaks at the bedside.
**What it does not show.** One center. National numbers differ and should be read alongside it.
**Who paid / conflicts.** Not confirmed.
**Source.** "A Single-Center Assessment of the Race Neutral eGFR Calculation and Access to Kidney Transplantation for Black Patients: A Policy Change Is Not Enough", Journal of Racial and Ethnic Health Disparities, DOI 10.1007/s40615-025-02573-9, PMID 41811668.

## Item 4
**X:** After race was removed from the kidney-function formula, one large U.S. center found 274 Black patients eligible for restored wait time. 109 (39.8%) got it, averaging 2.7 years, held back by missing old lab records. A policy change is not enough on its own.
**Story card:** "THE RULE CHANGED. MOST PATIENTS WERE STILL LEFT OUT." · **109 of 274** · "Got restored wait time at one large center." · Source: J Racial Ethn Health Disparities
**Facebook (about 100 words):** In 2022 the transplant system stopped using a race-based adjustment in the kidney-function formula that had kept many Black patients lower on the waiting list. Candidates were supposed to get their waiting time restored. A single-center study found 274 Black patients eligible and only 109 (39.8%) who got it, averaging 2.7 years of recovered time. The main obstacle was finding old lab results. One center is not the nation, but the lesson travels: if you are a candidate, ask your program whether your wait time was reviewed, and help find your old labs. Educational, not medical advice.
