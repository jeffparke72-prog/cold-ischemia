# Chapter 7: The Business of the Wait
EPIGRAPH: "It is difficult to get a man to understand something, when his salary depends upon his not understanding it."
BY: Upton Sinclair, I, Candidate for Governor: And How I Got Licked (1935)

:::note
**In this chapter.** *Question:* does the economics of dialysis help or hinder the movement of patients to transplant? *Answer:* the evidence suggests it hinders, mildly and structurally, not by conspiracy but by arithmetic. A market dominated by two companies is paid for keeping the chair full. The evidence for motive is thin; the evidence for pattern is not.
:::

Every person on the transplant list is, for the years they wait, a customer.

I mean that without cynicism. The great majority of the 143,982 adults on the kidney waiting list in 2024 (Source Notes S6) were on dialysis, in a chair, three afternoons a week. The chair belongs to a business. The business is paid by the treatment. That business has, over the last two decades, consolidated into one of the most concentrated markets in American medicine, and its incentives are one of the quieter forces shaping how fast patients move from the chair to the list and from the list to a kidney.

I want to treat this chapter carefully, because it is the one in which it is easiest to say more than the evidence allows. The people who run dialysis units are not villains. Many are nurses and technicians who know their patients' dogs by name. The companies employ tens of thousands of people, and they provide, most of the time, a treatment that works. But a book about the invisible ledger cannot ignore a place where money, accountability and the length of the wait meet.

## The story of a chair

Consider the unit, not the company. A dialysis chair is capital. The machine, the nurse, the technician, the water treatment system and the lease are fixed costs that must be paid whether the chair is occupied or not. A chair that is full three shifts a day, six days a week, is a chair that earns its keep. A patient who leaves, by transplant, by moving, or by death, creates a vacancy, and a vacancy costs money until a new patient fills it.

This is not a secret and is not unique to dialysis. It is the economics of any facility. What makes it consequential here is that the patient who leaves by transplant is leaving for a better life, and that the decision about whether and when that happens is shaped, in part, by people who work in the building the patient is leaving. The facility is the place where a patient is first told about transplantation, referred for evaluation, or not.

Do they tell? A national analysis of patients who began dialysis between 2007 and 2019, using forms that clinics submit when a patient starts treatment, found that about 15 percent were not told about transplantation at all. Patients who were informed reached the waiting list sooner. Facilities owned by large chains were more likely to inform patients, but their patients were not more likely to be waitlisted. And independent facilities that were acquired by chains became more likely to inform patients but less likely to waitlist them after the acquisition (Source Notes S42).

Hold that last finding for a moment. It does not show that chains are hiding transplantation. It shows that the knowledge and the result are not connected. Telling is cheap. Referring, evaluating and listing take staff, follow-up and effort, and they remove a chair's occupant at the end.

## What the numbers say

The best-known study of the question was published in the *Journal of the American Medical Association* in 2019. Researchers examined 1,478,564 patients treated at 6,511 facilities between 2000 and 2016. They found that for-profit facilities had significantly lower rates of placing patients on the waiting list than nonprofit ones. The study's senior author, Rachel Patzer, said in the coverage that the pattern was consistent with the incentives: facilities earn more when patients stay on dialysis (Source Notes S43). I will not quote the secondary numbers from that study, which I could not check. The headline is well established.

I should say what the study does not show. It shows association and not motive. A for-profit facility may serve patients who are poorer, sicker or farther from a transplant center. The authors adjusted for many such factors. But no adjustment can separate what an organization intends from what its incentives produce.

Other evidence points in the same direction. A 2026 study of how patients arrive at dialysis found that more than half started treatment without having had care from a kidney specialist beforehand or a permanent access in place, and that these unprepared patients were more likely to be at for-profit and smaller facilities, to be male, younger, Black or Hispanic, uninsured, not informed about transplant, and living in high-poverty neighborhoods (Source Notes S44). A federal payment experiment designed to encourage transplant referral, the ESRD Treatment Choices model, which applied to roughly a third of the country from 2021, reported through three years no measurable impact on waitlisting or on living-donor transplantation (Source Notes S45).

Put these together and a pattern emerges. The incentive to refer exists in theory; in practice it has not moved the needle. The payment design was not strong enough, or the effort required was too great, or the incentives for the opposite were not weak enough.

## A market with two heads

The conversation about incentives would be different in a competitive market, and this one is not.

A peer-reviewed analysis found that the two largest dialysis companies together held 59.1 percent of the market in 2005 and 77.1 percent in 2019 (Source Notes S46). Other estimates put their combined share anywhere from about 72 to 80 percent. Adam Smith, in *The Wealth of Nations* in 1776, wrote that "people of the same trade seldom meet together, even for merriment and diversion, but the conversation ends in a conspiracy against the public, or in some contrivance to raise prices." Smith was not describing dialysis. He was describing the natural tendency of concentrated trades. The most one can say is that the dialysis market has the structure he warned about.

The industry's finances are contested. The Medicare Payment Advisory Commission reported that the aggregate Medicare margin for outpatient dialysis was negative 0.2 percent in 2023 and 4.5 percent in 2024, and recommended that Congress eliminate the payment update for 2027. An industry trade group has argued that the commission's figure overstates the margin by roughly two points, and that the average conceals many struggling clinics (Source Notes S47). Both statements may be true. A modest Medicare margin can coexist with a much more profitable commercial insurance business, which is where the next part of the story goes.

## What the courts and the regulators have said

I will separate what is established from what is alleged, because the difference matters.

The established part is that the largest company entered a corporate integrity agreement with the federal government in 2014, as part of a resolution of allegations about arrangements with physicians. In July 2024 the Department of Justice announced a settlement of more than $34 million of allegations of illegal kickbacks, a fact I found referenced in a journal article and not on the Department's own page (Source Notes S48).

The alleged part is larger. A 2016 whistleblower alleged that the two largest companies steered patients toward a charity's premium assistance program, which pays insurance premiums for patients, in ways that moved them from Medicare onto private plans that pay far more per treatment; the charity, whose funding comes largely from the two companies by advocacy accounts, disputes these characterizations. California passed a law to cap payment for such patients, and a court enjoined it in 2019 (Source Notes S48). I did not find the resolution of the whistleblower's suit, and I do not claim it was proven.

A related allegation concerns the procedures that keep a dialysis patient's access working. In 2022 the Department of Justice sued a vascular access subsidiary of the second company, alleging that at least 1,288 of 2,303 angioplasty procedures among sixty patients, 56 percent, were medically unnecessary (Source Notes S49). It is an allegation. A predecessor company paid $3.59 million to resolve a similar False Claims Act matter. And industry advocates argue, with some evidence, that patients treated at independent access centers have more fistulas and fewer central venous catheters than those treated in hospitals (Source Notes S49).

I do not draw a conclusion from this list. I draw a pattern: a business in which the decisions that make the most money are the ones least visible to patients, and in which enforcement arrives years later, if at all.

## What the machines are not doing

There is a second way to see the business: by what its machines do not do.

In 2023, a large European trial of high-dose hemodiafiltration, a treatment that cleans blood using convection as well as diffusion, found that patients lived longer than on the standard high-flux hemodialysis the American system uses. Among 1,360 patients followed a median of thirty months, 17.3 percent of those treated with hemodiafiltration died, against 21.9 percent of those on standard dialysis, a hazard ratio of 0.77 (Source Notes S33). The benefit seemed to come mainly from fewer deaths from infection. The trial has critics, including about the patients selected.

[[FIG:hdf|Figure 7.2. Deaths over a median of thirty months in the CONVINCE trial of hemodiafiltration versus standard hemodialysis.]]

American patients cannot easily receive this therapy. The leading manufacturer received regulatory clearance for a machine capable of it in February 2024, and for an updated version in May 2025, with broader commercial use planned for 2026 (Source Notes S33). The company says roughly 160,000 in-center machines in the country could eventually be replaced. Whether the American industry will adopt it faster than it adopted home dialysis, whose share of new patients rose only from 6.8 percent in 2010 to 13.3 percent in 2020 (Source Notes S50), remains to be seen.

I cannot claim a motive for the delay. I can note that a treatment that costs more to deliver and does not increase the payment per treatment is a hard sell inside a bundle that pays a fixed amount.

## What must be done

*Make referral the default.* A randomized trial across 134 dialysis facilities in Georgia, covering more than 9,000 patients, found that a structured intervention improved referral for transplant evaluation and narrowed the racial gap in referral (Source Notes S51). That intervention should be the standard of care, funded and required in every facility, with referral to evaluation within ninety days of starting dialysis.

*Put the number on the first screen.* Every dialysis facility's rates of patient education, referral, evaluation and waitlisting should be displayed in the same place and the same size as its star rating, so that patients and families see it before they choose.

*Open the books.* The compensation of medical directors, the ownership stakes of physicians in vascular access centers and in dialysis facilities, and the relationships between charities and the companies that fund them should be disclosed in a public, searchable database.

*Ask the antitrust question.* A market in which two firms hold more than three-quarters of the business has earned an independent review by competition authorities. A review is not an accusation. It is due diligence on behalf of patients who cannot shop.

*Pay for what works.* Hemodiafiltration, home dialysis and early referral should be paid for in a way that makes them attractive to deliver, with a requirement that results be tracked in a national registry, so that whatever is learned, the system learns it quickly.

A kidney patient is not a customer. For years she is treated as one, and nothing in the economics of the wait makes the next chair her priority. The next part of the book looks at what has been invented, and left on the shelf, in the meantime.
