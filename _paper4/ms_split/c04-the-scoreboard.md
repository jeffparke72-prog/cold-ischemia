# Chapter 4: When a Measure Becomes a Target
EPIGRAPH: "When a measure becomes a target, it ceases to be a good measure."
BY: Marilyn Strathern, "'Improving Ratings': Audit in the British University System" (1997), after Charles Goodhart

:::note
**In this chapter.** *Question:* why would a transplant program decline a kidney it could use? *Answer:* because the system grades programs on the visible results of kidneys they accept and not on the invisible results of kidneys they refuse. We trace the rule that created the grading, the evidence that programs responded, the attempt to repair it, and the blind spot in the repair.
:::

In August of 2016 a reporter for STAT named Casey Ross published a story whose headline said exactly what it meant: *Hospitals are throwing out organs and denying transplants to meet federal standards.* It was picked up by PBS and a dozen other outlets, and then, like most such stories, it was forgotten (Source Notes S23). I want to bring it back, because it describes the machine this book is trying to understand, and because ten years of research since have mostly confirmed it.

## The story of a rule

Begin in 2007. That March, the federal government published a rule that changed how hospitals were allowed to perform transplants. Under the new rule, programs had to apply for Medicare approval and then keep showing, at each re-approval, that their results were acceptable. The yardstick was the one-year outcome: how many of a program's transplants were still working, and how many patients were alive, twelve months after surgery, compared with a statistical prediction of how many *should* be, given how sick the patients were and how good the organs were. A program whose results fell far enough below the prediction, and stayed there, could lose its Medicare approval and with it most of its revenue (Source Notes S24).

The intention was sound, and I want to say so plainly. In the years before the rule, some programs had poor results and little accountability. A patient has a right to know whether a hospital's transplants succeed. Measuring, publishing and enforcing that were reasonable things to do.

But look at what the rule rewarded. A transplant that failed within a year would be counted against the program that performed it. A transplant that was never performed, because the program declined the kidney or declined the patient, would not be counted at all. The yardstick measured the *visible* ledger, the yes that went wrong, and said nothing about the invisible one.

STAT's story described what happened next. A transplant surgeon at UMass Memorial, Adel Bozorgzadeh, had co-authored a study showing a sharp rise in the number of patients removed from waiting lists after the federal standards took effect. The reporter described hospitals discarding organs and declining to transplant the sickest patients out of fear that a few poor outcomes would damage their ratings. Soon after the study was published, CMS changed its benchmarks to give programs more leeway to fail (Source Notes S23).

Notice the sequence. The rule produced a behavior. The behavior produced a study. The study produced a revision. The revision is the only part of the sequence that most people remember, and it was not the end of the story.

## The people on the other side of the rule

To see what the scoreboard does to a program, imagine its director at the quarterly meeting.

She has seventy patients waiting. She is the person who would have to explain to her hospital's chief executive, and to its insurers, and to the federal agency, why the program's results slipped. She knows that three failures in a small year can move the number far. She knows that her program's published results appear on a public website where patients choose among hospitals, and where insurers choose which programs to include in their networks.

Now ask her what happens when she accepts a higher-risk kidney. If it works, she has performed a transplant that would not otherwise have happened; the patient lives, the result is unremarkable, and no one praises her. If it fails, she has a number to explain. She has been rational for years in a world where the downside of a yes is concentrated and the downside of a no is diffuse.

This is not speculation about her motives. It is the finding of a study of what happened when programs came under federal scrutiny. In 2018, a group at Johns Hopkins compared fourteen kidney transplant programs that had entered what is called a Systems Improvement Agreement with CMS, an agreement under which a struggling program agrees to a corrective plan, with twenty-eight matched programs that had not. Using national data from 2006 to 2015, they found that after the agreement, the programs' acceptance of kidney offers fell by 5.9 percentage points, a drop of about 22 percent. The programs accepted 26.9 percent of offers in the two years before the agreement and 22.1 percent in the two years after; the comparison programs went from 33.9 to 44.4 percent. The drop was largest for the most desirable kidneys (Source Notes S25).

Read that last sentence again. The programs under pressure did not only turn down marginal kidneys. They turned down the best ones too. A program in trouble becomes cautious across the board, because caution is the only thing the scoreboard rewards. The authors warned that efforts to improve outcomes could reduce access to transplantation, and they were right.

## The ratchet turns back, and the waste continues

In 2019 CMS published a rule, effective in November of that year, that removed the requirement that transplant programs meet outcome standards to keep their Medicare approval (Source Notes S26). The transplant surgeons' professional society celebrated, and said that the requirements had been so burdensome that they had led some programs to avoid transplants and discard organs, which is their claim and is not a federal finding (Source Notes S26).

You might expect the discards to fall. They rose. Nonuse was 18.2 percent in 2013. After the federal requirement was removed, it continued to climb, from the low twenties to 29.3 percent in 2024 (Source Notes S2).

I think this is the most important fact in this chapter, and it is easy to misread. It does *not* show that the 2007 rule was harmless. The Hopkins study and the reporting around it suggest it was not. What it shows is that no single rule explains the pattern. The one-year outcome is also tracked by the national network's performance committee, which reviews programs that fall below expectations. It is published by the registry. It is used by insurers deciding whom to contract with, by hospitals deciding whom to promote, and by patients deciding where to go. Remove one pressure and the others remain. The scoreboard is not a regulation. It is a culture, and cultures do not unlearn quickly.

## Trying to count the yes

To its credit, the system has begun to notice the asymmetry. In 2023, the network's performance committee began reviewing programs on a new measure: the offer acceptance ratio, which compares how many kidney offers a program accepts with how many a program with similar patients would be expected to accept. Programs with a ratio below 0.30 for adults are flagged for review (Source Notes S27). In July 2025 CMS launched a mandatory payment model, called IOTA, that scores participating transplant hospitals on three things: volume, efficiency, measured by that same offer acceptance ratio, and quality, measured by graft survival, with the three weighted 60, 20 and 20 out of a hundred (Source Notes S28). For the first time, a regulator has put a number on the yes.

I think this is the most important reform in the field in a decade, and I want it to work. But there is a blind spot in the way the ratio is built, and anyone interested in the discard problem should know it.

According to the registry's own technical documentation, offers are counted in the ratio only if they were accepted, or were declined before an offer was accepted. Offers for kidneys that were *eventually discarded* are not counted (Source Notes S27). The ratio measures the behavior of programs on the kidneys that someone, somewhere, eventually used. It is silent about the kidneys that no one did.

Think about what that means. The metric designed to reduce waste does not see the waste. A program that declines fifteen offers of a kidney that is eventually thrown away is, on this measure, indistinguishable from one that was never asked. Until it does, the ledger remains half blind. As of this writing no results from the IOTA model have been published, and the effect of the ratio on discards has not been measured.

## Why we keep making the same mistake

The sentence at the head of this chapter has a history. The economist Charles Goodhart wrote in 1975 that any statistical regularity "will tend to collapse once pressure is placed upon it for control purposes." The social scientist Donald Campbell said it more fully four years later, in the sentence that opens this book. The anthropologist Marilyn Strathern gave it the form that stuck: when a measure becomes a target, it ceases to be a good measure.

All three were describing the same thing, and none was describing transplantation. They were describing universities, schools, police departments, and the strange fact that a social indicator can be accurate only until someone's job depends on it. The lesson they drew was not that measurement is bad. It was that every measure creates a shadow, and that you must keep looking at the shadow.

The transplant system looked at the light, the number it could count, and forgot the shadow, the lives it could not.

## What must be done

*Count the shadow.* Every program's offer acceptance ratio should include offers for organs that were eventually discarded, and the national registry should publish, for each program, the number of kidneys it declined that were later transplanted elsewhere with good results. That is the closest thing to an audit of whether the no was right.

*Risk-adjust honestly.* The expected-outcome formula should account for cold time, delayed graft function and whether a kidney was machine-perfused. A program that uses a hard-to-place kidney well should be credited for the risk.

*Pair every penalty with an incentive.* A review triggered by a poor one-year outcome should be matched by a review triggered by a poor yield: programs that accept very few kidneys relative to their peers, given the same patients and the same offers.

*Put an expiry date on every rule.* Each metric and each memorandum should sunset in three years unless re-justified with evidence of its effect. The ratchet only turns one way because no one has to defend a guard once it is installed.

*Test regulation like a drug.* Before a change in oversight is imposed, state in advance what outcomes it is expected to produce, including an effect on the number of transplants, and measure afterward. The next chapter shows what happens when that is not done.
