# Chapter 11: The Pig, the Cell, and the Waiting Room
EPIGRAPH: "In the fields of observation chance favors only the prepared mind."
BY: Louis Pasteur, lecture at the University of Lille (1854)

:::note

It becomes urgent. Each technology would remove one old constraint, the clock or the lifelong drugs, and each would walk straight into the structure of Chapters 4 through 7. Count to 271.

That is how many days a man named Tim Andrews lived without dialysis on a kidney taken from a pig. The operation was done at Massachusetts General Hospital in January of 2025. The organ came from an animal whose genome had been edited by the company eGenesis so that it carried human genes and lacked several of its own, in the hope that a human immune system would leave it alone. It worked for 271 days, a record for such a kidney, until it began to fail and was removed in October (Source Notes S72). In January of 2026, according to Harvard Medical School's account, Andrews received a human kidney, becoming the first person to go from a pig kidney to a human one (Source Notes S72).

Let that number sit for a moment. For more than eight months a man who had been sentenced to a chair three afternoons a week was free of it. He could plan a week with no machine in it. When the pig kidney failed, he did not die. He went back to dialysis, and a few months later he was given a human organ. In the history of this disease there was, until recently, nothing like that.

I put this chapter in the middle of the book on purpose. The first half described a system that wastes the gifts it has. The second half describes the tools that might change it. Before I turn to what must be done, I owe you an honest look at the two most dramatic tools on the horizon, because a reader who has come this far will reasonably ask whether any of this matters if the shortage is about to be solved by a farm.

## What has actually happened

The record is short, and I will keep to what I can support. In March of 2024 surgeons at Massachusetts General transplanted a gene-edited pig kidney into a man named Richard Slayman, who died two months later; the hospital said at the time there was no indication that his death was the result of the transplant. A second patient, Towana Looney, received a pig kidney at NYU Langone in November 2024 and lived with it for roughly four months before it was removed because of rejection (Source Notes S73). These accounts come from news coverage, and I could not reconfirm every date in the sources open to me.

In the autumn of 2025 the regulators moved. The Food and Drug Administration cleared formal clinical trials in living patients. eGenesis received approval for a trial in September, and in November United Therapeutics announced the first transplant in its own trial, called EXPAND, of a kidney with ten gene edits, six human genes added and four pig genes knocked out, performed at NYU Langone, with twenty-four weeks of intensive monitoring and lifelong follow-up (Source Notes S74). A report in *Nature Medicine* in 2025 profiled the immune response in a living recipient of a gene-edited pig kidney, and a 2026 review in the *American Journal of Transplantation* surveyed the field (Source Notes S72).

That is the state of knowledge as of this writing: a handful of patients, a handful of months, a formal trial just begun. Nobody can say how it ends. But the direction of travel is clear enough to take seriously, and the first question to ask of any technology is what it would change.

## What a farm would change

The first thing is the clock. A pig kidney is not a gift that arrives at three in the morning from a stranger who has just died. It is a product, raised under controlled conditions, available on a schedule. Proponents argue that this means scheduled surgery, a recipient prepared and well, and, as a direct consequence, almost no cold ischemia time: the kidney comes out of the animal and goes into the patient in a controlled sequence. If that holds, then a large part of Chapter 3, the clock with no owner, would dissolve.

The second is supply. The list is not short of patients. It is short of organs, and a farm can in principle raise more.

The third is the discard. A kidney that is raised to a specification does not have a biopsy at two in the morning, an uncertain donor history, or a 58-year-old donor with some scarring. The factors that produce the 29.3 percent nonuse rate would, for this class of kidney, mostly not exist.

I find these genuinely hopeful. I do not think anyone should dismiss them, and I do not think anyone should be sure of them.

## What a farm would not change

Now the sobering part.

A pig kidney would still be offered to a patient, not given. It would still have to be accepted or declined by a surgeon, who would still have a scoreboard. Whether a xenotransplant counts in a program's published results, in the risk-adjusted outcomes that fed the pressure described in Chapter 4, is a question I could not find answered in any source. It should be answered *before* the first commercial transplant, not after the first program is flagged. If a failed pig-kidney transplant counts against a center the way a failed human one does, the invisible ledger will treat the pig exactly as it treated the marginal human kidney, and the surgeons who most want to use it will have the least reason to.

It would still have to be paid for. I found no published price. A technology that costs more than the dialysis it replaces will meet the five tests of Chapter 12 at a disadvantage; one that costs less will meet them with a tailwind. And it would still reach patients through the same hospitals and the same referral chains, which means the people who already have the best access to transplantation would be first in line. Chapter 2's lesson, that a kidney is lost in a queue before it is lost in a cooler, applies to a pig as to a person.

And it would still, probably, require immunosuppression. This is where the second technology enters.

## The cell

The reason transplant recipients take drugs every day for life is that the immune system sees a transplanted kidney as foreign and tries to destroy it. Those drugs work, and they cost. They increase the risk of infection and cancer, they have side effects of their own, and a patient who cannot afford them can lose the kidney. The graft survival figures from the registry, a median of about 11.7 years for kidneys from deceased donors and about 19.2 for living donors (Source Notes S52), look good, and they are also a measure of how long a body will tolerate a foreign organ under chemical restraint.

The dream of the field for fifty years has been *tolerance*: teaching the body to accept the organ as its own, so that the drugs can be stopped. The progress is real and recent. A phase 3 trial of a cell therapy called MDR-101, developed by Medeor Therapeutics for recipients of kidneys from certain living donors, treated 20 patients and compared them with 10 controls. In 2023 an interim analysis reported that 16 of 19 treated patients had been able to stop all immunosuppression at two years. The final results, published in the *American Journal of Transplantation* in July 2025, reported that about 95 percent of treated patients had been able to stop all immunosuppression roughly a year after treatment, and about 75 percent remained off the drugs for more than two years; four had to resume them (Source Notes S75). A nephrologist at a leading center, interviewed in April 2026, described tolerance as the frontier of kidney care (Source Notes S76).

The numbers are small. The therapy applies to a narrow group. The control group is ten patients. I would not tell a reader that the problem is solved. But think about what it would mean: a person who has taken a handful of pills every day for twenty years, who has been through the infections and the cancers and the monthly bill, could someday walk away from all of it. That is a different kind of freedom from the one a pig kidney offers, and in some ways a deeper one.

## The waiting room

Both technologies share a waiting room, and it is the one this book has been describing.

Whoever manages the first fifty xenotransplants will decide something important by accident: who the technology is for. The trials have inclusion criteria, and the people who meet them are not a random sample of the waiting list. Whoever receives the first cell therapy will be those with a living donor, which means those with a family willing and able to give. Neither is a scandal. Both will have consequences for fairness, and consequences that are easy to see now and hard to reverse later.

This is why I ask, in the fourth part of this book, that trials of new technologies report their enrollment by race, geography, income and sex, as they go. A technology that reaches only the well connected will deepen the divide it was meant to heal.

It is also why I think the five tests of Chapter 12 should be applied to these technologies in advance. Does the person who must act see the benefit? Does the one who pays gain? Does it move a number someone is judged on? Is it easy at three in the morning? If the answer to the third question is *no*, because a pig kidney that fails will hurt a program's results and one that works will not help them, then the technology will wait on the shelf, and we will have built a miracle and then hidden it.

## What must be done

*Decide the scoreboard first.* The federal agencies that oversee transplant programs should state, before the first commercial xenotransplant, how it will count in program evaluations, and should protect centers that adopt it responsibly from penalty for expected early failures.

*Publish the enrollment.* Every xenotransplantation and tolerance trial should publicly report who is enrolled, by race, region, income and sex, quarterly.

*Plan the payment.* The federal payer should publish, in advance, how it will pay for a xenotransplant and a tolerance therapy, so that the first patients are not chosen by their insurance.

*Prepare the system, not just the procedure.* The pump, the offer ledger and the fifteen moves of Part Four matter even if every promise of this chapter comes true, because the structure that a miracle will enter is the one we have, and it should be a good one.

Pasteur was talking about scientists. I think the sentence applies to systems. Chance, in the form of a pig, a cell or a trial, will favor only the system that is ready.

The last chapter of this part steps back from individual tools and asks why some good ideas spread in months and others take a generation, and how we can tell which of the slow ones deserve to win.
