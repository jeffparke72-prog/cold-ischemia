# Chapter 15: The Last Hour
EPIGRAPH: "A partnership not only between those who are living, but between those who are living, those who are dead, and those who are to be born."
BY: Edmund Burke, Reflections on the Revolution in France (1790)

:::note
**In this chapter.** *Question:* what do we owe a gift, and what would it mean to keep faith with one? *Answer:* a gift is held in trust. A system that wastes it has broken a trust, not merely lost an efficiency. The closing chapter restates the argument in three answers, says what would prove it wrong, and describes the night I want.
:::

There is a moment at the end of every transplant that nobody photographs.

The surgeon has joined the artery and the vein. The clamps come off. For seventeen hours, or nineteen, or thirty, the kidney has lain pale in its bath of cold fluid, and then, over the space of a few seconds, it takes on color, like a face coming back after a faint. A few moments later it begins to make urine. Someone in the room says something, usually something small and practical. The recipient, asleep, is the last to know.

I have never been in that room as the surgeon. I have been near it, and that is enough. What I understand about that moment is that it is the one place in the whole system where the gift becomes whole. Everything this book has described, the numbers, the memo, the scoreboard, the pump, is an argument about how many such moments there will be.

## Three answers

I promised at the beginning that you would be able to answer three questions without notes. Here are the answers, as plainly as I can give them.

*Where do the kidneys go?* About twenty-nine in a hundred of the kidneys recovered in the United States in 2024 were never transplanted. By my arithmetic that is roughly 8,800 kidneys, about twenty-four a day, in a year when 3,743 people died waiting and 4,901 more became too sick to be transplanted (Chapter 1).

*Why do they go there?* Because the system is built to notice a yes that goes wrong and to ignore a no that goes wrong. A surgeon who accepts a marginal kidney and fails is visible, counted and graded. A surgeon who declines it, and the patient who dies waiting, are neither. Programs under federal scrutiny accepted a fifth fewer offers afterward, including the best kidneys (Chapter 4). A single memorandum cut recoveries within seven weeks (Chapter 5). The clock that damages kidneys has no owner (Chapter 3). The front door has lost public trust (Chapter 6). And the business of dialysis is paid by the chair (Chapter 7).

*What would stop it?* Fifteen moves, in five groups, each with an owner and a test: pay for what works, make the clock and the list visible, fix the scoreboard, protect the front door and show the work, and open the industry and seat the patients (Chapter 13). Start with the cheap and fast. Choose one. Ask for a date (Chapter 14).

## What I may have gotten wrong

A book that argues this hard owes you its own weak points.

I may be overestimating the share of discarded kidneys that were viable. Experts reviewing the 2022 discards estimated 62 percent, and I have used that figure, but it is an estimate and the real figure might be lower. The French comparison is a model. My arithmetic about 8,800 kidneys and $185 million is mine and rests on assumptions I have tried to state.

I may be underestimating the legitimate reasons surgeons decline kidneys. Donors are older and sicker. A delayed graft is a real harm. A transplant program that takes on too many risky kidneys can do damage. The studies I cite on the effect of federal scrutiny are observational, and the memo analyses are early and brief.

I may be wrong about the pump's reach. It is possible that its benefit is concentrated in particular kinds of kidneys, and that the right national figure is lower than I imply. I have said the evidence is strong. I have not said it is complete.

And I cannot say whether the oxygen carriers will work. I hope they do. I have said so, and I have said what a proper trial would look like.

So here is what would prove me wrong. If the nonuse rate falls to 18 percent without any change in the incentives, the scoreboard is not the explanation. If a rigorous trial of the pump fails to replicate, I have misjudged the evidence. If the offer ledger is introduced and nothing changes about the way centers decide, the invisible ledger is not as invisible as I think. I would count each of these as news, and I would rather learn them from you than from a reviewer.

## The pig and the ledger

A future is arriving, and Chapter 11 described it: a gene-edited pig kidney that kept a man off dialysis for 271 days, and a cell therapy that let most of a small group of patients stop their anti-rejection drugs. If either becomes routine, it will enter exactly the system this book has described, offered through a computer, accepted or declined by the same programs, graded by the same scoreboard, shipped on the same clock and paid for by the same payers. A technology that arrives in a broken structure does not fix the structure. It inherits it. That is why the fifteen moves matter even if you believe, as I half do, that biotechnology will someday end the shortage.

## What we owe a gift

I said at the beginning that I would not talk much about philosophy. I want to break that promise for one page, because I think the philosophy is the point.

A gift is not a transaction. When a family says yes in a corridor at two in the morning, they are not selling. They are giving something that cannot be returned to someone they will never meet. The law reflects this: in the United States it is a federal crime to buy or sell an organ. We have decided, as a country, that the kidney is held outside the market.

But a thing held outside the market is held *in trust*. A trustee has duties. She must not squander what she has been given. She must not hold it for a reason that serves herself. She must account for it. The family, the donor, the recipient and the people still waiting are the beneficiaries, and the system is the trustee. By that standard the discard is not just inefficiency. It is a breach.

The conservative tradition has a name for this duty, and I have found no better. Edmund Burke wrote that a society is a partnership, not only among those who are living but between those who are living, those who are dead, and those who are to be born. A kidney donor is dead. The donor's family is living. The people still on the list are living, and their children, and the people who will one day need a kidney and are not yet born. The partnership is among all of them. Each kidney thrown away is a breach of faith with all of them at once.

The problem is not mysterious. We know what a trustee does. She keeps an honest ledger. She counts what she was given and what she used. She tells the beneficiaries. She does not hide behind a metric. The invisible ledger is a failure of trusteeship, and the remedy is the oldest one there is: make the accounts open.

Francis Bacon, at the beginning of the book, said that time is the greatest innovator. He meant that if you do not change on purpose, change will be done to you. I would add the corollary that this book has been trying to prove. Time is also the greatest discarder. What is not used in time is lost. A kidney has a deadline, and so does every patient on the list, and so does every reader who has come this far and is wondering what to do next.

## The night I want

Let me end where I began, with a night.

It is the same Tuesday, and the same phone rings at 11:42 in a transplant center forty minutes from Denise's house. The offer is the same: a fifty-eight-year-old donor, some scarring on a frozen section, nineteen hours of cold time. The surgeon on call is as tired as the first surgeon was, and she is looking at the same screen.

But a few things are different, and none of them is a miracle.

The kidney has been on a pump since recovery, and the numbers from the pump are on the screen beside the biopsy, because her center has a protocol and the procurement organization is paid to provide it. The kidney's journey has been tracked, and the dashboard shows that it was packed forty minutes after recovery and has lost less time than the median. The surgeon knows her center's offer acceptance ratio, and she knows the ratio counts offers for kidneys that are eventually discarded, which makes her more careful about saying no to one she would not say no to again in a year. She knows that her decision will appear, with its reason, in a report that Denise will read.

She says: *Accept.* It is not a certainty. It is a bet, a better-informed one, made in a system that no longer hides the cost of the other bet.

Or she says: *Decline,* and records a reason, and the reason is checked against what becomes of the kidney, and her center learns from it.

Either way, at 7:10 the next morning, Denise's phone shows a message. It says that a kidney was offered for her last night. It says what was decided, and why. And it gives a number to call.

She calls it. She asks what it would take to say yes next time.

That is all I want: a system in which the person at the end of the chain can ask that question, and someone has to answer.

I am not sure we will get there. I am sure that the people who could take us are mostly decent, mostly tired, and mostly waiting for someone to ask. So ask. The clock is running, and it has an owner now. The owner is whoever picks up the phone.
