# Prologue: Offer Fourteen
EPIGRAPH: "Life is short, and the Art long; the occasion fleeting; experience fallacious, and judgment difficult."
BY: Hippocrates, Aphorisms, section 1 (Adams translation, 1849)

The phone rings at 11:42 on a Tuesday night, and it is not Denise's phone.

It rings in a transplant center forty minutes from her house, on the desk of a coordinator who has done this for eleven years and can tell from the first syllable what kind of call it is. A donor has died in a hospital two states away. The family said yes. The kidneys have been recovered, packed in ice, and offered, through a national computer system that sorted every person waiting in the country by blood type, tissue match, years on the list and a dozen smaller things, to the people at the top of the list. Denise is near the top. She has been on dialysis for six years and on the list for five. She is fifty-four. She teaches fourth grade, and she has not missed a Monday, a Wednesday or a Friday treatment in all that time, because she has been told, over and over, that being reliable is how you get chosen.

She is asleep. Her phone is on the nightstand, face down.

Go back eight hours, to the other end of the night. In a hospital two states away a woman in her sixties is sitting beside a bed, holding a hand that is warm and will stay warm for a while yet. A neurologist has explained, twice, in plain words, what the tests show. A different person, a stranger with a lanyard and a soft voice, has asked whether the family has ever talked about donation. The woman says that her husband carried the little red heart on his driver's license for thirty years. She says yes. She signs. She will go home before the kidneys leave the building, and she will not know, for a very long time, what became of them. Perhaps she never will. The families who say yes are often told very little about what happened to the gift.

Surgeons arrive near midnight. The kidneys come out in the practiced, quiet way that strangers have been learning for seventy years, are flushed with cold fluid, and sealed in sterile bags inside a box of ice. From that moment a clock starts that no one in the building can see. The kidney has no blood flowing through it. It is living on what it stored, and on cold, and every hour that passes is an hour it will carry into whoever receives it.

The coordinator reads the screen aloud to the surgeon on call. The donor is fifty-eight. He had high blood pressure. The kidney was biopsied at the donor hospital, and a pathologist, reading a frozen slice of tissue in the middle of the night, saw some scarring. The kidney will have been without blood flow for about nineteen hours by the time it could reach the operating table. It is a kidney with a few warning signs and a good chance anyway, the kind that clinicians argue about, the kind that on any given night one team will take and another will not.

The surgeon is tired. She was in the operating room until nine. She thinks about Denise, who has diabetes, who is not young, who will be a little sicker than average on the table. She thinks about the number the government publishes every six months about how many of her patients' new kidneys are still working a year later, and about what happens to a program when that number slips below what a formula predicted. She thinks, because she is honest, that she may be right to say no. The kidney might not work right away. Denise might spend two weeks in the hospital she is trying so hard to leave.

She says: *Decline.*

The coordinator types a reason into a drop-down menu. It takes eleven seconds. Somewhere in a data center a list re-sorts itself, and the kidney is offered to the next person, and the next. By morning it will have been offered to a dozen people, in a dozen centers, with a dozen versions of the same thought.

At 7:10 Denise wakes up, checks her phone, finds nothing, and drives to her chair.

I have never met Denise. She is invented, and I am telling you so now because the rest of this book will insist on the difference between what is invented and what is counted. The night is a composite. Its parts are not. In a study of about fourteen million kidney offers made between 2008 and 2015, researchers found that candidates who died without a transplant had received a median of sixteen offers over 651 days on the list (Source Notes S1). Denise's call was offer fourteen. In the great majority of declines, the centers recorded the reason as something wrong with the organ or the donor. Some of those declines were surely right. The researchers said so, and so will I.

But sit with the arithmetic for a moment. In 2024, according to the national registry, 29.3 percent of the kidneys recovered from deceased donors in the United States were never transplanted into anyone (Source Notes S2). Eleven years earlier the figure was 18.2 percent. The families said yes. Surgeons drove through the night. Planes flew. And nearly three in every ten of those kidneys never reached a body.

That is the number this book follows.

I did not set out to write an angry book, and I do not think this is one. Everyone in the story I am about to tell is, as far as I can find, trying to do the right thing: the surgeon who says no, the coordinator who types the reason, the regulator who writes the rule, the family who signed the form. The tragedy, if there is one, is that a system made of decent people can arrange itself so that the safest choice for each of them is the costliest choice for someone they will never meet.

It is also not a story without hope. Along the edges of the problem sit tools that were proven years ago and never fully used: a pump that keeps a kidney alive in transit, and that, in a ten-year follow-up of a randomized trial, left more kidneys working a decade later. A molecule borrowed from a marine worm that carries forty times more oxygen than our own, by its makers' account. A fleet of new dialysis machines that, in a large European trial, lowered the death rate, and that American patients are only now beginning to see. A memo, sent on a single day in August of 2025, that changed the behavior of an entire national network within weeks, and in the wrong direction.

You will meet all of them. By the end I want you to be able to answer three questions without notes: where the kidneys go, why they go there, and what, specifically, would stop it.

Denise, in the meantime, has an appointment with her nephrologist next week. She will ask whether anything has come up. She will be told, kindly, that she is still on the list.
