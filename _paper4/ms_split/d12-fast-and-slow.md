# Chapter 12: The Fast Idea and the Slow Idea
EPIGRAPH: "The reformer has enemies in all who profit by the old order, and only lukewarm defenders in all those who would profit by the new order."
BY: Niccolò Machiavelli, The Prince, chapter 6 (Thomson translation, 1910)

:::note
**In this chapter.** *Question:* why do some good ideas spread in months and others in generations, and how can we tell a slow idea that deserves to win from one that deserves to lose? *Answer:* speed depends less on the quality of the evidence than on five practical tests, and the same tests that explain why the pump waited also explain why a cheap, appealing idea about dialysis temperature was adopted by intuition and then failed a large trial.
:::

In October of 1846, in the amphitheater of a Boston hospital, a dentist named William Morton put a man to sleep with ether while a surgeon removed a tumor from his neck. The patient felt, he said afterward, nothing. The news crossed the Atlantic within weeks. Within months, surgeons in European capitals were using ether. Within a few years, nearly every operating room in the Western world was (Source Notes S77).

Seven months after that, in the spring of 1847, in a maternity hospital in Vienna, a young obstetrician named Ignaz Semmelweis noticed that women delivered by medical students died of childbed fever at a far higher rate than women delivered by midwives, and he made a guess about why. The students came to the delivery ward straight from the autopsy room. He ordered them to wash their hands in a solution of chlorinated lime. By the commonly cited figures, the death rate in his ward fell in two months from about 18 percent to about 2 percent. His colleagues largely refused to believe him. He lost his post, and died in 1865 in an asylum. The idea that germs cause infection, and that surgeons should clean their hands and instruments, took most of a generation to become routine (Source Notes S77).

Same century, same hospitals, same profession, two ideas. One spread in months. The other took decades.

## Why anesthesia won

The surgeon and writer Atul Gawande asked this question in an essay called "Slow Ideas" in *The New Yorker* in 2013, and his answer is the best short theory of adoption I know. Anesthesia, he argued, was an obvious, immediate, visible benefit to everyone in the room. The patient stopped screaming. The surgeon could work slowly. The benefit arrived the moment the idea was used and could be seen by anyone watching. Antisepsis, by contrast, demanded effort now for a benefit later, invisible and statistical: "pain now for gain later," in the phrase quoted from him. A surgeon had to fuss with carbolic acid and scrub, and the reward was an infection that did not happen, which no one could see. It took regulation and a change of professional culture to make the practice stick (Source Notes S77).

Gawande's contrast has a corollary that matters here. A review of the essay notes that money does not explain the difference: the incentives ran in the right direction for both ideas. What mattered was the shape of the benefit: who could see it, how fast, and what it cost the person asked to change.

The surgeon with a marginal kidney, the organization with a pump, the dialysis manager with a new machine, are all in the position of the nineteenth-century surgeon deciding whether to wash his hands.

## Five tests

From this, and from the cases in the earlier chapters, I have built a short method. I use it as a checklist and not as a law. Ask five questions of any new tool.

*One: is the evidence good?* Randomized, replicated, with meaningful follow-up, not a company's press release.

*Two: can the person who must act see the benefit?* A pump that prevents a complication a surgeon will see in a patient eight days later passes. A tool whose benefit shows up as a statistical improvement in outcomes five years from now fails.

*Three: does the person who bears the cost also gain?* If the benefit goes to one party and the cost to another, the idea is an orphan.

*Four: does it improve a number someone is judged on?* A tool that raises a program's scoreboard result will be adopted. A tool that improves lives but not a metric will not.

*Five: does it require someone to change a routine at 3 a.m.?* Every tool that must work in an operating room at night, or in a courier chain, or in a dialysis unit's third shift, has to work for the person who is tired.

The table is crude, and the entries in several cells are my judgment. But look at the pattern. The pump, the best-evidenced idea of the lot, fails three of the five tests. The idea that was easiest to adopt, and was adopted by intuition, is the one whose largest trial found nothing.

## The idea that deserved to lose

I owe you the story of the cooler fluid, because a book about slow ideas has to include a fast one that was wrong.

For years, nephrologists had observed that some patients felt better, with fewer drops in blood pressure, when the fluid used in dialysis was a little cooler than body temperature. Small studies supported the idea, some with dozens of patients. The reasoning was sensible: cooler blood constricts vessels and holds pressure. The change was almost free. A unit could implement it in an afternoon, and patients who felt better said so.

In 2022 the largest trial ever done in hemodialysis tested it. Called MyTEMP, it was a cluster-randomized trial across dialysis centers in Ontario, using the province's health records, and it compared a personalized cooler dialysate, 0.5 to 0.9 degrees below each patient's own temperature, with a standard fluid at 36.5 degrees. The outcome was cardiovascular death or hospital admission for a heart attack, stroke or heart failure. It occurred in 21.4 percent of patients receiving the cooler fluid and 22.4 percent of those receiving the standard, a difference too small to be distinguished from chance. Blood-pressure drops during treatment were the same (Source Notes S4). The authors concluded that there was no justification for adopting cooler fluid for all patients, and noted that some patients found it uncomfortable.

John Stuart Mill wrote that "he who knows only his own side of the case, knows little of that." I have told you much of one side of the case in this book, the side that says the system is too slow. MyTEMP is the other side. A system that adopts every plausible idea at once would be as bad as one that adopts none, and for the same reason: it has no way of finding out.

The lesson is not that cooler dialysis is useless. Some patients may benefit, and the trial measured heart events, not how a patient feels. The lesson is that intuition is a poor guide, and that fast adoption without a trial is just a different kind of failure.

## Why evidence alone does not win

The physicist Max Planck, in the last pages of his autobiography, offered a bleak account of how scientific ideas succeed. "A new scientific truth does not triumph by convincing its opponents and making them see the light," he wrote, "but rather because its opponents eventually die, and a new generation grows up that is familiar with it."

It is a funny sentence, and the field of kidney medicine cannot afford it. Planck's remedy, waiting for a generation to pass, is a remedy for physicists. Patients on the list die on a shorter schedule. In 2024 alone, 3,743 candidates were removed from the kidney waiting list because they died (Source Notes S6). The cost of waiting for the old to retire is counted in people.

What the five tests suggest is that adoption is not mostly a matter of persuasion. It is a matter of design. A tool becomes routine when the person who must use it can see the benefit, pays a fair price, is judged by the result, and finds it easy to do at night. None of these requires waiting for anyone to die. All of them require someone to decide that adoption is their job.

## The owner of the adoption

And that is the missing piece. In the case of the pump, the oxygen carrier, hemodiafiltration, and the offer ledger of Chapter 2, no institution has the task of moving a proven idea from the journal to the bedside. The journals publish, the regulators approve, the payers reimburse, the societies recommend. None of them owns the gap between what is known and what is done.

Machiavelli, five centuries ago, said what to expect: that the reformer will have many enemies and few defenders. He was describing princes, but the pattern generalizes. The defenders of the new order are lukewarm because they do not yet have what it will give them. The enemies are fierce because they have what the old order gave them now.

## What must be done

*Make adoption someone's job.* For any intervention with randomized evidence of benefit in kidney disease or transplantation, the responsible federal agency should be required to publish, within twelve months, an adoption plan: who pays, who owns the process, who measures, and by what date. If it chooses not to adopt, it should say why.

*Run the cheap, large trial.* MyTEMP was possible because Ontario's dialysis centers were part of a registry that could randomize at the level of the center and measure outcomes through health records. The United States has the data infrastructure to do the same. A national program of pragmatic trials of plausible dialysis practices, including temperature, session length, hemodiafiltration and home therapy, would turn intuition into evidence quickly and cheaply.

*Apply the five tests in public.* Whenever a federal agency or professional society reviews a new tool, it should publish how the tool scores on the five tests, so that the barriers to adoption are visible and can be addressed.

*Sunset the ideas that fail.* A practice that has been tested and found useless should be removed from guidelines and payment, and the people who adopted it should hear about it.

Slow ideas and fast ideas are not opposites. They are two ways of ignoring the evidence. The fourth part of this book is about the decisions that follow from taking it seriously.
