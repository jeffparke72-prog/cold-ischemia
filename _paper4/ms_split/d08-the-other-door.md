# Chapter 8: The Other Door
EPIGRAPH: "Greater love hath no man than this, that a man lay down his life for his friends."
BY: John 15:13 (King James Version, 1611)

:::note
**In this chapter.** *Question:* if the front door of the system, the deceased donor, leaks, what about the other door, the living donor? *Answer:* it is the most reliable supply we have, it has not grown back to its 2019 level, and the reason is partly that we ask the person who gives a kidney to carry costs that the program saving the most money does not. It is the invisible ledger again, with a human name on it.
:::

Meet a man I will call Teodoro. He is invented, and everything about his situation is ordinary.

He is forty-one and he wires houses. He is paid by the hour, and he has no paid leave. His younger brother, thirty-seven, is on dialysis, has been for two years, and has been told by a transplant center that the quickest route to a new kidney is a living donor. Teodoro is a match. He is also, as the evaluating team's financial coordinator explains to him, kindly and without expecting him to be surprised, going to need about six weeks away from work after the surgery, during which no one will pay him. The center will pay for the evaluation and the operation, as the law and custom require, but not for the gas to drive three hours each way for the appointments, not for the motel, not for his lost wages, not for the child care when his wife drives him, not for the rent.

Teodoro does the arithmetic on the drive home. He is a good brother. He will do it. And as he does the sum, he understands something that is invisible to the system he is about to enter: that he is about to save a government program about fifty-five thousand dollars a year, for as many years as his brother's kidney lasts, by paying out of his own pocket for the privilege.

I want to take a chapter to look at this door, because it is the one supply of kidneys that does not depend on a tragedy, a clock, a pump, a regulator or a memo. It depends on one person's yes. And because it is the cleanest illustration in the book of what happens when the benefit of a good thing lands on one party and the cost on another.

## What a living kidney is worth

A kidney from a living donor tends to outlast one from a deceased donor, and the registry numbers are striking: in one analysis, the median survival of a transplanted kidney from a living donor was about 19.2 years in recent transplants, against 11.7 years for deceased-donor kidneys (Source Notes S52). A living-donor transplant is scheduled. The cold time is minutes. The surgery can happen when the recipient is well, before the years of dialysis have taken their toll.

For the payer the arithmetic is as plain as I have given it elsewhere. In 2021 traditional Medicare spent about $99,325 per person on in-center hemodialysis and about $43,913 on a person with a functioning transplant, a difference of $55,412 a year (Source Notes S10). Every living-donor transplant that lasts the median span returns that difference for years.

Now ask who pays. In the American system, by custom and by law, the recipient's insurance covers the donor's medical evaluation and surgery. What it does not cover is nearly everything else, and the person who absorbs that is the donor.

## The number that has not recovered

Here is the count. A news report that cites national registry figures puts the number of living kidney donors in the United States at 6,290 in 2023, 6,419 in 2024 and 6,522 in 2025, against 6,867 in 2019 (Source Notes S53). I give this as a single-source figure, and a registry table should replace it. If it is right, living donation in 2025 was still about 5 percent below where it stood before the pandemic, in a country whose waiting list had grown.

It is also worth stating what long-run analyses have found. Living kidney donation in the United States fell after a peak in the middle of the 2000s, with the drop largest among men, Black adults and younger and lower-income people (Source Notes S54). A 2026 article in the *Medical Journal of Australia* found 253 living-donor kidney transplants in 2024, against 354 in 2008, with lower socioeconomic groups disadvantaged (Source Notes S54). The pattern is not American alone. And it is not what you would expect of a pure act of generosity, which should not depend on one's income.

That pattern is the signature of a cost. When a gift is made harder for the poor than for the comfortable, the thing that is being measured is not generosity. It is the price of generosity.

## What the law just did

In February of 2026 Congress did something about it. The Honor Our Living Donors Act, enacted as part of the Consolidated Appropriations Act, 2026 (Public Law 119-75) on February 3, directs the federal living-donor reimbursement program to stop considering the recipient's household income in deciding whether a donor qualifies, and to reimburse donors for travel, lost wages, and child and elder care, up to a ceiling of $6,000 per organ, with a duty to report annually to Congress. The agency published a request for public comment on its approach in July 2026. In the donor-side test that remains, priority in the proposal goes to donors at or below 350 percent of the poverty guidelines (Source Notes S55).

I regard this as one of the most important pieces of transplant legislation of the decade, and I want it to work. I also want to point to two details in the sources. One, a letter from a physicians' group reported that roughly 21 percent of donors reach the $6,000 cap, which means that for one donor in five the ceiling does not cover the cost. Two, the program is a reimbursement, which means the donor must front the money and wait.

Private programs have grown up to fill part of the gap. The National Kidney Registry and the American Kidney Fund describe programs that offer wage reimbursement and life-insurance protection to some donors (Source Notes S56). The American Kidney Fund's May 2026 report card on state protections for living donors found gains in several states and little or no progress in many (Source Notes S56). These are welcome. They are also a reminder that for years the cost of a gift was handled as an act of charity by nonprofits, not as an obligation of the program that saves the money.

A bill pending in the House would go further. The End Kidney Deaths Act, introduced in April 2025, proposes a refundable tax credit for people who donate a kidney to a stranger, $10,000 in the year of donation and in each of the following four years, for $50,000 in all, applying to kidneys removed after 2026 and expiring in 2036. It had about sixty cosponsors by one campaign's count in March 2026, and a policy tracker rated its chance of passage low (Source Notes S57). Its supporters say it would increase donation and cut dialysis costs. Its critics worry about the ethics of financial incentives for an organ that federal law forbids buying. I am not endorsing or condemning it. I raise it because the argument it provokes is the right one: the present arrangement, in which the donor's generosity is rewarded with a bill, cannot be right.

## The chain

There is one innovation in this part of the system that has worked, and it deserves its due.

The idea of paired exchange is simple to state: two donor-recipient pairs, each incompatible with the other, swap. The mathematics lets the swaps grow into cycles and then into chains, begun by a donor who gives to no one in particular. The National Kidney Registry reported a chain that began in 2008 and produced thirty transplants across seventeen hospitals in eleven states over four months (Source Notes S58). The University of Alabama at Birmingham announced a long chain in which 74 donors and 74 recipients took part, later describing more than a hundred recipients (Source Notes S58). The Registry says that in 2024 it facilitated 1,744 transplants, had by then passed its ten-thousandth living-donor transplant, and was expected to account for roughly 30 percent of living-donor kidney transplants in the country in 2025 (Source Notes S59). Those figures come from the organization itself.

I mention the chain for the lesson, not the statistics. The chain is a tool that turned a refusal, the wrong blood type, into a transplant. It was an invention, it worked, and it scaled, and it is a good example of the orphan innovation escaping. The reason, I think, is that its benefit and its cost both landed on a party that could manage them: a nonprofit registry whose whole job was to make the matches.

## The silence of asking

There is another barrier, and it costs nothing to fix.

Most people on the list never ask anyone for a kidney. The reasons are human. They do not want to be a burden, and they cannot imagine saying the words. A program developed at Johns Hopkins, called Live Donor Champion, was built around this silence: instead of asking the patient to ask, it trains a friend or relative to speak for her. In a pilot with fifteen candidates and fifteen matched controls, and a follow-up of 163 candidates in which 81 produced referrals of potential donors, the program was associated with roughly 5.5 times the odds of a donor referral (Source Notes S60). The evidence is from conference abstracts and registry entries, and there is no randomized trial that I found. But the program costs little, and a randomized trial would be easy to do.

A final barrier is time. One paper applying process-improvement methods to the living-donor evaluation reported that about a quarter of potential living-donor transplants are missed for reasons related to inadequate timing (Source Notes S61). That is a finding with a familiar shape. The person is willing; the kidney is a match; and the process takes so long that the moment passes, the donor's circumstances change, or the recipient gets too sick.

## What must be done

*Finish the law.* The living-donor reimbursement program created by the 2026 act should reimburse promptly, not after months, with an advance for travel and lodging; and the ceiling should be set against the real costs, with an annual adjustment, because a ceiling that one donor in five hits is a ceiling that is too low. The program saves money. It can afford to be generous.

*Make a champion available to every candidate.* Every transplant center should offer the champion model, or something like it, to every candidate, and a randomized trial of it should be run. It is the cheapest intervention in this book.

*Shorten the evaluation.* Each center should publish the time from a potential donor's first call to a decision, and set a target. The process-improvement literature shows it can be shortened.

*Protect the donor.* Job-protected leave, protection from insurance discrimination and life-insurance coverage for donors should be the baseline in every state, not a patchwork. The American Kidney Fund's report card is the right scoreboard. It should be a matter of federal law.

*Study the tax credit honestly.* A pilot of a donor credit, with an independent evaluation, would answer the questions that the argument has raised: does it increase donation, who benefits, and does it distort the gift? I would rather know than argue.

Teodoro, in the end, gives his brother a kidney. He loses six weeks of pay and, though he does not tell his brother, nearly his truck. What he gets is a brother at the next Thanksgiving. The system that gained fifty-five thousand dollars a year from his decision will never know his name. It is a small thing to ask that, the next time, the system say thank you and mean it.
