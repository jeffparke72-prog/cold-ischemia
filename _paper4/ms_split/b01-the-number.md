# Chapter 1: The Number
EPIGRAPH: "The cost of a thing is the amount of what I will call life which is required to be exchanged for it, immediately or in the long run."
BY: Henry David Thoreau, Walden (1854)

:::note
**In this chapter.** *Question:* how many donated kidneys does the United States fail to use, and how does that compare with the people who die waiting? *Answer:* roughly 29 in every 100, which by my arithmetic is about 8,800 kidneys a year, close to the number of people removed from the list in the same year because they died or became too sick. Not one-for-one, but too close to ignore.
:::

I found the number in a table.

That is how most terrible numbers are found. They do not announce themselves. They sit in the overview chapter of an annual report, in the third paragraph, written by statisticians who have learned to speak of grief in the passive voice. The report is the annual data report of the national transplant registry for 2024, published in 2026, and the sentence says that the proportion of deceased-donor kidneys recovered for transplant but not transplanted rose to 29.3 percent (Source Notes S2).

I read it twice. Then I did what I would ask you to do now, which is to put it beside the year before, and the year before that, and the year the series began.

## The story of a line

In 2013, 18.2 percent of recovered kidneys went unused. By 2022 the figure was 26.6 percent. In 2023 it was 27.9. In 2024 it was 29.3.

[[FIG:nonuse|Figure 1.1. Share of recovered deceased-donor kidneys not transplanted, United States. Source: OPTN/SRTR annual data reports.]]

Notice what is happening around that line. Over the same decade, the system set records for everything else. A record 27,660 kidney transplants were performed in 2024, up from 27,351 the year before (Source Notes S2). A record 49,249 adults were added to the waiting list in a single year, and 143,982 adults were on it (Source Notes S6). More donors, more recoveries, more transplants, more people waiting, and a rising share of the gifts going to waste. It is the kind of pattern that looks, at first glance, like a paradox. It is not. It is what you would expect if the supply were growing faster than the system's willingness to use it.

The registry's own authors seem to read it that way. In the 2024 report they note that progress in recoveries and transplants has been offset by rising nonuse, longer cold ischemia times and higher rates of delayed graft function under broader sharing of organs (Source Notes S6). The sentence is carefully written. It does not say whose fault any of it is.

The same pattern has now reached the evening news. In February 2026 an investigative television team reported that about one in five donated organs of every kind in the United States is never transplanted, and that for kidneys the share is higher still (Source Notes S7). The registry's table and the reporter's headline are the same fact, told in two voices.

Behind every one of those unused kidneys there is a yes. A family sat in a corridor, heard the question, and answered it. Nothing in the registry records what it felt like to say yes, or what it would have felt like to learn, a year later, that the gift had been left on a shelf. That is the part of the loss that has no column.

## Doing the arithmetic

The registry reports a rate. To see the loss you need a count, and the count is harder to find than it should be, so I did what I could with what I had. This is my arithmetic, and I give the inputs so that you can check it.

In 2024 there were 27,660 kidney transplants in the United States. Roughly 6,400 of them used kidneys from living donors (Source Notes S8), which leaves about 21,200 from deceased donors. If 29.3 percent of recovered kidneys went unused, then those 21,200 represent the other 70.7 percent, so about 30,000 kidneys were recovered, and about 8,800 were not used. That is roughly twenty-four a day.

I would not defend the last digit. Counts from the registry will differ a little from my estimate, because of how some multi-organ and paired recoveries are classified. A cross-check: for 2022, a report based on national data counted 7,540 unused kidneys, 26 percent of those recovered (Source Notes S9). At the rate the series was climbing, 8,800 two years later is the right order of magnitude.

Now set a second number beside it. In 2024, 3,743 candidates were removed from the kidney waiting list because they died, and another 4,901 were removed because they had become too sick to be transplanted (Source Notes S6). Together, 8,644 people.

[[FIG:ledger|Figure 1.2. Kidneys recovered but not used (my estimate) and candidates removed from the list for death or illness, 2024.]]

I need to be careful here, and I want to be seen being careful. The 8,800 kidneys and the 8,644 people are not the same thing. A great many of the unused kidneys would not have suited the people who died; a great many of those people were too ill or too complicated for any kidney. Experts who reviewed the 2022 discards estimated that roughly 62 percent of the unused kidneys would have been viable, which would mean 4,675 dialysis patients who could have received one and did not (Source Notes S9). Four thousand six hundred is not eight thousand. It is also not a small number.

What the comparison does is change the scale of the question. It makes it impossible to say that the shortage is simply a matter of there not being enough kidneys. In the same year the system lost nearly as many gifts as it lost lives, and it recorded the first as a statistic and the second as a tragedy.

## What a kidney is worth

There is one more number I want to put on the table, because it is the one that will matter to the people who write the checks.

According to the national dialysis registry, the per-person annual cost to traditional Medicare in 2021 was about $99,325 for a patient on in-center hemodialysis and about $43,913 for a patient with a functioning transplant (Source Notes S10). The difference is $55,412 a year.

Suppose nonuse returned to its 2013 level of 18.2 percent. Of 30,000 recovered kidneys, 5,460 would go unused instead of 8,800. That is about 3,300 more transplants a year. At the 2021 difference, that is roughly $185 million a year in dialysis cost avoided, in a scenario where the only change is that the kidneys already recovered are put to use. I offer this as a scenario, not a forecast. It leaves out the cost of the transplants themselves, the drugs, the complications. But it does not include what matters most to the people involved, which is time. Each of those 3,300 people would be on dialysis for years less than they otherwise would be.

## Who gets thrown away

The kidneys that go unused are not a random sample. They have a profile, and the profile is instructive.

Kidneys from donors aged sixty-five or older went unused 68.9 percent of the time in 2024. Kidneys that had been biopsied at the time of recovery went unused 40.8 percent of the time; kidneys that had not been biopsied, 6.4 percent (Source Notes S2). That last contrast is the most striking in the whole report, and I will spend a chapter on it. A tool meant to assess quality appears to be one of the strongest predictors of waste.

The reasons recorded for declines point the same way. In one analysis of national discards, the single most common reason was that no recipient could be located, at about 35 percent; the second was biopsy findings, at about 29 percent (Source Notes S11). The first of these means something worth pausing on. It means that the kidney was acceptable on paper, and that the machinery of offers could not find, in time, a person and a center willing to take it.

## Is there a right rate?

Honest people disagree about this, and I do not know the answer.

What can be said is that the rate in the United States has not always been this high and is not this high everywhere. An analysis published in 2018, using a somewhat different definition from the registry's, found that the discard rate rose from 14.9 percent in 2000 to 19.0 percent in 2015 (Source Notes S12). A comparison of American and French practice over the years 2004 to 2014 found that the United States discarded 17.9 percent of the 156,089 deceased-donor kidneys it recovered, and France discarded 9.1 percent of its 29,984. The kidneys that Americans transplanted came from donors with an average age of about 36; the French, about 51. When the authors applied the French pattern of acceptance to American donors, they estimated that 17,435 of the kidneys discarded in the United States, 62 percent, would have been transplanted, adding an estimated 132,445 years of kidney function over the decade (Source Notes S3).

That last figure is a model, not an observation, and the French system differs from ours in many ways. But the contrast in who gets transplanted should make us curious: a country with older, riskier donors used more of its kidneys.

## The median of sixteen

The last piece of evidence is the one that gave this book its first scene. In the study of about fourteen million offers, candidates who died without a transplant had received a median of sixteen offers over 651 days on the list. In the great majority of declines, about 93 percent, the centers attributed the decision to the quality of the organ or the donor. The authors cautioned that some declines were surely right and that they could not follow the fate of the transplants that followed (Source Notes S1).

I find this study more honest than most of its critics and most of its admirers. It does not say that Denise was wronged. It says that a person can wait for 651 days and be passed over sixteen times, and not once be the cause of it.

## What must be done

Three things follow from the number, and each will return in Part Four.

*Publish it.* The nonuse rate should be reported monthly, by organ procurement organization and by transplant center, in a form a patient can read. A rate that is published once a year in a statistical report is a rate no one is accountable for.

*Set a target.* A national goal, with a number and a date, would turn a trend into an obligation. If the goal is to return to 18.2 percent in five years, say so. If it is something else, say that.

*Count the yes.* The system counts the kidneys it transplants and the graft failures it must explain. It should also count the kidneys it declines, the patients who were next in line, and what happened to them. Chapter 2 shows how that can be done.

The number is not the argument. It is the thing the argument has to explain. For that we will have to leave the registry and follow one offer through one night.
