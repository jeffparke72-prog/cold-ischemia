@@PART Three | The Shelf
# What Was Left Behind
EPIGRAPH: "The future is already here; it is just not evenly distributed."
BY: Attributed to William Gibson (c. 1993)

A shelf is where you put what you intend to use later.

Part Three is about the things this field has invented, tested and put on the shelf, and about the reasons they are still there. It takes four of them, in rough order of how well the evidence supports them.

The first is not a machine at all. It is a person: the living donor, whose yes has been the most reliable source of kidneys for half a century and whose cost the system has, until very recently, left her to bear. The second is a pump, with a randomized trial and a ten-year follow-up behind it, used for fewer than four kidneys in ten. The third is a family of oxygen-carrying molecules, one borrowed from a cow and one from a marine worm, whose history includes five failed companies and whose current evidence is early and partly company-sourced. The fourth is a pair of futures, a gene-edited pig kidney and a cell therapy that could end the need for anti-rejection drugs, each of which would remove an old constraint and each of which would walk into the structure of Part Two.

The last chapter in this part steps back and asks why some good ideas spread in months and others in generations. It also tells the story of an idea that deserved to lose, because a book about slow adoption that never admits a bad idea was adopted too fast is only advertising.

The reader should note a difference between this part and the one before it. In Part Two, I was describing behavior. Here I am describing evidence, and the standards are different. Where a claim rests on a randomized trial I say so. Where it rests on a company's press release, I say that too. If you keep one rule in mind, let it be this: a proven tool that goes unused and an unproven tool that is hyped are two different failures, and the cure for each is the same experiment.
