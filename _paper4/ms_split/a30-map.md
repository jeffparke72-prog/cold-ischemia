# A Map of This Book
EPIGRAPH: "Would you tell me, please, which way I ought to go from here?" "That depends a good deal on where you want to get to," said the Cat.
BY: Lewis Carroll, Alice's Adventures in Wonderland (1865)

This book has one destination and four stages. Knowing the route in advance will make the middle easier to follow, and it will let you skip with confidence if your time is short.

[[FIG:map|Figure 1. The structure of the book: four parts, fifteen chapters, one question answered at each stage.]]

## The four parts

**Part One, The Throwaway,** asks *what is actually happening to the kidneys.* Chapter 1 puts a number on the loss and compares it with the number of people who die or become too sick to be transplanted. Chapter 2 follows a single offer through a single night, step by step, so that you can see where the decision is made and by whom. Chapter 3 explains the clock: how cold, oxygen-starved time damages a kidney, why it has grown longer, and why no one is accountable for it.

**Part Two, The Scoreboard,** asks *why the system behaves this way.* Chapter 4 shows how a measure becomes a target, using the federal programs that grade transplant centers. Chapter 5 tells the story of a single memo in the summer of 2025 and what it did to the national supply of kidneys in forty-eight days. Chapter 6 turns to the organ procurement organizations and the trust on which all donation depends. Chapter 7 follows the money into dialysis, the industry that earns its living while the list does not move.

**Part Three, The Shelf,** asks *what has already been invented, or is within reach, and has been left unused.* Chapter 8 is about the living donor, the most reliable supply we have and the one whose costs we leave to the donor. Chapter 9 is about a pump, and the sixteen years between its proof and its partial adoption. Chapter 10 is about oxygen carriers: a molecule from a cow, a molecule from a marine worm, five bankrupt companies, and a research program that has not yet earned its claims. Chapter 11 looks at the two largest possibilities on the horizon, the gene-edited pig kidney and the cell therapy that could end anti-rejection drugs, and asks what they would find when they arrived. Chapter 12 compares the ideas that spread fast with the ideas that spread slowly, including some that deserved to lose, and gives a method for telling them apart.

**Part Four, The Way Out,** asks *what, exactly, should be done, and by whom.* Chapter 13 lays out fifteen specific moves, each tied to an institution, a mechanism and a number. Chapter 14 turns them into a ninety-day plan for a patient, a clinic or a community group. Chapter 15, the closing chapter, steps back and asks what we owe a gift.

Each part opens with a short essay that says what the part argues, so that you can decide how closely to read it.

## How each chapter is built

Every chapter has the same four parts, in the same order, so that you always know where you are.

*In this chapter* is a short box at the start that states the question and the answer, so that you can decide whether you need the details.

*The story* is a narrative, either a documented event or a clearly labeled composite, that makes the problem concrete.

*The evidence* sets out what is known, with the numbers, and with a plain statement of how strong each source is.

*What must be done* closes the chapter with the steps that follow, named by actor. Chapters 13 and 14 collect them.

## Three ways to read it

If you have one evening, read the prologue, the introduction, and chapters 1, 4, 9 and 13. That is the argument in outline.

If you are a patient or a care partner, read chapters 1, 2 and 14 first. They tell you what to ask your center and how to ask it. If a friend or relative might donate, add chapter 8.

If you work in transplantation, policy or journalism, read chapters 4, 5, 9, 10 and 13. They contain the material that is least often discussed together, and the evidence most open to challenge.

## How to read the numbers

You will meet a great many figures. Two conventions will keep them honest.

Where a number comes from a registry, a trial or a federal document I could confirm, it is cited to a Source Note in Appendix A, graded A. Where it comes from a credible report of such a source, it is graded B. Where it comes from a single advocacy group, a manufacturer or a news release, it is graded C, and I say so in the text. The grade is a promise about how much weight a claim can bear.

Where I have done arithmetic of my own, I label it as my arithmetic and show the inputs, so that you can redo it and tell me if I am wrong. I would rather be corrected in public than flattered in private.
