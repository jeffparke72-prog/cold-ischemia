# The Question Beneath the Numbers
EPIGRAPH: "Not everything that counts can be counted, and not everything that can be counted counts."
BY: Attributed to William Bruce Cameron, Informal Sociology (1963)

Part One is about arithmetic, and I want to say at the outset what arithmetic can and cannot do.

It can tell you that nearly three in ten of the kidneys recovered in the United States in 2024 were never transplanted. It can tell you, by my estimate, that this is roughly 8,800 kidneys, and that in the same year 8,644 people were removed from the waiting list because they died or became too sick to be transplanted. It can tell you that a kidney's risk of failing rises with every hour in the cold. It cannot tell you, by itself, who is to blame, or whether any particular no was wrong.

That is why the part is built the way it is. The first chapter puts a number on the loss, as plainly as the sources permit, and does the arithmetic in the open so that you can check it. The second chapter slows down a single night and follows one offer through the hands that handle it, so that you can see where the decision is made and what the person making it can and cannot see. The third chapter follows the kidney itself, hour by hour, and asks who owns those hours.

I would like you to read this part with two attitudes at once. Be skeptical: not every discarded kidney was good, and the surgeon who declines is usually acting in good faith. And be curious: a figure this large, rising this steadily, in a system staffed by capable and decent people, is not an accident. It is a result. Someone, or something, is producing it. Part Two asks what.
