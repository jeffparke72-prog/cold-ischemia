# Chapter 3: The Clock Has No Owner
EPIGRAPH: "I returned, and saw under the sun, that the race is not to the swift, nor the battle to the strong... but time and chance happeneth to them all."
BY: Ecclesiastes 9:11 (King James Version, 1611)

:::note
**In this chapter.** *Question:* how much does the time a kidney spends in the cold matter, and who is responsible for it? *Answer:* it matters a great deal, in a measurable and steady way, and in a chain of six organizations, no one is responsible for the total. The most expensive variable in the system is the one with no owner.

Follow the kidney, not the offer.

Here is one imagined journey, a composite again, from the instant its donor's blood stops flowing through it to the instant a recipient's does. I have made the timeline plausible and unremarkable, not extreme. Most kidneys have a worse night than this one.

| Hour | What happens | Who owns it |
|---|---|---|
| 0 | Cold fluid is flushed through the kidney; it is bagged and boxed in ice | Recovery team and procurement organization |
| 1 to 5 | Offers are made, declined, remade; the kidney is accepted by a center in another state | National matching system and the centers |
| 5 to 7 | A courier takes the box to an airport; a flight is found | Procurement organization and courier |
| 7 to 11 | The flight; a delay for weather; ground transport | Airline, courier |
| 11 to 14 | Arrival; crossmatch and preparation; the recipient is brought in | Transplant center |
| 14 to 17 | Operating room; the kidney is placed in the patient | Transplant center |
| 17 | Blood flows through the kidney for the first time in 17 hours | — |
| Total | Seventeen hours of cold, starved time | **No one** |

Look at the last row. It is the one that matters, and the one that has no name in the right-hand column.

## What the cold does

A kidney removed from a body is not dead. It is living on what it stored. Cold slows its chemistry the way a refrigerator slows milk, but it does not stop it. Without blood flowing through it, its cells run out of oxygen and then of the molecule that carries their energy. Waste builds up. Cell membranes start to leak. And then the second injury comes, the one that surprises most people: when blood finally flows back in, the sudden return of oxygen to starved cells produces a burst of harmful chemistry that causes damage of its own. Researchers call the whole sequence ischemia-reperfusion injury, and it is the biological mechanism behind most of what follows.

The consequence is measurable in a way that is rare in medicine. A French cohort of 3,839 kidney transplants performed between 2000 and 2011 found that each additional hour of cold ischemia raised the risk of graft failure by about 1.3 percent and the risk of death by about 1.8 percent. Those are small steps. But steps compound. A kidney that spent thirty hours in the cold, compared with six, carried about 40 percent more risk of failure (Source Notes S19).

[[FIG:hazard|Figure 3.1. Relative risk of graft failure and of death rising with hours of cold storage, from a French cohort. Each added hour compounds the last.]]

A 2026 study from Freiburg, small and from a single center, looked at 74 pairs of kidneys from older donors and found that when cold time exceeded twelve hours, compared with under eight, the odds of delayed graft function were more than six times higher (an odds ratio of 6.3, with a wide margin of uncertainty), and the risk of death was roughly tripled (Source Notes S20). I would not rest a policy on a study of 74 pairs. I mention it because it points the same way as the larger studies, and because its subject is the kind of kidney, older and less than perfect, that the system is most likely to discard.

And the effect of delayed graft function is not trivial. When a transplanted kidney does not work at once, the patient needs dialysis in the first week, often stays longer in the hospital, and, in registry studies, is more likely to suffer rejection and a shorter graft life. In 2023 about 26 percent of deceased-donor kidneys in the United States went through it (Source Notes S13).

## Why the hours have grown

You might expect a system to shorten the hours over time. This one has done the opposite, and the reason is instructive.

In 2021, the allocation system for kidneys was changed. Previously organs were offered first within a local area and then more widely. The new system replaced the old boundaries with circles of fixed radius around the donor hospital, to give candidates in more places a fair chance at the best kidneys. It was a response to a legitimate complaint about geography. It also meant that more kidneys traveled farther. Analyses of the change found that regional sharing added roughly 2.9 hours of cold time compared with local sharing, and that the national network's own committees acknowledged that broader sharing increases the time it takes to place an organ, the hours it spends in the cold, and the rate of discard (Source Notes S21). The authors of the 2024 national report say the same in their opening paragraphs (Source Notes S6).

Notice that no one made a mistake. A policy was designed to solve one problem, fairness across geography, and it created another, longer clocks, because the second problem had no owner to raise a hand.

The pattern is also uneven. A study of 206 transplant centers found that the median cold time for kidneys recovered at the same hospital where they were transplanted ranged from about 3.3 hours to about 29 hours across centers, and that kidneys recovered on weekends were more likely to be discarded than transplanted (Source Notes S21). Time is not a fixed fact of nature. It is a variable that some organizations manage better than others.

## Six hands, no owner

It is worth naming the hands.

The donor hospital controls the pace of recovery. The procurement organization controls packaging, transport and offers. The national network's computer controls the order of the list, and the allocation rules it enforces influence how far a kidney must travel. The centers control the speed of their decisions and the readiness of their operating rooms. The airlines and couriers control the journey. And the federal agency that supervises all of them has, in recent years, been rebuilding the machinery of oversight, but has not yet made the total number of hours for any given kidney the responsibility of any single party.

The federal government has noticed. A working group convened by the Health Resources and Services Administration produced a final report in May of 2025 with twenty recommendations on organ transport, and in 2025 the Federal Aviation Administration launched a website devoted to the air transport of organs (Source Notes S22). Industry briefings have also noted that the contract then in force did not permit the agency to require organs to be tracked in transit (Source Notes S22). These are steps in the right direction. They are also, notice, steps on one slice of the clock.

## The accountability test

Here is a test I will apply to every system in this book. *If a number is important, someone's job should depend on it.*

By that test the clock fails. The national registry publishes median cold times, and the researchers who study them agree they matter. But if a kidney is lost to a six-hour delay at an airport, there is no person or organization to whom that loss is charged. If a procurement organization turns a kidney around faster than its peers, it earns nothing for it. If a transplant center delays a decision to gather a team, the cost lands on a kidney and a patient it will never see.

Contrast what happens to a number that someone does own. A program's one-year graft survival is owned by the program's surgeon and director; if it slips, a review begins. It is therefore watched, discussed, and managed. The clock is not. The difference in how the two numbers are treated is the difference between a number that has a name beside it and one that does not.

## What an owner would do

Suppose we gave the clock an owner. What would change?

First, the hours would become visible. A public dashboard of cold time per kidney, by procurement organization, by center and by route, updated monthly, would show who is fast and who is slow, and why. That is not a technological challenge. The data are already collected.

Second, someone would be responsible for the total. This might be a named *organ custodian*: the procurement organization that recovered a kidney would remain accountable, in a formal sense, for its journey until it is transplanted or declined, with the authority to reroute, to arrange a faster flight, to change a plan. Today that authority is split.

Third, tools would follow. A tracked kidney is a managed kidney. A kidney that can be kept healthier during its hours, by machines that perfuse it and by additives that feed it, is a kidney whose clock matters less. Part Three is about those tools.

## What must be done

*Publish the clock.* Cold time per kidney, per procurement organization and per center, monthly and public.

*Name the owner.* Make a single party formally responsible for each kidney's journey, from the moment of recovery to the moment of use or decline.

*Test every rule for its effect on time.* Before the next change to the allocation system, require an estimate of what it will do to the average hours in the cold, and check afterward.

A clock with no owner is a clock that will keep running. In the next part of the book we turn from what is happening to why, and the first reason is a scoreboard.
