# Chapter 14: The Ninety Days
EPIGRAPH: "While we are postponing, life speeds by."
BY: Seneca, Letters to Lucilius, letter 1 (Gummere translation, 1917)

:::note
**In this chapter.** *Question:* what can a patient, a care partner, a clinic or a community group do in the next ninety days, without waiting for Congress? *Answer:* ask twelve specific questions, send three specific letters, keep a one-page log, and report what you learn. None of it requires permission. All of it produces the one thing the system lacks, which is information it cannot ignore.
:::

Denise, in my telling, does not give up.

I have kept her waiting for eleven chapters, and she has had a long time to think. In the weeks after the night of the fourteenth offer she read the same material you have, and she did what a fourth-grade teacher does with a list of facts she wants to remember: she turned it into questions. Her ninety days are below. They are invented, as she is, but each step is something a real person can do, and I have tried to keep every one of them within reach of someone with a telephone, a notebook and a Saturday.

## Days 1 to 7: the twelve questions

Denise's first appointment after reading was with her transplant coordinator, and she did not ask for a kidney. She asked for information. These are the questions, in the order she asked them. You may copy them.

1. *How many kidney offers has my name received in the last twelve months, and how many did the center accept or decline on my behalf?*
2. *What reasons were recorded for the declines?*
3. *Does this program use offer filters that screen out some kidneys before they reach the surgeon? Which filters apply to me?*
4. *Which categories of kidneys have I agreed to consider: older donors, higher-risk donors, donors with hepatitis C, kidneys from donation after circulatory death? Can I change that?*
5. *Does this center use machine perfusion? What share of the kidneys it accepts are pumped, and who provides the pump?*
6. *How does the program's offer acceptance ratio compare with what is expected for patients like me, and how does its one-year graft survival?* (These are in the public program report from the national registry, and a good coordinator will know where to find them.)
7. *If a higher-risk kidney is offered at night, who decides, and will someone call me?*
8. *Would it help me to be listed at a second center? What would that involve?*
9. *How is my status decided if I am made inactive, and how would I know?*
10. *Is this center taking part in any trials of preservation or perfusion?*
11. *What happened, afterward, to the kidneys this center declined last year: how many were transplanted elsewhere, and how did they do?*
12. *Who represents patients on this program's quality committee?*

Notice what these have in common. None of them demands that the center accept a kidney. None of them accuses anyone. Each asks the program to look at what it already knows. A good center will welcome them, because a good center is proud of its practices. A center that finds them hostile has told you something about itself.

Denise wrote the answers in a notebook, including the ones that were "I'll have to check," and the date she asked. Her coordinator, she noticed, wrote some things down too.

## Days 8 to 30: three letters

Letters are underrated. A telephone call can be forgotten; a letter must be answered, and it creates a record.

*The first letter* went to her transplant center's medical director, politely, with the twelve questions attached. It asked for written answers within thirty days and for a meeting.

*The second* went to her member of Congress, and it was one page. It said who she was, that she had been on the list for five years, and that she had read that about 29 percent of recovered kidneys were never transplanted. It asked three things: that the representative request from the federal transplant agency a public date for an expedited-placement pathway (move 5), a quarterly offer statement for every candidate (move 8), and a rule on who pays for kidney perfusion (move 1). It closed with the sentence she had learned matters most: *Please reply with a date.*

*The third* went to the dialysis facility where she spent her Mondays, Wednesdays and Fridays. It asked whether the facility had a policy of referring every new patient for transplant evaluation within ninety days, and what share of its patients had been waitlisted in the last year (move 2). She copied the regional administrator.

If you would rather not write from scratch, here is the form of a model letter. Change the facts to yours.

> Dear [Name]. I am [a patient on the kidney waiting list / a care partner / a living donor] in [town]. I have read that in 2024 about 29 percent of donated kidneys in the United States were never transplanted, up from 18 percent in 2013. I am writing to ask that you request from [agency] a public plan, with dates, to [specific move]. I would be grateful for a written reply by [date, thirty days out]. Sincerely, [Name].

## Days 31 to 60: find the others

Denise did not stop at letters. She called the social worker at her dialysis unit and asked whether anyone else had questions like hers. Two did. They became three. They met for an hour on a Saturday at a library and wrote down the answers they had gotten and the ones they had not.

This is the step most people skip, and it is the one that matters most. One patient's questions are an anecdote. Five patients' answers, compared, are a pattern. If three of five centers in a region say they have never been asked about offer filters, that is a finding. If four of five cannot say what share of their kidneys are pumped, that is a finding. The kidney community has been, in one sense, atomized: everyone waits alone, in a different chair.

A group of five can also do what one cannot. It can ask for a meeting with the center's director and expect to get it. It can attend a hospital board's public session. It can write to the local newspaper. And it can find a researcher: many transplant researchers are eager for patients who will read their papers and ask questions.

## Days 61 to 90: report

At the end of ninety days Denise sat down with her notebook and made a one-page report. It had four parts.

*What I asked, and when.* A dated list.

*What I was told.* Answers, in her own words, with the person's name and role.

*What I could not find out.* The most important part. The gaps are the evidence.

*What I would like next.* One request, with a date.

She sent it to her center's director, to her representative, to the head of her dialysis company's regional office, and to the local reporter who covers health. She sent a copy to the patient organization she belongs to. She kept a copy for herself.

## A log you can keep

Any patient can keep a simple record. One page, with five columns, is enough.

| Date | What I asked | Whom | What I was told | What I'm waiting for |
|---|---|---|---|---|

Keep it for ninety days. Bring it to every appointment. The value is not in any single entry. It is in the habit of expecting an answer.

## For clinics and community groups

The same plan works from the other side of the table.

A **transplant center** can, this quarter, publish its offer acceptance ratio and one-year outcome in plain language on its own website; list the kinds of kidneys it declines most often and why; and offer every candidate the offer ledger of Chapter 2 before anyone requires it. The first center to do so will be a story.

A **dialysis facility** can report its referral and waitlisting rates to its patients, make a transplant coordinator's name and number visible on the wall, and add a monthly question to its patient survey: *How long did it take you to feel like yourself after your last treatment?*

A **procurement organization** can send every donor family a letter within thirty days of a donation saying what became of each organ, and publish its quarterly pause log.

A **community group**, a church, a union, a veterans' post, a parent association, can host a one-hour session with a local transplant surgeon and a patient, and ask the twelve questions aloud.

## What the ninety days are for

They will not change the national system on their own. Denise knows that. She will probably not receive a kidney because of any of this, and I will not pretend that she will.

What the ninety days do is move a piece of the invisible ledger into the visible one. Each question asked, each answer logged, each letter replied to or ignored, is an entry with a name and a date. The system is built to respond to what it can see. For the first time, a few of its customers have started to show it.

Seneca's sentence at the head of the chapter is the reason I wrote this book. We are always postponing something. A patient waiting for a kidney is, in the cruel arithmetic of Chapter 1, waiting on someone else's postponement. It is not asking too much to ask the postponers for a date.
