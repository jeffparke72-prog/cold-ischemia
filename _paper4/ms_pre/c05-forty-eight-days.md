# Chapter 5: Forty-Eight Days
EPIGRAPH: "Early and provident fear is the mother of safety."
BY: Edmund Burke, Speech on the Petition of the Unitarian Society (1792)

:::note
**In this chapter.** *Question:* how fast can the system change behavior, and in which direction? *Answer:* in under seven weeks, on the strength of an email that threatened discipline, and in the direction of fewer transplants. The same system has needed sixteen years to adopt a pump. Fear travels faster than benefit.
:::

On August 6, 2025, an email went out to the people who run the American organ transplant network. It was a memorandum, not a rule. It changed no law, no regulation, no policy approved by the network's board. It said, in the careful language of federal oversight, that the practice of placing organs out of the order in which a computer had ranked the candidates was a concern, and that it could lead to disciplinary action (Source Notes S29).

Forty-eight days later, researchers at New York University looked at what had happened.

## The story of a memo

To understand why a memorandum could matter, you need to know about a practice with an unhelpful name.

When a kidney is offered to the person at the top of the list and declined, and then to the next, and the next, a point may come at which a procurement organization, watching the clock, decides to stop working down the list and call a center it knows will say yes. The practice is called allocation out of sequence. It can be a rescue. A kidney that has been turned down a dozen times, from a donor with a score that makes most programs hesitate, may find its only chance of use in a center that has the surgeons, the machine perfusion and the willingness to take it. The practice grew rapidly. By one count, kidneys placed out of sequence unilaterally by procurement organizations rose seventeen-fold between 2020 and early 2026 (Source Notes S30).

It can also be a problem. The match run exists because it embodies a promise: that the person who has waited longest, or who is sickest, or whose tissue fits best, will be offered an organ before someone further down. Every kidney placed out of order is a kidney offered to someone who was, by the rules, not next. If the practice is applied inconsistently, it can favor some centers and some patients over others, and it can be done in ways no one outside the process can see. The federal agency that oversees the network worried about exactly that, and it was right to.

So the memo said, in effect: follow the list.

Imagine the morning it arrived. A procurement organization's on-call team opens the email over coffee. They know the practice has been tolerated for years. They know that the last time a federal agency used the word *discipline* about a procurement organization, it was followed by a review, a corrective plan and a story in the local press. They do not know whether the memo means what it says. They know that the cost of misreading it falls on them.

## Forty-eight days

A research group at NYU led by Syed Husain compared the period from August 6 to September 23, 2025, with the same weeks of the previous year and with other stretches of 2025. They found that kidney recoveries, kidney transplants and out-of-sequence allocations all fell after the memorandum. If the drop continued, they estimated, the country would perform roughly 1,500 fewer kidney transplants a year than in recent reference periods (Source Notes S29).

A separate analysis, summarized by an advocacy group for donation science, reported that the number of kidneys recovered each day fell from about 85 to about 76, and that discard rates did not rise (Source Notes S29). That detail is worth a pause. If discards did not rise and recoveries fell, then the missing kidneys were not recovered and thrown away. They were never recovered at all. Procurement teams, facing the risk of discipline, appear to have declined to take on the hardest placements.

A third study in the *American Journal of Transplantation*, with a title that deserves to be quoted in full, found that eliminating out-of-sequence allocation for thirty days decreased the use of hard-to-place kidneys, in a paper called "There is nothing equitable about a wasted organ" (Source Notes S29). Three groups, three methods, one direction.

I need to be careful with the numbers, because these are early analyses, some are abstracts or trade summaries, and one is a short window. The estimate of 1,500 is a projection if the drop were to persist. I do not know whether it persisted. What I am confident about is the shape: a federal signal, delivered cheaply, produced a measurable change in a national system within weeks.

## The asymmetry of speeds

Put the forty-eight days beside the other timelines in this book.

A randomized trial showed in 2009 that a pump kept kidneys working longer. By 2023 the pump was in use for about 39 percent of kidneys (Source Notes S31). Using kidneys from donors exposed to hepatitis C, made possible by new antiviral drugs and tested in a ten-patient trial in 2018, moved more slowly than the first reports suggested: in 2024 the nonuse rate for kidneys from donors with antibodies but no detectable virus was still 32.2 percent (Source Notes S32). The largest trial of hemodiafiltration, which found fewer deaths, appeared in 2023, and American patients are only now beginning to receive it (Source Notes S33).

Against these, the memo: one email, no vote, no public comment, no evidence review, forty-eight days.

This is the central asymmetry of Part Two, and it applies far beyond transplantation. A threat of punishment reaches every desk in a system in a day. A promise of benefit has to be believed, funded, trained for, scheduled and billed before it moves anyone. The system's reflexes are fast in the direction of caution, and slow in the direction of change. If you want to understand why a good idea sits on a shelf, start by asking how fast a bad one could be installed.

## The case on the other side

I have been asked, by people whose opinions I respect, not to make this chapter a villain story, and I have tried not to.

The agency has a duty to protect the integrity of the list. Candidates who have waited for years have a right to expect that the order will be honored. Out-of-sequence placement, done in the dark and unevenly, can undermine that trust, and trust is the thing every other part of this system depends on. The federal agency has also, in the last two years, been working to rebuild oversight of a network that had been criticized for decades, including by Congress. Seen from the agency's desk, the memo was a reasonable attempt to restore discipline to a practice that had grown fast and quietly.

And I do not know what happened next. In April 2026 the network's board heard an update from a working group that had drafted a definition of what counts as an offer, criteria for an expedited placement pathway, and a framework for auditing it. The board took no action on the proposals at that meeting, and several projects remained paused pending federal review (Source Notes S34). I have not found a report of how the question was settled by the autumn.

What I can say is that an expedited pathway with a clear definition and a public audit would solve both problems at once: it would keep the list honest and it would keep hard-to-place kidneys moving. The memo got the first without the second. It was a good instinct delivered through a bad instrument.

## What the chapter proves

It proves something narrower and more useful than that regulators are careless. It proves that the system is *responsive*. Within seven weeks, a national network of fifty-five procurement organizations and nearly two hundred kidney transplant programs altered what it did.

That is good news. A system that can change in seven weeks can change. The question is whether we will ever point that speed in a better direction. Later chapters describe the tools, the reforms and the plan. Here we simply note that the reflexes exist.

## What must be done

*Assess before you send.* Before any federal memorandum, rule or policy touching organ placement is issued, the agency should publish, in a page, what it expects the effect to be on the number of kidneys recovered and transplanted, and commit to a thirty-day check against that expectation. A rule that could cost 1,500 transplants a year deserves a forecast.

*Replace the memo with a pathway.* Define an expedited placement route for kidneys that have been declined a set number of times, with clear criteria, real-time logging and a public audit. Fairness and utilization are not opposites. They are two requirements of the same design.

*Publish rapid evaluations.* When a signal changes the system, the data on its effect should be available in weeks and not years. Husain's group did the work in less than two months, with public data. The agency can do the same.

The scoreboard and the memo are two of the forces that shape what happens to a kidney. The third is the organization that decides which kidneys exist at all.
