# Introduction: The Invisible Ledger
EPIGRAPH: "The more any quantitative social indicator is used for social decision-making, the more subject it will be to corruption pressures and the more apt it will be to distort and corrupt the social processes it is intended to monitor."
BY: Donald T. Campbell, "Assessing the Impact of Planned Social Change" (1979)

A kidney is a gift with a deadline.

It comes from a person who has died, usually without warning, and from a family that agreed in the first hour of its grief to let a stranger keep living. It leaves one body and has to reach another within a window the surgeons measure in hours. Every hour it spends in the cold, without blood flowing through it, adds a little damage that the recipient will carry. That damage is real, measurable, and in many cases avoidable. It is also not the only way a kidney is lost. It can be lost in a cooler. It can be lost in a queue. It can be lost in a phone call that takes eleven seconds.

This book is about that loss. It makes a claim that I intend to defend with every source I can find, and I would rather state the claim now than make you hunt for it.

*The American transplant system throws away roughly three in every ten of the kidneys it recovers, not mainly because the kidneys are bad, but because the system rewards the people who say no and punishes the people who say yes; and the tools that could change the arithmetic have been proven, published, and left on the shelf.*

Each half of that sentence deserves a warning label.

The first half does not say that every discarded kidney was good. Many were not. Donors are older and sicker than they used to be, as the country is, and a surgeon who declines a badly scarred kidney is doing her job. The claim is narrower and harder to dismiss. It is that the *rate* of nonuse has climbed from 18.2 percent in 2013 to 29.3 percent in 2024 (Source Notes S2), that a French analysis of American discards found that most of them, by the standards of French practice, would have been transplanted (Source Notes S3), and that the people who decline kidneys are operating inside a structure whose incentives lean one way. I will show you that structure piece by piece.

The second half does not say that every new technology works. Some do not. In 2022 the largest trial ever done in dialysis tested a cheap, appealing idea, cooler dialysis fluid, in more than fifteen thousand patients, and found it did nothing for the heart (Source Notes S4). I will tell you about that trial, because a book that tells you only the stories that flatter its argument is not worth reading. What I am claiming is that for a short list of tools, the evidence has been in hand for years, and that the delay is better explained by who pays, who is blamed, and who owns the problem than by any doubt about the science.

## Two ledgers

The idea that organizes this book is simple enough to say in a minute and to fight about for a year.

Imagine every decision in the transplant system recorded in one of two ledgers. In the first, the visible ledger, go the things that can be counted and blamed. A surgeon accepts a marginal kidney; it fails in eight months; the failure is recorded, attributed to her program, and published. A procurement organization recovers organs from a donor whose neurological status turns out to be uncertain; a federal investigation follows. A dialysis center spends more on a patient than the formula allows; the margin shrinks. All of these are entries in the visible ledger, and each has a name, a date, and an owner who can be held to account.

In the second, the invisible ledger, go the things that cannot. A kidney declined for the fourteenth time goes unused. A candidate who would have lived five more years with it dies on the list in the spring. A pump that would have kept a kidney healthy is left in a warehouse because no one in the chain gets credit for using it. There is no row for these, no name, no owner. The loss is real, but it is distributed so thinly across so many hands, and spread so far in time, that it never lands on anyone's desk.

The Campbell epigraph above says what happens next, and it says it more bluntly than I could. When you measure a social process and attach consequences to the measure, people respond to the measure. The measure here is the program's one-year outcome, counted in the visible ledger. The thing we actually care about, how many people end up with a working kidney, lives in the invisible one.

I will argue that almost everything strange about the American kidney system, including the discards, the delay in adopting proven tools, and the oddly deliberate pace at which good ideas arrive, can be read as a consequence of one asymmetry. *The system is built to notice a yes that goes wrong, and to ignore a no that goes wrong.*

## Four ideas to carry through

I will use four names, and I will use them often.

**The invisible ledger** is the asymmetry just described: the costs of acting are recorded and attributed; the costs of not acting are neither.

**The ratchet** is how the invisible ledger gets stronger over time. Every scandal produces a rule or a metric or a memo meant to prevent the last mistake, and almost none is ever withdrawn. Caution accumulates. A system that adds a guard against every visible failure and never subtracts one will, given enough years, guard itself into paralysis. We will see a ratchet turn in chapter 5, in a span of forty-eight days.

**The orphan innovation** is a tool that works but has no parent: no one whose budget it fits, no one whose metric it improves, no one who is paid for the hours it saves. A machine that benefits the recipient but is paid for out of the procurement organization's budget is an orphan. So is a drug that preserves a kidney but is sold to no one who buys. Orphans do not die. They sit in the corner and wait, sometimes for sixteen years.

**The clock with no owner** is the last and perhaps the most important. The time a kidney spends in the cold is the single variable that every party agrees matters and that no party is accountable for. The donor hospital, the procurement organization, the airline, the courier, the transplant center and the national computer each own a slice of the hours. No one owns the hours.

## The cast, in two minutes

Because the system has more acronyms than a war, here are the seven players who will appear on nearly every page.

**Donor families** consent, or refuse, at the bedside. Nothing begins without them.

**Organ procurement organizations**, called OPOs, are the fifty-five regional nonprofits that identify donors, talk to families, manage the donor's care, recover the organs and offer them to the national list. They are the front door.

**Transplant centers** are the hospital programs that decide whether to accept a given kidney for a given patient on their list. They are the back door, and they hold the last, fastest and least visible decision.

**The Organ Procurement and Transplantation Network**, the OPTN, writes the rules for sorting the list and runs the matching computer. It operates under contract to the **Health Resources and Services Administration**, HRSA, a federal agency, which has spent the past two years rebuilding it.

**The Scientific Registry of Transplant Recipients**, the SRTR, is the statistician: it publishes each program's results, adjusted for how sick its patients were, twice a year. Its numbers become the scoreboard.

**The Centers for Medicare & Medicaid Services**, CMS, pays for most dialysis and most transplants, and decides which programs and which OPOs may keep operating. When CMS speaks, boards listen.

**The dialysis industry**, dominated by two companies, is where almost everyone on the list spends the years while they wait. Its economics matter more than its modest share of the news would suggest.

By most accounts I found, a candidate on the list is not told, as it happens, who has turned down what on her behalf. That is not a conspiracy. It is simply how the process is built, and we will come back to it.

## What this book is, and is not

It is an argument assembled from public records, peer-reviewed trials, registry reports, federal documents and reporting by journalists who did the slow work of checking. It uses sources as current as this month wherever I could find them, and it says so when the best available evidence is older. It grades its sources, openly, in the appendix, because the difference between a randomized trial and a press release is not a technicality.

It is not a history of transplantation, and it is not a patient guide. It does not tell you whether to accept a particular kidney. It does not say that the surgeon who declines is a villain, because I do not think she is. If I have done my job, you will finish the book more sympathetic to her, not less, and angrier at the structure that made her decision so easy.

It is not, finally, a document that pretends to a neutrality its author does not have. I have spent a long time on the losing side of the clock this book describes, and I have watched people I know lose to it. That is why the book is written the way it is: with the numbers first, and the people close behind. I have invented people where I needed to illustrate a pattern, and I have said so each time. Everyone I name was named in a public record.

## What I am asking

Most books about health policy end with a call to awareness. I have little patience for awareness. The kidney community is aware. What it lacks is a short list of specific demands, addressed to named institutions, with numbers attached and dates beside them. The fourth part of this book is that list. It contains fifteen moves, an order in which to make them, and a plan for the first ninety days.

If you read only one chapter, read the last. If you read all of them, I hope you will arrive there angry in the useful way, the way that picks up the phone.

Denise is still waiting. So are about a hundred and forty thousand other people (Source Notes S5). Let us begin with the number.
