# Chapter 2: Anatomy of a No
EPIGRAPH: "It may seem a strange principle to enunciate as the very first requirement in a Hospital that it should do the sick no harm."
BY: Florence Nightingale, Notes on Hospitals (1863)

:::note
**In this chapter.** *Question:* who decides that a kidney will go unused, and how? *Answer:* a single tired professional, in minutes, using a drop-down menu, under pressure from a scoreboard she did not design. The decision is not irrational. It is rational for her, and costly for the person next in line. Nobody records the cost.
:::

I want to slow down a night that, in life, takes less than a minute.

What follows is a composite. I built it from the published descriptions of how offers work, from the research on how centers decide, and from the structure of the national system. Nothing in it is a transcript. Every constraint in it is real.

## 11:42 p.m. — The call

The offer arrives as it always does, not as a call about a person but as a record: a donor number, a blood type, an age, a set of lab values, a score. The coordinator has seen thousands. She does not read the whole screen. She reads the parts that predict the next ten minutes.

The score is the first thing. Since the allocation overhaul of 2014 the system has summarized the quality of a deceased donor's kidney in a single number, the kidney donor profile index, a percentile that runs from 0 to 100. A low number means a kidney expected to last longer than most; a high number, shorter. The index is a statistical tool, built from the donor's age, blood pressure, kidney function, cause of death and other factors. It was revised in 2024. It is also, in practice, a sorting device: the higher it is, the more likely the kidney is to be declined.

The second thing she reads is the clock. The kidney has not yet been recovered, so the question is when it will arrive. Distance, weather, flights, a courier who may or may not be on call. The system has grown larger in recent years, and so have the journeys. Since the 2021 change that shared kidneys across wider circles around each donor hospital, the time kidneys spend in the cold has gone up, and with it the rate of what clinicians call delayed graft function, the need for dialysis in the first week after the operation. In 2023 that rate was about 26 percent (Source Notes S13).

The third thing is the biopsy. At the donor hospital, someone has cut a thin wedge from the kidney, frozen it, and sent it to a pathologist, perhaps a general pathologist who rarely reads kidneys, perhaps at two in the morning. The report says some of the filtering units, the glomeruli, look scarred.

## 11:44 p.m. — The screen

The coordinator now does what no algorithm does for her: she thinks about her patients.

At her center, a candidate who is sixty-three and on dialysis has been on the list for four years. Another, fifty-four, has diabetes. A third is younger and healthier but sits lower on the list. Each is a different bet. A kidney with a few warning signs is a good bet for the older patient, who might die waiting for a better one, and a worse bet for the younger, who has decades to use a long-lasting organ.

She has one more thing to consider, and it is the one no one writes in a textbook. The center's last stretch of results.

## 11:51 p.m. — The weighing

The surgeon, reading the same screen, is carrying a ledger she did not choose.

On one side are the consequences of a no. They are nearly invisible. The candidate does not know she was offered the kidney. The kidney goes to someone else, or does not. If the candidate dies, no record connects her death to this offer. The decline will appear, at most, as a line in a national database, a "reason" chosen from a menu.

On the other side are the consequences of a yes, and they are anything but invisible. If the kidney does not work right away, the patient may stay in the hospital longer, the program's costs rise, and the surgeon will have to explain to a frightened family why the thing they hoped for is not yet working. If the kidney fails within the year, the failure is counted. It will appear in the published results for the program, adjusted for how risky the transplants were, and compared with a prediction. If the program falls too far below the prediction, a review follows. We will come to what a review can do in the next chapter. For now, notice only the asymmetry. This is the invisible ledger at work, in a real room, at midnight.

Nightingale's line at the head of this chapter is one of the most famous in the history of hospital reform, and it is the right place to start. *Do no harm.* A no is the choice that cannot harm the patient on the table. It is not the same as doing no harm. It is only the choice whose harm happens somewhere else.

## 11:53 p.m. — The eleven seconds

She says no. The coordinator selects a reason from a drop-down list and clicks. The record is sent.

How good is the judgment behind a click like that? The research is uncomfortable. A kidney is, on average, offered about seven times before someone accepts it (Source Notes S14). In a 2017 randomized study of sixty-eight physicians shown the same offers, the variation among the decisions of doctors within a single center was roughly ten times the variation among centers. In a 2023 survey, the statistic that measures agreement among experienced reviewers looking at identical cases, called Fleiss's kappa, was 0.13, a value that statisticians describe as slight (Source Notes S14). In plain terms: shown the same kidney, two skilled surgeons would often disagree, and the difference between them would be larger than the difference between hospitals.

Centers differ, too. A study of 193 adult programs, using offers from 2021 to 2023, found that programs accepted a median of 12.5 percent of offers of kidneys that were not considered suboptimal, and a median of 7.2 percent of suboptimal ones; the spread was widest for kidneys with more than thirty-six hours of cold time (Source Notes S15). No one believes that the patients at hospital A are three times more suited to marginal kidneys than the patients at hospital B. The difference is a difference of appetite for risk.

## The biopsy at two in the morning

I said the biopsy would matter, and it does, more than almost anything else on that screen.

Procurement biopsies, the quick frozen sections read at the donor hospital, are a standard tool. They are also, according to a body of studies, poorly reproducible. In one single-center analysis, the classification of a kidney as optimal or suboptimal at procurement matched the later reading of the same transplanted kidney in only about 64 percent of cases, and for the most important feature, scarring of the glomeruli, agreement barely exceeded chance (Source Notes S16). In another, repeat biopsies of the same kidney agreed on glomerulosclerosis at a kappa of 0.12; and the second biopsy, not the first, predicted how the graft later fared. A case-control study of discards found that only one feature, glomerulosclerosis greater than 20 percent, was independently associated with discard (Source Notes S16).

And the national result: kidneys that had been biopsied went unused 40.8 percent of the time in 2024, against 6.4 percent for kidneys that had not (Source Notes S2). Some of that is selection; a surgeon biopsies the kidneys she is already worried about. But some of it, plausibly, is the biopsy itself, an imperfect measurement turned into a verdict.

## What happens after a no

A no does not end the night. It starts another clock.

The kidney has been offered to the next candidate on the list. If that center also says no, to the next. Each decline takes minutes to hours; each hour is an hour of cold time. A study from the allocation system's own analysts noted that waiting for an offer to be declined can lengthen ischemic time (Source Notes S17). By the time a kidney reaches a center that would accept it, it may be too old to be worth accepting. That is the logic that produces the single largest reason for discard in one national analysis: *no recipient located*. The recipient exists. By then, the kidney no longer does.

## The first fix, and what it proves

There is one reform already underway that tells us something. Offer filters are a tool that lets a program tell the system, in advance, which kinds of kidneys it will never accept for its candidates, so that those offers are not sent. In a pilot, participating centers avoided about two-thirds of the offers they would otherwise have received, and some increased their transplant volumes while receiving up to 75 percent fewer offers (Source Notes S18). The board of the national network has now approved a version in which filters are switched on by default, based on a program's own acceptance history (Source Notes S18).

I mention it for two reasons. It is evidence that the process contains a great deal of wasted motion that can be removed without harming anyone's results. And it shows the pattern this book is about: a tool that improves the system as a whole was available, and it took years to get from a pilot to a default.

## What must be done

*Give every candidate an offer ledger.* The most important thing the night reveals is that the person whose life depends on the decision does not see it. A quarterly statement, sent to each candidate, saying how many kidneys were offered on her behalf, how many were declined, and the reasons, would let her ask the questions the system now makes unnecessary. It would not force a center to accept a kidney. It would force a center to look at what it is declining. Chapter 13 develops this idea.

*Make the reasons real.* A drop-down menu is not a reason. Reasons should be specific, audited and compared with what happened to the kidney afterward. If a kidney was declined for an organ-quality concern, and a different center transplanted it into a patient who did well, the first decision should be visible to the first center.

*Take the second look.* A kidney that has been declined more than a set number of times should be routed, automatically, to the centers with the best record of using hard-to-place kidneys. That is not a change to the clinical judgment. It is a change to the plumbing.

Denise's surgeon was not careless. She was human, rational and unobserved. The next chapter tells you what makes a kidney's hours so costly, and why no one owns them.
