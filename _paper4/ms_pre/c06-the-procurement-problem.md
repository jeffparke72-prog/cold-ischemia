# Chapter 6: The Procurement Problem
EPIGRAPH: "But he that filches from me my good name / Robs me of that which not enriches him, / And makes me poor indeed."
BY: William Shakespeare, Othello, act 3, scene 3 (c. 1603)

:::note
**In this chapter.** *Question:* what happens to the supply of kidneys when the organizations that recover them lose the public's trust? *Answer:* the supply shrinks fast, and not only for the organizations at fault. Procurement is the front door of the whole system, and the evidence of the past two years is that the front door has been left unwatched.
:::

In the last week of October 2024, the Associated Press reported a number that I think is the most honest measure of public trust ever published about organ donation. In the days after news coverage of a man in Kentucky who had been mistaken for dead, an average of 170 people a day removed themselves from the national donor registry. In the same week of 2023 the number had been about one-tenth of that (Source Notes S35).

One hundred and seventy a day. They did not write letters. They did not march. They opened a website, clicked a box and took their names off a list. The head of the national association of procurement organizations told the reporter that trust of that kind "takes years to regain."

## The story of a pause

The case behind the headline is documented, and its details are worse than the headline.

It involved an organization in Kentucky, one of the fifty-five regional nonprofits that make up the front door of the American transplant system. A procurement organization's job is to find potential donors in the hospitals of its region, to talk to families, to manage the donor's care between death and recovery, to recover the organs and to offer them to the national list. It is a difficult, delicate and underpaid job, done in the worst hours of other people's lives.

In March of 2025 the Health Resources and Services Administration, the federal agency that oversees the network, released a report on the Kentucky organization. It reviewed about 350 cases, 351 in the agency's own summary, in which a family had authorized donation after circulatory death but the recovery had not been completed. In 73 of them, the agency concluded that the process should have been reconsidered sooner, because the patient's neurological status was high or improving. In 20.8 percent, the patient had features not conducive to this kind of donation at the initial or a later assessment. In at least 28 cases, 8.0 percent, no cardiac time of death had been recorded, which the report said suggested the patient might have survived to leave the hospital. The department's headline figure was that concerns were found in 103 of the 351 cases (Source Notes S36).

The organization disputed the characterizations. By then it had merged with a neighbor in Ohio, in October 2024, into a new entity, and the federal agency had directed the network to reopen a case that its own peer-review committee had closed a few days earlier. The corrective actions announced included a root-cause analysis, a rule that any staff member could halt a donation, and a requirement that all pauses for safety concerns be reported to the network (Source Notes S36). The agency said it had received reports of similar patterns at other organizations.

I want to be fair to everyone in this story, as the sources allow. The organization says its practices were lawful and its staff were trying to honor families' wishes. Many procurement professionals I have read say that the media coverage has distorted a practice that, done properly, saves lives. I do not know how many of the concerns in the 103 cases would withstand full clinical review, and the agency's own phrase is *concerns*, not *violations*. But nothing in the documents suggests that the registry's 170 departures a day were an overreaction to a mere rumor.

## The first decertification

In September of 2025 the federal government did something it had not done before. It decertified an organ procurement organization in the middle of a certification cycle. The target was the organization serving the region around the University of Miami. The department cited unsafe practices, poor training, chronic underperformance, understaffing and paperwork errors, and the organization said it would not appeal (Source Notes S37). Separate congressional inquiries into other allegations are, so far as I can find, allegations only.

Then there is the larger machinery. Under a rule finalized in 2020, procurement organizations are sorted every cycle into three tiers according to two measures, how many donors they produce relative to the number of eligible deaths in their region, and how many organs from those donors are transplanted. The top quarter, Tier 1, is recertified automatically. The middle compete for their regions. The bottom, Tier 3, is to be decertified. The first cycle under this system ended in 2026, using 2024 data. In January 2026 CMS proposed a further rule to decertify any organization that is in Tier 3 in all its service areas (Source Notes S38). I could find no published roster of the organizations expected to be decertified, and the process, as of this writing, is not finished.

One more study belongs here, because it asks a question that is easy to ignore. Researchers examining the organizations projected to fall into Tier 3 found that they serve populations that are disproportionately non-white. The metric that decides which organizations survive may fall hardest where the need is greatest (Source Notes S39).

## What the front door has to do with the back door

Why does a book about discarded kidneys have a chapter about procurement?

Because trust is the raw material. Every kidney this book discusses began as a family's yes. If the number of people who say yes falls, there are fewer kidneys to discard and fewer to transplant. The 170-a-day figure is not a news story. It is a supply forecast.

And because procurement is where the scoreboard of Chapter 4 meets the clock of Chapter 3. An organization judged on organs transplanted, and not on organs recovered and then wasted, has every reason to maximize the first; it has fewer reasons to ask whether the kidneys it recovers can be placed. The organization that recovers a kidney bears the cost of recovery, the risk of reputational damage if something goes wrong, and, as we saw in the last chapter, the cost of a federal memorandum if it tries to place a hard kidney outside the sequence. It has no scoreboard line for a kidney it saved.

I do not claim that the metrics caused what happened in Kentucky. I found no evidence of that. I claim that a system that measures only the end of the chain, and that has shown, repeatedly, that it responds to what it measures, should expect trouble at the beginning.

## What the other front doors look like

It is possible to build a system in which families are treated differently. In Spain, which has led the world in donation for three decades, the national organization places a physician coordinator in each hospital whose job is to identify potential donors and speak with families. In 2025 Spain reported 51.9 deceased donors per million people, close to the 52.6 it had reached the year before, and roughly 6,300 transplants (Source Notes S40). The comparison with the United States must be handled with care, because definitions, laws and cultures differ. But the lesson that most Spanish-model scholars draw is not about technology. It is about people placed where the families are, with time and training, and with the national organization on the hook for the result.

The research on families suggests why this matters. In an Australian interview study of 49 potential donor families, no family that had consented regretted the decision, while some that had declined did. Dissatisfaction with how hospital staff behaved was more likely to trouble a family than the donation itself (Source Notes S41). What hurts, in the long run, is not the yes. It is the manner in which the yes is received, and the silence afterward.

## What must be done

*Install an independent safety officer in every procurement organization,* with authority to halt a recovery and a direct line to the federal agency that cannot be overridden by the organization's own leadership. The agency has begun to require something like this. It should be permanent and universal.

*Publish the pause log.* Every halted donation and the reason for it should appear in a quarterly public report. Pauses are not scandals. They are evidence that the system is working. A system that does not pause cannot be trusted.

*Close the loop with families.* Within thirty days of a donation, every donor family should receive a written account of what became of each organ. The family that said yes should not learn about the outcome from a newspaper. This is also the best defense against the next 170-a-day week.

*Measure trust.* The registry opt-out rate should be tracked weekly by region and reported alongside the performance tiers. It is the only metric that tells the system, quickly, whether it has gone too far.

*Count what is wasted.* Procurement organizations should be graded on kidneys recovered and then not used, not only on kidneys transplanted, so that the organization that recovers a kidney has a stake in whether it finds a body.

A procurement organization's good name is, in Shakespeare's phrase, the one possession that enriches no thief. It also underwrites everything else in this book. The next chapter follows the money away from the hospital, and into the building where most of the people on the list actually live.
