# Chapter 9: The Pump
EPIGRAPH: "Hope deferred maketh the heart sick: but when the desire cometh, it is a tree of life."
BY: Proverbs 13:12 (King James Version, 1611)

:::note
**In this chapter.** *Question:* what do we do with a tool whose benefit was proven sixteen years ago? *Answer:* we use it for fewer than four kidneys in ten, mostly where an organization that did not choose to bear the cost agrees to provide it. The pump is the clearest case in this book of an orphan innovation.
:::

Here is the best experiment in the history of kidney preservation, and it begins with a coin.

In the years before 2009, a team of researchers in the Netherlands, led by Cyril Moers and working with transplant centers in six European countries, did something simple and beautiful. They waited for a deceased donor whose two kidneys would be transplanted into two different patients. Then they flipped a coin. One kidney went into a machine that kept cold preservation fluid flowing through it, called a hypothermic machine perfusion pump. The other was packed in ice, the way kidneys had been packed for fifty years. Same donor, same body, same night, same hospital system, same cold. Only the method of preservation differed. They did this 336 times, which means that 672 patients received kidneys in the trial (Source Notes S52).

The design was clever for a reason that is easy to miss. The great problem in comparing how kidneys are preserved is that kidneys differ. A kidney from a healthy twenty-five-year-old will do well in almost any condition; a kidney from a sixty-eight-year-old with high blood pressure will not. Compare pumped kidneys with iced kidneys across many donors, and the differences between the donors drown the difference between the methods. But two kidneys from the same donor are as close to identical as nature allows.

## What the coin showed

The result, published in the *New England Journal of Medicine* in January 2009, was this: delayed graft function, the need for dialysis in the first week after transplantation, occurred in 70 of the pumped kidneys and in 89 of the iced ones, with an adjusted odds ratio of 0.57 (Source Notes S52). That is about 20.8 percent against 26.5 percent. The pumped kidneys also had better graft survival at one year.

The trial's first-year result made headlines in transplant circles and then did what such results do: it entered the literature and slowly altered practice.

Sixteen years later, in November 2025, the same group published a ten-year follow-up. It appeared as a short correspondence in the same journal and reported that the benefit had persisted: more kidneys that had been pumped were still working a decade later than those that had been iced. A press release from the investigators' university put the figures at 79 percent against 73 percent. The device's manufacturer has summarized the result as a 27 percent lower risk of graft failure over the ten years (Source Notes S53). Because the manufacturer is not neutral, I give that figure with a flag, and I note that the investigators' own figure shows a six-point gap.

There is an old principle in medicine that an intervention proven in a randomized trial should become the standard of care within a few years. The pump has a randomized trial, a decade of follow-up, the consistent testimony of surgeons, and a recent survey in which 92 percent of respondents endorsed it for helping them choose which kidneys to use and 90 percent endorsed it for reducing delayed graft function (Source Notes S54).

## What the country did with it

Then, in 2023, an analysis of the national registry found that the pump was used for about 39 percent of deceased-donor kidneys (Source Notes S31).

Thirty-nine percent. A second analysis, a 2026 survey of eighty-eight transplant centers that use the pump, put the pattern in sharper relief. Centers that use it represent 35 percent of the country's active kidney programs and 63 percent of the volume. In other words, the busiest centers have it, and most of the small and mid-sized ones do not. And the pump is mostly not run by the centers. Most centers neither own pumps nor employ a dedicated perfusionist; about three-quarters of those surveyed said the pumping is done exclusively by the procurement organization (Source Notes S54).

The most-cited barrier in the survey, named by 58 percent of respondents, was the procurement organization *refusing* to pump. Not cost in the abstract. Not skepticism about the evidence. Refusal.

There is a second finding in that survey that I find more revealing than the first. Asked what they would do if a kidney they wanted pumped could not be pumped, 60 percent of the surgeons said they would *sometimes* decline the kidney, and another 20 percent said they would. In other words, for four respondents in five, the absence of a pump is at least sometimes a reason to say no.

Connect that to the first chapters. The kidneys that are most likely to be discarded are the ones with the greatest uncertainty: older donors, biopsy findings, long cold times. Those are the kidneys for which a pump makes the most difference. Pumping both reduces the risk of delayed function and gives the surgeon a way to assess the kidney, since the resistance and flow measured during perfusion are informative. In the survey, 92 percent endorsed it for that purpose. A tool exists that would turn a marginal no into a confident yes, and its availability depends on whether an organization that is not the beneficiary chooses to provide it.

## The orphan

This is what I mean by an orphan innovation.

Consider who gains from a pump. The recipient gains: fewer days on dialysis, a longer-lasting kidney. The transplant center gains: fewer complications, a more reliable result, better numbers on the scoreboard of Chapter 4. The Medicare program gains: a patient who leaves dialysis sooner. A kidney that would have been discarded and instead is transplanted gains the most.

Consider who pays. In the American system, as the survey indicates, it is frequently the procurement organization that bears the labor, the logistics and, I infer, much of the cost of running the machine. I could not find a published federal rule that says in plain terms who is responsible for paying for perfusion, or any published figure for what it costs the organization per kidney. The manufacturer claims a saving of nearly $10,000 per case from reduced delayed graft function, which is marketing and I treat it as such. What I can say is that the benefit of the pump accrues largely to someone other than the party that is asked to provide it.

That is the classic structure of a tool that does not spread. The person who would have to move it has no reason to; the person who would benefit has no way to. Its evidence is excellent. Its sponsor is no one.

## A country that decided

In September 2022, Belgium made a different choice. Its national health insurer began reimbursing hypothermic machine perfusion for kidneys from older donors and from donors after circulatory death, on a national basis, through a national preservation service. A study published in 2025 analyzed the first year. Delayed graft function occurred in 14.4 percent of the pumped kidneys, which is well below the American rate. The national number of kidneys from donors after circulatory death that were transplanted rose from 90 to 175 a year, a change the authors called statistically significant; the number from older donors rose by less, and not significantly. The authors estimated annual savings of €3.59 million from reduced dialysis (Source Notes S55).

I need to hold this up to scrutiny. It was a retrospective analysis, not a trial; the numbers of kidneys are small; the savings estimate depends on assumptions I have not been able to read in detail; the vendor that supplies the service says it won the national tender. Belgium also differs from the United States in a thousand ways. But the contrast in structure is instructive. In Belgium, someone decided that the pump was a service, assigned an owner, set a price, and agreed to pay. In the United States, the pump is a favor.

## Not every idea at the shelf deserves to win

I have a duty to say what has *not* worked, because a book that praises one innovation and ignores the failures of its neighbors is advocacy of the worst kind.

Warming the kidney while it is pumped, rather than cooling it, seemed a logical next step. A randomized trial of warm perfusion for kidneys from donors after circulatory death, published in 2023, found that delayed graft function occurred in 60.7 percent of the warm-perfused kidneys and 58.5 percent of the iced ones, no difference (Source Notes S56). A 2026 trial adding two hours of warm perfusion to the cold pump found no improvement in immediate function. A trial of adding oxygen to the cold pump for kidneys from older donors found no benefit, though it was underpowered. A 2025 review concluded that the effect of oxygen during perfusion has been mixed and the clinical benefit of warm perfusion unproven (Source Notes S56).

That is why the cold pump stands alone as proven and why the other tools in this chapter's neighborhood, including the oxygen carriers in the next, deserve to be examined with suspicion. The point of this book is not that every new tool is wonderful. It is that for one of them the evidence is in, and the system has not acted on it.

## What must be done

*Set a standard.* The national network's kidney committee should define, within twelve months, which kidneys should be machine-perfused, by donor type, age and expected cold time, and require that perfusion be made available when a center requests it for a kidney in that group. The 2009 trial is old enough to have earned a standard.

*Assign an owner and a price.* The federal payer should state explicitly who pays for perfusion, and pay it. The Belgian model, a national service with a national reimbursement, is one template. A pass-through of the full cost to the organization that does the pumping is another. What cannot continue is an arrangement in which the cost falls on one party and the benefit on another.

*Fix the thresholds.* The survey found that acceptable resistance and flow cut-offs vary widely, and that inaccurate parameters were a recognized cause of kidneys being underused. The network should publish evidence-based thresholds, update them annually, and audit how they are used.

*End the refusal.* No procurement organization should be permitted to decline to pump a kidney a transplant center has requested for a recognized indication, except for a documented clinical or logistical reason, reported publicly.

*Publish pump use.* The percentage of each organization's and each center's kidneys that were perfused, and the outcomes, should appear in the dashboard of Chapter 3.

The pump has waited sixteen years. In a ten-year follow-up the people who waited with it lived with the difference. Next, we look at a tool that has not yet earned the same evidence, and has been waiting just as long.
