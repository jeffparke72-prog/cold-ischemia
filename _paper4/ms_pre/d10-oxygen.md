# Chapter 10: The Worm, the Cow, and the Blood That Wasn't
EPIGRAPH: "But ask now the beasts, and they shall teach thee; and the fowls of the air, and they shall tell thee."
BY: Job 12:7 (King James Version, 1611)

:::note
**In this chapter.** *Question:* can a molecule that carries oxygen outside the body help a kidney survive the cold, and why has the idea taken so long? *Answer:* it might, and no one yet knows. The first generation of such molecules failed spectacularly as a blood substitute, and the companies that made them mostly failed too. A second use, inside the organ's preservation fluid, avoids the old safety problem, and the evidence for it is early, small, and partly company-sourced. This chapter says exactly how far it goes.
:::

By his own account, Franck Zal was looking out of a laboratory window at a beach.

It was at the marine station in Roscoff, on the coast of Brittany, where the tide goes out a long way. He was a biologist who studied the blood of sea creatures, and on the sand below him were thousands of lugworms, the fat, pink, burrowing animals that fishermen dig for bait. At low tide, when the water drained away and the sand began to dry, the worms lay in their burrows with almost no oxygen. They did not die. He asked himself how (Source Notes S57).

The answer, as he came to tell it, was in their blood. The lugworm carries its hemoglobin not inside cells, as humans do, but loose in the plasma, in giant molecules, much larger than ours. A human hemoglobin molecule can hold four molecules of oxygen. By one description, a lugworm's can hold 156 (Source Notes S57). He made the case that such a molecule might help keep human tissue alive when blood could not reach it, and in 2007, with the backing of his research institutions, he founded a company, Hemarina, to find out.

I begin with Zal because the story is a good one and because the rest of this chapter will be less romantic. A good story is not evidence. It is the reason someone looked.

## The cow, and the graveyard

To see why a molecule from a worm is a novelty and a molecule from a cow is a cautionary tale, you have to know the graveyard.

The idea of a blood substitute is old and appealing. Blood is scarce, perishable and has to be typed and matched. A solution of hemoglobin, the oxygen-carrying protein stripped out of red cells, would in theory need no matching and could be kept on a shelf. Researchers were infusing such solutions into animals more than a century ago. The fear of contaminated blood in the 1980s turned the idea from a curiosity into an industry (Source Notes S58).

The industry's history is the history of a handful of companies and one conclusion. Baxter, a major pharmaceutical company, developed a human hemoglobin chemically cross-linked into a stable form, and in a trial in trauma patients the product produced, by one secondary review's account, a 72 percent increase in deaths compared with saline. Hemosol, in Canada, developed a hemoglobin linked with a sugar-derived reagent; its late-stage trials were stopped in 2003 after cardiac events and the company went insolvent within a few years. Northfield Laboratories, in Illinois, developed a polymerized human hemoglobin and ran the field's boldest trial, in ambulances, in patients who could not consent; the regulatory outcome was unfavorable and the company filed for bankruptcy in 2009. Biopure, in Massachusetts, made two bovine products: one approved for dogs in the late 1990s, one for people in South Africa in 2001. In 2003 the American regulator halted a proposed trauma study and rejected an application for use in orthopedic surgery. The Securities and Exchange Commission later found misleading statements about the regulator's stance, and in 2005 brought charges against the company's chief executive and others. On July 16, 2009, Biopure filed for bankruptcy protection (Source Notes S58).

The verdict on the whole class came in 2008, in a pooled analysis in the *Journal of the American Medical Association* by Charles Natanson and colleagues. It combined sixteen trials of five products in 3,711 patients. The treated patients died more often than the controls, 164 against 123, a relative risk of 1.30. They had more heart attacks, 59 against 16, a relative risk of 2.71 (Source Notes S59). The paper did not distinguish among molecules, and it did not need to. Regulators, editors and investors drew the obvious conclusion. The field went quiet.

I tell the story with some detail because the lesson of the graveyard is often misread. The lesson is not that oxygen carriers do not work. It is that injecting free hemoglobin into a person in shock appears to be dangerous, probably because the molecule scavenges the body's own nitric oxide, tightening blood vessels and stressing the heart. That is a lesson about a *route*, not about a molecule.

## The dog

One product survived, in a way that tells you something. Oxyglobin, made from bovine hemoglobin, was approved in the United States for anemic dogs in the late 1990s, before any comparable product cleared a regulator for humans anywhere in the wealthy world. It sat in a bag on a shelf, needed no cross-match, and could be hung by a tired clinician at two in the morning. It also, in time, disappeared from clinics when its manufacturer's finances failed, leaving veterinarians to search the country for the last units.

The dog got there first because the question asked of the dog was easier. A dog in crisis with no donor blood has an alternative of nothing, and a regulator can approve on the sign of improvement. A human regulator must be convinced that the product beats red cells. The difference between the two questions is the difference between the two histories (Source Notes S58). I mention it because it points to the thing the molecule can do, and the thing the market could not support.

## A different route

Now consider a different place to put the molecule.

A kidney on a pump, or in a cooler, is outside the body. Its cells are starving, as we saw in Chapter 3, and the damage is done by lack of oxygen and by what happens when oxygen returns. Suppose the preservation fluid in which the kidney sits, or through which it is pumped, contains an oxygen carrier. The molecule is not injected into a patient in shock. It is in the kidney's bath. The exposure of the patient who receives the organ is a fraction of what was tested in the trauma trials. The risk is not zero. It is a different, much smaller regulatory and safety question.

That is the design behind the product from Hemarina, called HEMO2life, built on a purified form of the worm's hemoglobin, called M101. In a study called OXYOP, which ran at French transplant centers from 2016, sixty kidneys preserved with the additive were compared with the matching kidney from the same donor preserved in the usual way (Source Notes S60). The investigators' follow-up reported that patient survival at four years was 98.3 percent in the group that received the additive, against 86 percent among controls, a difference with a p-value of 0.016. The manufacturer reports less delayed graft function in some subgroups. The product received a European marketing mark, and has been used in other settings, including a 2021 double upper-limb transplant in India (Source Notes S60).

I cannot say how seriously to take these numbers, and I want to tell you why.

First, the study was small. Second, the paired design, which is a strength of the Dutch pump trial, was in this case compromised: by the way the study was arranged, the cold time was longer in the control kidneys than in the treated ones, and cold time alone changes outcomes (Source Notes S60). A benefit that might be partly due to a shorter cold time is not a clean benefit. Third, the survival figure is a four-year follow-up of recipients, an outcome that depends on many things other than the kidney. Fourth, the sources are mostly the company and its distributors, and a published paper from the group discloses that its founder has a financial interest. Fifth, the sources disagree about how many patients took part, sixty or one hundred and twenty.

A randomized trial, OxyOp2, was designed to answer the question properly, and its protocol was published in 2023. I could find no results (Source Notes S60).

None of that makes the work worthless. It makes it a first signal. It is exactly the kind of finding that deserves to be followed by a larger, better trial, and exactly the kind that often is not.

## The program

A second effort is closer to home. A company called BHOC Therapeutics is developing cell-free bovine hemoglobin carriers, and its transplant program, BHOC Transplant, describes a research plan: whether such a carrier could keep a donated organ supplied with oxygen through preservation, machine perfusion and the return to body temperature, beginning with one organ, a kidney, and one protocol, with every measurement defined in advance. The Foundation I founded has written about it, and I describe it here as its sponsors describe it: as a research concept. They state that it is not an approved treatment, that BHOC has not been shown to prevent rejection or change transplant outcomes, and that results and time gained are still to be proven (Source Notes S61).

I like that language, and I would like it applied to every other claim in this chapter. A claim of benefit without a trial is a hypothesis. The most valuable thing a patient community can do with a hypothesis is insist on the experiment.

## What the autopsies teach

The five failed companies teach something beyond their molecules, and a few of the lessons apply to anyone watching this field today.

The *single-product company* has nothing to absorb a bad trial; every one of these companies was built on one molecule. *Judgment by class* means a failure of one product taints all of them: a bovine polymer is not chemically a cross-linked human protein, but the 2008 analysis did not distinguish. *Governance failures* are scientific variables: a sponsor caught shading its statements pays a tax on every later claim. *Improving comparators* mean the blood supply became safer, shrinking the original advantage. And *survival through niches* suggests that a modest market, veterinary use, a few foreign approvals, compassionate access, can keep alive a manufacturing process, a regulatory file and a clinical record that no start-up could build from nothing (Source Notes S58).

There is also a simple checklist, which an outsider can apply from public documents. Watch the ratio of announcements to publications. Watch how the company speaks about the regulator. Watch whether the first trial is an all-or-nothing wager. Watch whether the company can describe its manufacturing plainly. Watch the cash runway against the date the trial will read out.

Apply it to any claim you read about organ preservation, including mine.

## What must be done

*Run the trial.* A properly designed, independently funded, registered randomized trial of an oxygen carrier in kidney preservation is overdue. It should use paired kidneys with equalized cold time, preregistered primary outcomes, delayed graft function and one-year kidney function with graft survival at five years, blinded adjudication, and publication of results whatever they are. By my arithmetic, a trial of unpaired recipients, hoping to cut delayed graft function from 26 percent to 18 percent, would need several hundred patients per arm; a paired design needs fewer. That is a modest sum by the standards of drug development, and well within the reach of a public funder.

*Pay for evidence, not for hope.* A federal payer can offer coverage on condition that patients are enrolled in a registry or a trial, so that use produces knowledge.

*Protect the molecule from the company.* The oxygen-carrier field has repeatedly lost working science because a sponsor failed. Public funders, including the defense and health agencies with an interest in blood substitutes, should hold the dossiers, manufacturing know-how and clinical records of products that show promise, so that the next bankruptcy does not erase them.

*Seat the patients.* Every trial should include patients and donor families in design and oversight. A trial that patients trust enrolls faster and drops out less.

The worm and the cow are, for now, a possibility. The next chapter turns to two larger possibilities, a gene-edited pig kidney and a cell therapy that could end the need for anti-rejection drugs.
