# Chapter 13: Fifteen Moves
EPIGRAPH: "Power concedes nothing without a demand. It never did and it never will."
BY: Frederick Douglass, speech on West India Emancipation (1857)

:::note
**In this chapter.** *Question:* what, exactly, should be done, by whom, and how will we know it worked? *Answer:* fifteen moves in five groups, each with an owner, a mechanism, the evidence behind it, and a test you can check in a year. Nothing here overrules a surgeon's judgment about a patient. Everything here changes the structure that surrounds the judgment.
:::

A demand has three parts: a request, an addressee and a date. Most advocacy fails because it has one of the three. This chapter tries to supply all of them.

Before the list, a word about what I am not proposing. I am not proposing that surgeons be forced to accept kidneys they believe will harm a patient. I am not proposing to eliminate the one-year outcome measure or to abandon oversight of procurement. I am not proposing any technology as a cure. Every move below is a change to payment, information, accountability or evidence, the four levers that, in the earlier chapters, appear to govern behavior.

## Group one: pay for what works (moves 1 to 3)

**1. Pay for the pump and the donor.** In two places in this book the benefit of a proven thing lands on one party and its cost on another. The pump's cost falls on a procurement organization that does not gain; the living donor's cost falls on a person whose gift saves the payer about $55,000 a year (Chapter 8). The federal payer should state in a published rule who pays for kidney perfusion and pay it, either through a pass-through to procurement organizations or a payment to centers, and the living-donor reimbursement created in 2026 should pay promptly, in advance for travel and lodging, with a ceiling set against real costs (Source Notes S68). Belgium's model, a national service with a national reimbursement, produced a rise in transplanted donation-after-circulatory-death kidneys from 90 to 175 a year (Source Notes S55). *Evidence:* Chapter 9. *Risk:* cost; mitigated by the savings from reduced delayed graft function, which one Belgian estimate put at €3.59 million a year. *Test:* a rule published, and the share of kidneys perfused rising from 39 percent toward a published target.

**2. Make early referral the default.** A trial across 134 Georgia dialysis facilities and more than 9,000 patients found that a structured referral intervention improved referral for evaluation and narrowed the racial gap (Source Notes S51). That should be the standard of care: every patient informed, and referred for evaluation within ninety days of starting dialysis unless there is a documented reason, with the percentage published by facility. *Risk:* referral is not transplant, and a flood of referrals could lengthen evaluation queues. *Test:* the share of new dialysis patients referred within ninety days, by facility.

**3. Fund the trials.** For the oxygen carriers of Chapter 10, hemodiafiltration, home dialysis, session length and other plausible tools, public money should buy the experiment that no sponsor will. Coverage with evidence development, in which a payer covers a new tool on condition that patients are enrolled in a registry or a trial, can turn use into knowledge. *Evidence:* MyTEMP shows that a very large, pragmatic trial in hemodialysis is feasible at the level of the facility using health records (Source Notes S4). *Test:* three registered trials launched with public funding.

## Group two: make the clock and the list visible (moves 4 to 8)

**4. Publish the clock.** Cold time per kidney, per procurement organization, per center and per route, updated monthly, with a named organ custodian responsible from recovery to use. The 2021 allocation change added about 2.9 hours of cold time to regional sharing (Source Notes S21). A public goal of recovering those hours would turn a complaint into an engineering target. *Test:* a public dashboard and a median cold time that moves.

**5. Replace the memo with a pathway.** Define an expedited placement route for kidneys that have been declined a set number of times, with criteria, real-time logging and a public audit, so that fairness to waiting candidates and use of hard-to-place kidneys are served together. The network's working group has already drafted a definition of an offer and criteria for an expedited pathway (Source Notes S34). It should be finished, approved and tested. And any federal communication likely to change allocation behavior should carry a forecast of its effect on transplants and a thirty-day review. *Evidence:* Chapter 5. *Test:* a pathway in place and a first public audit.

**6. Standardize and guarantee perfusion.** Evidence-based thresholds for pump resistance and flow, published and updated annually; a rule that no procurement organization may refuse a transplant center's request to pump a kidney in a recognized indication without a documented reason, reported publicly. In the survey of centers, refusal was the most-cited barrier at 58 percent (Source Notes S54). *Test:* thresholds published; refusals counted.

**7. Take the second look.** After a kidney has been declined a set number of times, automatically route it to the programs with the best records of using hard-to-place kidneys. The offer-filter pilot showed that centers could avoid two-thirds of the offers they would otherwise receive and, in some cases, increase their transplant volumes (Source Notes S18). Routing is the other side of the same coin: sending fewer offers to centers that never accept them, and more to centers that do. *Test:* the median number of offers before acceptance, which has been seven (Source Notes S14), falling.

**8. Give every candidate an offer ledger.** A quarterly statement to each person on the list showing how many kidneys were offered on her behalf, how many were declined, the reasons, and what became of them. It would not oblige any center to accept an offer. It would oblige the system to let the person whose life depends on the decisions see them. The research on 14 million offers found that candidates who died had received a median of sixteen (Source Notes S1). Most of those candidates never knew. *Risk:* confusion and anxiety; mitigated by a plain-language explanation and a coordinator available to discuss it. *Test:* first statements delivered, and the share of candidates who say they understood them.

## Group three: fix the scoreboard (moves 9 to 11)

**9. Count the shadow.** The offer acceptance ratio, which now drives federal review and a portion of payment, excludes offers for kidneys that were eventually discarded (Source Notes S27). It should include them. The registry should also publish, for each program, the number of kidneys it declined that were transplanted elsewhere with good results. *Test:* a revised ratio.

**10. Risk-adjust for time and perfusion.** The formula that predicts a program's expected results should account for cold time, delayed graft function and whether a kidney was machine-perfused, so that a program that uses a hard kidney well is credited for the risk. A published safe-harbor rule would let programs accept a defined class of difficult kidneys without being penalized for the expected share that fail. *Test:* the adjusted model published and its effect on program rankings reported.

**11. Put an expiry date on every rule.** Every metric and memorandum should lapse in three years unless re-justified with evidence of its effect on the number of transplants. The 2019 removal of outcome requirements for transplant program re-approval was the first reversal of the ratchet in this book (Source Notes S26). It should not have taken twelve years, and it should not be the last. *Test:* sunset clauses written into every new rule.

## Group four: protect the front door and show the work (moves 12 and 13)

**12. Protect the front door.** An independent safety officer in every procurement organization, with the authority to halt a recovery and a direct line to the federal agency; a quarterly public pause log; a written account to every donor family within thirty days. The registry opt-out rate should be tracked weekly and published beside the performance tiers (Source Notes S35; Source Notes S36). *Test:* officers in place and a first public pause log.

**13. Show the declines.** Each transplant center should publish, annually, its decline reasons and, for kidneys it declined that were later transplanted elsewhere, the outcomes. It is not an accusation. Some declines will turn out to have been right, and the publication will show it. Today a center cannot know whether its caution was wise. *Test:* a first public report from at least one hundred centers.

## Group five: the industry and the voice (moves 14 and 15)

**14. Open the books on dialysis.** A public database of medical-director compensation, physician ownership of vascular access centers and dialysis facilities, and the financial relationships between charities and the firms that fund them; facility-level referral and waitlisting rates displayed beside the star rating; and an independent review by competition authorities of a market in which two firms hold more than three-quarters of the business (Source Notes S46). *Test:* the database and the review.

**15. Seat the patients.** Every board, advisory committee and trial steering group that makes decisions about allocation, preservation or dialysis should include patients, living donors and donor families with a vote. Not a listening session. A vote. The reason is practical: a body that includes the people who pay for its mistakes makes fewer of them. *Test:* seats filled, and attendance published.

## The order

You cannot do fifteen things at once. In my judgment the sequence is this.

*First, the cheap and fast.* Moves 4, 5, 8, 9, 11, 12 and 13 cost little and can begin by administrative action. They produce information, which is the raw material for everything else.

*Second, the structural.* Moves 1, 2, 6, 7 and 10 require a rule, a payment or a model, and a year or two. They convert the information into incentives.

*Third, the investments.* Moves 3, 14 and 15 are slower, and they change who is in the room.

## The targets

A plan without a number is a wish. Here are three.

Return the nonuse rate to 18.2 percent within five years. By my arithmetic in Chapter 1, that is about 3,300 more transplants a year, and at the 2021 cost difference, roughly $185 million a year in avoided dialysis cost, as a scenario and not a forecast (Source Notes S2; Source Notes S9).

Cut the median cold time of a kidney by three hours, undoing the added hours of the broader sharing.

Raise the share of kidneys perfused from 39 percent to a figure the network sets in consultation with surgeons and organizations, and publish it, within twelve months.

I have chosen these because they are measurable, because they can be checked by anyone with the registry's tables, and because they are wrong in only one way: if we hit them and nothing else improves, I will have been mistaken, and I will say so.

## What must be done

*Choose one.* It is not possible for any reader to push fifteen levers. It is possible for each of us to choose the one that is closest to where we stand, and to ask the person who controls it, in writing, for a date. The next chapter shows how.
