# Chapter 15: The Patient Who Cannot Afford to Be Saved
EPIGRAPH: "The rich ruleth over the poor, and the borrower is servant to the lender."
BY: Proverbs 22:7 (King James Version, 1611)

## The last gate is a pharmacy counter

A transplant is not a single operation. It is the first day of a lifelong prescription. The surgery replaces a failed organ, and the immunosuppressive medication that follows persuades the body not to reject it. If the medication stops, the organ is lost, and the surgery, the donor's gift, and the years of waiting are all forfeit. A system that devotes enormous effort to delivering the kidney and little to ensuring that the recipient can keep taking the pills has built a bridge that ends at the water's edge.

## The record: the thirty-six-month cliff and its repair

For decades, Medicare coverage for patients whose only qualifying condition was kidney failure ended 36 months after a transplant, unless the patient was disabled or over 65. Since December 2022, a benefit known as Medicare Part B-ID has covered FDA-approved immunosuppressive drugs indefinitely, whatever the patient's age or disability status. In 2023 the benefit carried a premium of $97.10 per month (Appendix A, item H1). Before the change, advocacy and clinical sources documented patients who rationed or skipped doses because of cost. A 2019 brief from the Department of Health and Human Services observed that for patients who lose Medicare at 36 months and have no other coverage, the cost of continuing immunosuppression may be prohibitive (Appendix A, item H1). Testimony to Congress cited a report in the *New England Journal of Medicine* that nearly 70% of kidney transplant programs had experienced a death or a lost transplant because a patient could not pay for antirejection drugs, although that figure reached me through secondary testimony and I could not confirm the original (Appendix A, item H1).

The repair appears to have been economically sensible as well as humane. A cost-effectiveness analysis modeled lifetime extension of immunosuppressant coverage and found that, from Medicare's perspective, it was cost-saving and produced better outcomes than the policy it replaced, and a federal analysis estimated savings of up to $300 million over ten years (Appendix A, item H1). Here is a rare policy in which doing right and saving money pointed the same direction, and it took years to enact.

I could find no peer-reviewed study that measures how the new benefit has changed the burden of out-of-pocket cost or financial distress among recipients after 36 months. That is a measurement gap, and it is consequential, because a policy that cannot be evaluated cannot be improved.

## The record: Medicaid, the new uncertainty

The next threat is not a cliff that has been built but one that has been forecast. The 2025 federal budget reconciliation law, known informally as the One Big Beautiful Bill Act, made changes to Medicaid whose effect on kidney patients has been analyzed mostly by advocacy organizations. The Congressional Budget Office, as cited by the National Kidney Foundation, estimated that more than 11 million people would lose Medicaid coverage and that more than $700 billion in federal funding would be removed over the budget window (National Kidney Foundation, 2025). The Foundation reports that nearly half of dialysis patients rely on Medicaid and that provisions on work requirements, provider taxes, and eligibility checks could reduce their access (National Kidney Foundation, 2025). A nephrology society newsletter notes that a transplant can cost more than $400,000, with $10,000 to $14,000 each year thereafter for immunosuppressive drugs (American Society of Nephrology, 2025).

Several provisions have dates attached. Work requirements of 80 hours per month are scheduled to begin in January 2027, with exceptions for certain groups, and required eligibility redeterminations every six months are scheduled to begin by December 2026, according to a fact brief published in August 2026 (Gigafact, 2026). One analysis of an earlier Senate draft cited a $35 per-treatment copayment for some dialysis patients above the poverty line, and I treat that figure as unverified, since it described a version of the bill that may have changed before enactment. The largest reductions and many implementation dates fall after 2026.

Let me state what I do and do not know. I know that the sources agree that Medicaid is a major payer for patients with kidney failure and that the law changes Medicaid's rules. I do not know how many dialysis or transplant patients will lose coverage, how many transplant recipients will lose access to immunosuppressant coverage, or whether exceptions will protect them. The sources I reviewed are advocacy statements from mid-2025 and one fact brief from August 2026, and the enacted text should be consulted directly. This is a forecast and not a finding, and an honest book treats it so.

## The reading: poverty compounds at every gate

When this chapter is laid beside Chapters 5 and 11, a pattern emerges that I think deserves to be called the compounding of poverty. At the first gate, patients in disadvantaged neighborhoods face lower odds of being listed and transplanted (Residential neighborhood disadvantage, n.d.†). At the donor gate, the financial cost of giving falls most heavily on the people with least slack (Tushla et al., 2015†). At the final gate, the cost of staying transplanted depends on insurance that is itself under strain. Each of these is a modest effect. Their product is a pathway on which the poorest patient is least likely to arrive and, if they arrive, least likely to remain.

I find this the most morally uncomfortable finding of the whole audit, and I want to describe it without partisanship. A person of the political right can state it as a failure of efficiency: a society that spends heavily to deliver a graft and then risks its loss for the sake of a premium has misallocated its money. A person of the political left can state it as a failure of justice: access to a life-saving treatment ought not to depend on a postal code. Both statements are correct, and neither requires the other's premises.

## What would help, in the order of cost

The cheapest reform is measurement. A national report, published annually, of the share of recipients who experience a gap in immunosuppressant coverage or who report skipping doses for cost would cost almost nothing and would turn a rumor into a number. The second is navigation. A free, plain-language service that helps a family map its coverage before the transplant and again at the thirty-sixth month could prevent losses that cost far more than the service. The third is policy design. Any change to Medicaid eligibility should include an explicit exception for transplant recipients on immunosuppressants, because the cost of the exception is trivial beside the cost of a lost graft. I make this argument knowing that I may be wrong about the details of enacted law. I am not wrong about the principle.

# Chapter 16: A Conservative Case for Reform
EPIGRAPH: "A state without the means of some change is without the means of its conservation."
BY: Edmund Burke, Reflections on the Revolution in France (1790)

## What kind of conservative argument is this?

The word conservative has been appropriated by so many causes that it requires a definition before it is useful. I use it in the older sense, the one Edmund Burke would have recognized. A conservative, in that tradition, wants to preserve valuable institutions, and understands that preservation sometimes requires reform. The transplant system is a valuable institution. It was assembled by a gifted generation of surgeons, ethicists, and legislators, and it has saved an enormous number of lives. Precisely because it is valuable, it should be defended against its own decay, and decay is what the figures in this book describe.

The argument that follows rests on four conservative commitments. Institutions should be accountable to those they serve. Information is the cheapest regulator, and the best. Incentives should be aligned before behavior is lamented. And public programs should be judged by their outcomes and not by their intentions. None of these commitments belongs to a party, but together they point toward a program of reform that is modest in cost and demanding in transparency.

## The platform

What follows is a twelve-point platform. Each plank is tied to a chapter that supplies its evidence, and each is the author's argument and not a finding of any agency.

**1. Publish the Pareto of nonuse.** Classify every unused kidney by its recorded reason and by whether the nonuse was unavoidable, and publish the ranked chart annually (Chapter 4).

**2. Audit the decision.** Measure the reproducibility of kidney-acceptance decisions, publish the result, and provide structured decision aids and a second-look protocol for kidneys declined repeatedly (Chapter 7).

**3. Publish referral and waitlisting by facility.** Release adjusted, facility-level rates of patient information, referral, and waitlisting in a form that patients can search (Chapter 5).

**4. Make the cold chain visible.** Publish the median time from recovery to transplant by procurement organization and by center, with the distribution and not only the median (Chapter 6).

**5. Score in constellations.** Report transplant volume, graft survival, and offer-acceptance rate together, adjusted for case mix, so that no single number can be gamed unobserved (Chapter 8).

**6. Make the franchise contestable in fact.** Carry out the tiers as written, publish the roster, and adjust the metrics for community characteristics so that the system does not punish those who serve the hardest populations or excuse those who underperform (Chapter 8).

**7. Preserve the stop-the-line right.** Maintain the right of any staff member to halt a donation, publish the count of halts, and commission independent audits of death determination (Chapter 10).

**8. Measure trust monthly.** Publish registry registrations and removals every month, together with family-consent rates by organization (Chapters 10 and 14).

**9. Remove every out-of-pocket cost for living donors.** Index the reimbursement ceiling to actual costs, simplify eligibility, and publish the number of donors who applied and were not reimbursed (Chapter 11).

**10. Pilot, do not presume.** Authorize a time-limited, pre-registered pilot of a credit for non-directed donors with independent evaluation of its effect on donations, donor well-being, and public trust (Chapter 11).

**11. Embed the trials.** Build randomization into the registry, require independent replication of manufacturer-announced results, and pay for outcomes (Chapter 12).

**12. Name an owner.** Assign responsibility for the whole patient journey to a single accountable office, and give every dialysis patient a one-page map on the day they begin treatment (Chapter 2).

The cost of most of these planks is the cost of analysis and publication. They require data that are largely already collected. That is the reason I call the program conservative. It does not ask the nation to spend its way out of the problem. It asks the nation to look at its own ledger.

## Sequencing: where to start

Twelve planks cannot be implemented on the same morning, and an honest platform says which come first. I would begin with the four that cost least and teach most: the Pareto of nonuse, the facility-level referral figures, the cold-chain intervals, and the monthly trust series. All four are acts of publication. They require analysts and a decision to disclose, and they require no new rule, no new payment, and no change in anyone's clinical practice. They also have a useful property from the standpoint of reform politics. They are hard to oppose in principle, since the argument against publishing a number that a federal registry already holds must rest on something other than the public interest, and they generate the evidence needed to decide how much more should be done.

The second wave consists of the planks that require institutions to change how they decide: the reproducibility audit and decision aids, the constellation scoring, and the stop-the-line and audit arrangements. These need the cooperation of centers and procurement organizations, and they are likelier to receive it once the first wave has shown that disclosure is survivable. The third wave contains the planks that need Congress or a significant outlay: the reimbursement ceiling for living donors, the credit pilot, and the registry-embedded trials. Allocating the work among the actors is straightforward in principle. The oversight agency and the network board own most of the first two waves, the payment agency owns the franchise and metric reforms, Congress owns the third, and patients and care partners own the pressure that keeps all three moving.

## The strongest objections, and the replies

Every serious reform should be tested against its best critics. I offer four, and my answer to each.

*The equity objection.* Advocates of broad organ sharing argue that expanding the geography of allocation was necessary to reduce unfair disparities, and that criticizing the resulting increase in cold ischemia time risks reversing a hard-won gain. This is a fair objection, and I do not propose to reverse the policy. I propose to measure its costs and to attack them directly, with faster logistics, better preservation, and decision support, so that equity and speed can be pursued together. The annual data report itself acknowledges the trade-off (SRTR & OPTN, 2026a).

*The clinical-judgment objection.* Surgeons will argue that variation in acceptance reflects legitimate judgment about individual patients and that standardization threatens the art of medicine. There is truth here, and a proper aid preserves judgment and does not replace it. But the measured agreement among clinicians looking at the same offer, with a kappa of 0.13 in one survey, is so low that it is hard to believe all of it reflects judgment (Appendix A, item E2). Some of it is noise, and noise is not an art.

*The fairness-to-organizations objection.* Procurement organizations argue that the tier metrics penalize those who serve disadvantaged communities, and the minority-impact analysis cited in Chapter 8 gives the argument weight. My reply is that the remedy for an imperfect metric is a better metric and not no metric. The metric should be adjusted, and the adjustment should be debated in public. But the franchise should remain contestable.

*The ethics objection.* Ethicists will argue that any payment for donation risks coercion. I share the concern, which is why my proposal is a pilot with pre-registered evaluation and not a program, and why I insist that reimbursement of costs, which no one disputes, should be complete before any further step is considered.

## What would change my mind

A document of advocacy should say what evidence would lead it to retreat. If the publication of classification data showed that nearly all nonuse was unavoidable, the first plank would shrink in importance. If a rigorous study showed that acceptance decisions are highly reproducible after accounting for patient differences, the second would. If a well-run pilot of donor credits increased donations at the cost of exploiting the poor, the tenth would be dead, and I would say so. If independent trials showed that oxygen carriers, whatever their theoretical appeal, do not improve outcomes, the Foundation that publishes this book would have to say that too. Conviction without the possibility of refutation is only temperament.

# PART FIVE: CONTROL

The last letter of the method is the one most often forgotten. Improvement that is not controlled decays, and a nation that has waited this long for better numbers should not lose them again through inattention. Part Five describes how to keep what is gained.

# Chapter 17: A Control Plan for a Nation
EPIGRAPH: "Order is Heaven's first law."
BY: Alexander Pope, An Essay on Man, Epistle IV (1734)

## Why improvement decays

The final phase of Lean Six Sigma is control, and it is the phase most often skipped. Teams that celebrate an improvement and then disband find, within a year or two, that the process has drifted back toward its old behavior. The reason is not mysterious. A process left unwatched reverts to the mean of the habits that created it. Control is the set of mechanisms, such as dashboards, alarms, owners, and review cycles, that prevent the reversion.

The most important of those instruments was invented by Walter Shewhart at Bell Laboratories in the 1920s. The control chart plots a metric over time and draws limits around the average, so that a viewer can distinguish ordinary variation from a signal that something has changed (Shewhart, 1931). It converts anxiety into rules. A point beyond the limits, or a run of points on one side of the average, means investigate. Anything else means leave the process alone. The nation's transplant system publishes many numbers annually. It does not, so far as I could determine, publish them as control charts, and a number published once a year cannot reveal a drift that begins in March.

## A dashboard for the journey

The scorecard below collects the measures proposed in this book. The baselines are drawn from the sources cited in earlier chapters. The targets are the author's proposals and are anchored, wherever possible, to values the system has actually achieved.

| Measure | Baseline | Proposed aim |
|---|---|---|
| Recovered kidneys not transplanted | 29.3% (2024) | Back to 26.6% within 12 months; 18.2% as a long-run aim |
| Deaths on list plus removals for being too sick | 3,743 plus 4,901 (2024, annual report) | Publish together, with 60-day follow-up of delisted patients |
| Patients not informed of transplant at dialysis start | 15% (2007 to 2019) | Under 2% |
| Agreement among clinicians on identical offers | Fleiss kappa 0.13 (one survey) | At least 0.60 |
| Median cold ischemia, same-hospital transplants | 3.3 to 29 hours by center | Publish the distribution, then narrow the spread |
| Registry removals | Spike of 170 per day, October 2024 | Monthly public series with control limits |
| Living donors reaching the reimbursement cap | 21% | Under 5% |
| Recipients with a gap in immunosuppressant coverage | Not measured | Measure annually |

Sources for baselines: SRTR & OPTN (2026a, 2026b); Villani et al. (2024†); Associated Press (2024); Appendix A items E2 and E5 and G3. Targets are author's proposals.

I have deliberately included two measures for which no baseline exists. A dashboard that lists only what is already known would reinforce the assumption that the known is what matters.

## Ownership, cadence, and the discipline of the boring

Control plans fail when they lack three things: a named owner, a regular cadence, and a response rule. The owner is the single accountable office proposed in Chapter 16. The cadence should be monthly for fast-moving measures such as registry removals and quarterly for slower ones. The response rule says what happens when a signal appears: who convenes, within what time, and what must be published about the investigation. These arrangements are tedious, and their tedium is the point. The discipline of the boring is what separates a reform from a press release.

## A ninety-day and twelve-month plan

A control plan without a calendar is a wish. The sequence below is the author's proposal. It assumes that the single accountable office of Chapter 16 exists, or that an existing agency has agreed to act as its interim host, and it relies almost entirely on data that are already collected.

*In the first ninety days*, five things can be done without new legislation. The host office can publish the design of the scorecard, with the definition of every measure, so that critics can attack the definitions before anyone's performance is judged. The registry can release the classified breakdown of reasons for kidney nonuse in 2024 from existing records, which is the Pareto chart of Chapter 4. The federal forms that dialysis facilities already file at the start of treatment, which supplied the analysis of 133,414 patients in Chapter 5, can be summarized by facility and published. Registry registrations and removals can begin to appear monthly. And two service areas can be invited to run the seam project of Chapter 13, with a procurement organization, three centers, a patient representative, and an analyst around one table.

*By twelve months*, the work should reach the stage of control. The first control charts for nonuse and for registry removals should be public. The reproducibility study of acceptance decisions should be complete, with its agreement statistic reported alongside the 0.13 of the earlier survey. The annual report to Congress that the living-donor law now requires should include the count of applicants who were not reimbursed. Any national awareness campaign should be launched with a pre-registered evaluation. And a protocol for registry-embedded randomization of preservation methods should be circulated for comment.

A short risk register belongs in any plan of this kind. *Metric gaming* is the first risk, and the defense is the constellation scoring of Chapter 8. *Data lag* is the second, since annual reports describe a year that ended long ago, and the defense is monthly provisional series labeled as such. *Resistance* is the third, and it will come from some centers and some organizations that fear exposure. The defense is to protect participants from penalty during pilots, to publish the early results as learning and not as ranking, and to be unembarrassed about asking patients and families to read the numbers. A reform that depends on everyone's enthusiasm will not survive. A reform that depends on a published number will.

## What a patient or care partner can do tomorrow

Not every reader works in a hospital or an agency, and a book that offers a platform only to professionals has failed most of its audience. The following questions can be asked now, by anyone, without waiting for policy to change. I offer them as practical suggestions and not as medical advice.

Have I been told clearly that transplantation is an option for me, and who can refer me today? If I am already referred, what is the next step, and when is it due? Does the center that is evaluating me publish its waiting times and its rate of accepting organ offers, and may I compare it with another? Because the national report counts candidates once for every center where they are listed, it is evident that listing at more than one center is possible, and a patient may reasonably ask a transplant team whether it is appropriate in their case (SRTR & OPTN, 2026b). What will the medications cost me in the first month, in the first year, and after the thirty-sixth month, and who can help me map my coverage? If I am considering donating, what costs will I bear, and what reimbursement is available to me, in writing, before the operation?

A family that asks these questions is exercising the one form of quality control that has always been available to patients. It is the right to be a difficult customer.

## A closing note on method

I said at the outset that the method of this book is Lean Six Sigma, and I should say plainly what I have and have not done with it. I have defined a customer, mapped a process, measured defects, analyzed causes, proposed improvements, and sketched controls. I have not run a controlled improvement project, and no one should mistake a book for one. The method has served here as a way of organizing arguments and as a standard of honesty about what is known. Its most valuable habit is the one I commend to every reader in every role: ask what the number is, ask who measured it, ask what would change it, and ask who is responsible for changing it.

# Epilogue: The Readiness Is All
EPIGRAPH: "The readiness is all."
BY: William Shakespeare, Hamlet, Act V, Scene 2

I began with a hum, and I will end with a silence. Every transplant story eventually arrives at a moment when the machinery stops being abstract. A telephone rings or it does not. A cooler is carried or it is not. A signature is written or withheld. In that moment, every table in this book collapses into a single human act, and the act is performed by a person who was trained, rushed, fatigued, and hopeful, working inside a system that someone designed.

I do not believe that the people who work inside this system are the problem. I believe they are, for the most part, its rescue. I believe the problem is the design they inherit, and that design is a human artifact, which is to say that it can be amended by human hands. That belief is not optimism. It is the opposite of fatalism, and the opposite of fatalism is the only available foundation for work of this kind.

I have lived in these corridors long enough to know what hope costs and what it buys. I know the arithmetic on the back of the envelope. What I have tried to do in these pages is to carry that arithmetic out of the corridor and set it on the table where policymakers, clinicians, and neighbors can see it. The numbers are imperfect and the sources have gaps, and I have said so wherever they occurred. But the direction is unmistakable, and the direction is toward a system that can be measured, owned, and improved.

Patients and care partners, you are the customer, and the customer is entitled to ask. Clinicians, you are the craftspeople, and craft improves when it is observed. Regulators, you hold the pen, and the pen is mightier when its ink is public. And to anyone who has read this far without a stake in any of it, I offer the simplest of invitations. Ask who pays, who measures, and who is responsible. Ask again next year. The cooler is on the move, and the readiness, as the old play has it, is all.
