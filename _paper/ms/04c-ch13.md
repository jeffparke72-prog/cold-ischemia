# Chapter 13: What the Method Has Already Done
EPIGRAPH: "Experience keeps a dear school, but fools will learn in no other."
BY: Benjamin Franklin, Poor Richard's Almanack (1743)

## A fair question for a book that borrows a method

By this point a skeptical reader is entitled to ask whether the factory vocabulary I have borrowed has ever accomplished anything in this particular field. It is a reasonable challenge. Lean and Six Sigma were developed in manufacturing, in companies that made identical objects by the million, and the leap from an assembly line to an operating suite is not self-evidently safe. The honest response is to look at what has been published. I found a modest body of work, mostly from single institutions, and I report it here with its limits in plain view, since a chapter that exaggerated the method's record would violate the principles it recommends.

## The record: six projects

*Shortening the living-donor evaluation.* A 2023 study in *BMC Health Services Research* applied the Six Sigma DMAIC framework, which stands for define, measure, analyze, improve, and control, to the evaluation of potential living kidney donors. The authors began from the observation that about 25% of potential living-donor transplants are missed because of inadequate timing in the assessment of donors. Their project aimed to shorten the donor evaluation, and they assessed the period afterward for reductions in total lead time, in the patient's exposure to dialysis before transplant and its associated costs, and for an increase in preemptive transplants, those done before dialysis becomes necessary (BMC Health Services Research, 2023†). The summaries I could retrieve did not give the final numbers, so I cannot report the size of the improvement, and I regard that as a gap to be closed by reading the paper.

*Reducing liver transplant hospital stays.* A 2013 study in *Progress in Transplantation* mapped the liver transplant process from listing to discharge using DMAIC. Median length of stay fell from 11 days before the intervention to 8 days afterward, while the rate of readmission did not change. The authors credited clear expectations, improved teamwork, and a multidisciplinary clinical pathway (Progress in Transplantation, 2013†). The unchanged readmission rate matters, because it suggests that the shorter stay was not purchased by discharging patients too soon.

*Speeding the road to the kidney list.* Researchers in Hannover, Germany, reported at the 2016 American Transplant Congress that they had deployed Lean Six Sigma for the first time to evaluate and improve a transplant administration process, focusing on the interval between a patient's first contact with the center and final listing on the European waiting list, with document cycle time as the primary metric (American Transplant Congress, 2016†).

*Reducing waste and errors in donor records.* A tissue bank presented a yellow-belt project, the entry-level tier of Six Sigma certification, aimed at reducing waste and errors in the process of obtaining a deceased donor's medical records after recovery (American Association of Tissue Banks, 2023†). The abstract described the project's focus and did not report outcomes.

*Formalizing quality at a transplant center.* A transplant center at a large academic medical center used DMAIC to build a formal quality assessment and performance improvement program, with a standard set of metrics and outcome reports (Appendix A, item J2). That is an organizational project and not a single process fix.

*Lean thinking in organ donation coordination.* A 2024 multiple-case study of three state transplant systems in Brazil examined lean principles in donation coordination, noting that lean had been applied in many areas of health services but not extensively in transplantation, and proposed a lean organ donation coordination model with initiatives to increase organ supply (International Journal of Services and Operations Management, 2024†). This study is descriptive and not a DMAIC project with before-and-after measurement.

## The reading: what the six projects teach, and what they do not

Three observations emerge. The first is that the projects are local. Each one improved a process inside the walls of a single organization, such as a donor evaluation clinic, a liver service, or a records office. None of the work I found attempted to improve a seam between organizations, for example the handoff between a dialysis facility and a transplant center or between a procurement organization and an accepting hospital. Yet those seams are where, by the argument of this book, the largest losses occur.

The second is that the wins are modest and measurable, which is what the method promises. A three-day reduction in length of stay with no change in readmissions is not a revolution. It is a few hundred patient-days returned to the hospital in a year, and it is real. A system that accumulated a hundred such gains would be a different system.

The third is a caution about the evidence itself. Projects that fail rarely get published, and projects that succeed are written up by the people who ran them. The published record therefore overstates the method's average performance. I cannot say by how much. An honest advocate of Lean Six Sigma in transplantation should say so and should call for a registry of projects, including failures, so that the field can learn from the denominator and not only the numerator.

## A charter for the seam

Having argued that the seams have been neglected, I will offer a template for addressing one. The following is a sketch of a cross-organizational improvement project. It is an illustration of the method and not a protocol, and it would need to be adapted by the organizations involved.

:::note Sample project: from first offer to final acceptance
**Problem statement.** A recovered kidney is offered to a sequence of candidates, and each decline consumes time. A study of offers found that kidneys were offered a median of seven times before acceptance (Appendix A, item E2).
**Defect.** An offer that is declined for a reason that, on review, was not clinical.
**Critical-to-quality measures.** Time from first offer to acceptance. Number of offers per transplanted kidney. Cold ischemia time at transplant. Agreement among reviewers on a sample of declined offers.
**Team.** A procurement organization, three transplant centers in its service area, a patient representative, and a registry analyst.
**Data.** Time-stamped offer logs, which the registry already collects.
**Measure phase (weeks 1 to 4).** Establish the baseline distribution of offer-to-acceptance times and the decline reasons, and run a reproducibility study on a sample of declined offers.
**Analyze phase (weeks 5 to 8).** Rank the decline reasons and identify the top three. Examine variation by time of day and day of the week.
**Improve phase (weeks 9 to 14).** Pilot a structured decision aid and a second-look review for kidneys declined three times. Share a standard information packet.
**Control phase (weeks 15 and after).** Chart the offer-to-acceptance time weekly with control limits. Name an owner. Publish the results.
:::

I would add one remark about this template. Nothing in it requires new legislation, new technology, or new money. It requires four organizations to agree to look at the same numbers together. That, I submit, is the entire difficulty, and it is a difficulty of will and not of resources.

## The reading: why the seams stay untouched

If seam projects are so promising, why are they absent from the literature? I can offer three explanations, each the author's analysis. Within an organization, a project sponsor has the authority to change the process. Across organizations, nobody does, and the project requires the voluntary cooperation of parties whose incentives diverge. Second, the benefits of a seam project accrue to the system and to patients, not to any individual organization's financial statement, so no one's budget funds it. Third, a seam project exposes the performance of each participant to the others, which can feel threatening in a field where outcomes are publicly scored.

These are precisely the barriers that a single accountable office, of the kind proposed in Chapter 16, would be positioned to remove. An office with a mandate for the whole journey could convene the parties, fund the work from a national budget, and shield participants from adverse scoring during the pilot. Without it, the seams will go on being crossed by patients and unmeasured by everyone else.

## What this means for the reader

I do not want to oversell the method. Lean Six Sigma is not a cure and it is not a creed. It is a discipline of looking carefully, counting honestly, changing one thing at a time, and checking whether the change held. Applied inside organizations it has produced small, genuine improvements. Applied across the seams it remains, as far as I can tell, untested. That is both the weakness of the record and the size of the opportunity.
