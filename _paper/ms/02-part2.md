# PART TWO: MEASURE
EPIGRAPH: "The cost of a thing is the amount of what I will call life which is required to be exchanged for it, immediately or in the long run."
BY: Henry David Thoreau, Walden (1854)

Measurement is where a quality project either earns its authority or forfeits it. A claim that cannot be expressed as a count, a rate, or a time is an impression, and impressions, however sincere, do not change budgets. Part Two takes three measurements that, in my judgment, define the performance of the kidney system: how many recovered kidneys go unused, how many patients never reach the list, and how many hours an organ spends in transit.

# Chapter 4: The Number That Should Embarrass Us

## The record: nonuse in 2024

Registry analysts avoid the word "discard," preferring "nonuse," and the preference is instructive, since it concedes that the fate of a recovered kidney is often a decision and not a destiny. The latest annual data report states that 29.3% of recovered kidneys were not transplanted in 2024, compared with 18.2% in 2013 (SRTR & OPTN, 2026b). The intervening reports trace the climb. Nonuse stood at 26.6% in 2022 and 27.9% in 2023 in the previous edition (SRTR & OPTN, 2025). Because registry definitions shift slightly between editions, for example in how a pair of kidneys recovered together is counted, the intermediate points should be read as approximate. The direction is not in doubt, and neither is the pace: nearly eleven percentage points in eleven years.

Three details in the 2024 report deserve to be isolated and examined, because each of them says something different about why kidneys go unused.

The first is the biopsy. Kidneys from donors who underwent a procurement biopsy went unused 40.8% of the time, against 6.4% for kidneys that were not biopsied (SRTR & OPTN, 2026b). The obvious explanation is selection: surgeons are likely to biopsy kidneys they already suspect are marginal. That explanation is surely part of the truth. It would be an error, however, to stop there, because a biopsy is also a subjective act. It samples a small fraction of the organ, and it is read by a pathologist who may be summoned at three in the morning. A rate of 40.8% is high enough to ask a respectful and unavoidable question about how consistently those readings translate into decisions.

The second detail is hepatitis C. Nonuse of kidneys from donors who were antibody-positive but had no detectable virus fell to 32.2% in 2024 from 49.5% in 2016, which the report attributes to broader use of direct-acting antiviral drugs (SRTR & OPTN, 2026b). Run the arithmetic and the relative decline is about 35%. A single innovation in a different corner of medicine, a drug that cured a disease, changed the fate of a third of the kidneys that had been carrying a certain stigma. That strikes me as instructive about the nature of nonuse. A large share of it is not biological. It is a function of what clinicians believe they can safely do, and beliefs respond to evidence and to innovation.

The third detail is about the reasons for nonuse. One analysis of registry data from 2010 through 2021 reported that "no recipient located" had become the most frequent stated reason, reaching about 60% of the reasons recorded (Transplantology, 2024†). I classify that figure as Grade C because I could confirm it only from a summary, and I would not stake an argument on it alone. But if even approximately true, it suggests that a major share of nonuse is a failure of placement and not a verdict on organ quality, meaning that the kidney was acceptable in principle and nobody could be found to take it in time.

## Giving the problem an operational definition

Six Sigma insists on an operational definition: a rule so precise that two people applying it independently would count the same defects. Let me propose one. The unit is a recovered kidney. A defect is a recovered kidney that is not transplanted. The opportunity count is one per kidney. By these conventions, the defect rate in 2024 was 29.3%, or 293,000 defects per million opportunities.

By the usual convention of the discipline, which includes a long-term allowance of one and a half standard deviations of drift, that rate corresponds to a sigma level of roughly 2.04. For comparison, the 2013 rate of 18.2% corresponds to about 2.41, the 2023 rate to about 2.09, and a mature, well-controlled process, often described as operating at four sigma, would show about 6,200 defects per million, which is 0.6% (George et al., 2005). I calculated these values myself and they are illustrations, not official statistics.

[[FIG:sigma|Figure 3.2. Estimated sigma level of kidney nonuse, 2013–2024, treating each unused recovered kidney as a defect. Author's calculation from published nonuse rates.]]

A fair critic will object that not every unused kidney is a defect. Some organs are genuinely unsuitable, because of disease, injury, or anatomical problems, and no process could have transplanted them. That objection is correct, and it is the reason a real improvement project would begin by classifying each unused kidney as either unavoidable or potentially avoidable, using agreed criteria, and publishing the split. I do not know what that split is. I do know that the registry already records stated reasons, which means the raw material for the classification exists. The most useful single thing a policymaker could do about nonuse would be to ask for the Pareto chart, the ranked bar graph of reasons named for the Italian economist Vilfredo Pareto and made a staple of quality work by Joseph Juran (Juran, 1951). If three reasons account for most of the loss, the improvement target is obvious. If the reasons are diffuse, the target is the process itself.

## The reading: what the arithmetic of nonuse implies

Here is a calculation that requires no special data. For every one thousand kidneys recovered, a nonuse rate of 29.3% means 293 are not transplanted. At the 2013 rate of 18.2%, the figure would have been 182. The difference is 111 kidneys per thousand recovered. I stress the proportion because the registry's absolute counts shift with each year's donation volume, but the proportion travels cleanly: if the system could return to the performance of a decade earlier, roughly one additional transplant would result for every nine recovered organs.

It would be glib to assume that recovery is easy. The report that documents the rise in nonuse also notes that broader geographic organ sharing, introduced to improve equity, has coincided with longer cold ischemia times and higher rates of delayed graft function, and it characterizes the progress of recent years as offset by those costs (SRTR & OPTN, 2026a). I read this as an honest admission that the system pursued one virtue, wider fairness in sharing, and incurred a cost in another, speed and organ condition. Well-run improvement projects expect trade-offs of this kind and treat them as inputs to design, and not as occasions for assigning blame.

I will lay out the competing explanations for rising nonuse so the reader can see which ones the record supports and which it merely invites. *Donor drift*: donors are, on average, older and sicker than they were, and some of the rise is real. *Risk aversion*: centers may decline marginal kidneys to protect their outcome statistics, a hypothesis frequently voiced and not directly quantified in the literature available to me. *Logistics*: sharing across greater distances adds hours. *Placement bandwidth*: the sequential offering of a kidney down a list consumes time that the kidney does not have. *Information gaps*: clinicians cannot see what they cannot measure, as the hepatitis C example suggests. The record supports the second-to-last of these most concretely, and the last most elegantly. It leaves the first and the second open.

## What Chapter 4 asks of the reader

I would like the reader to carry one thought forward. A nonuse rate of 29.3% is not an accusation against any single surgeon or coordinator, and it should not be received as one. It is a property of the system as a whole, the way the failure rate of an assembly line is a property of the line. A property of a system can be changed only by changing the system, and a system changes when someone with authority decides that a published number is intolerable. That is the sense in which the number should embarrass us. It is not a verdict on anyone's character. It is a verdict on our patience.

# Chapter 5: The Gate Before the Gate
EPIGRAPH: "Hope deferred maketh the heart sick: but when the desire cometh, it is a tree of life."
BY: Proverbs 13:12 (King James Version, 1611)

## A funnel with five mouths

The list is not the first queue. Before a person can wait for a kidney, that person must pass through a series of gates, and each gate narrows. They must be told that transplantation is possible. They must be referred to a center. They must be evaluated. They must be accepted for listing. Only then does the organ-allocation process, the one that makes headlines, even begin. Quality engineers have a name for the cumulative performance of such a sequence. They call it rolled throughput yield, and it is computed by multiplying the pass rates of each stage.

A hypothetical will show why this matters. Suppose each of five gates passed eighty percent of the patients who reached it. Each stage would look respectable, but the rolled yield would be 0.8 multiplied by itself five times, or about 33%. Two out of every three patients would have exited before reaching the list. I offer these figures as arithmetic and not as findings, since the real stage-by-stage rates are not published in the sources I could reach. The purpose of the illustration is to explain why a system can be full of conscientious people at every gate and still lose most of its candidates in transit.

## The record: who is told, and who is not

A national analysis of the federal forms that dialysis facilities file when a patient begins treatment, covering 133,414 patients between 2007 and 2019, found that 15% had not received information about transplantation (Villani et al., 2024†). Patients who were not informed were more often older, female, white, and covered by Medicare. Those who were informed reached the waiting list sooner and spent less time waiting. The study also found something subtler, a finding that complicates any simple story about corporate behavior: patients at chain facilities were more likely to be informed, yet that did not translate into higher rates of waitlisting or transplantation. Patients at independent facilities that were acquired by a chain were more likely to be informed after the acquisition but less likely to be added to the list.

An earlier analysis compared what 388 dialysis patients reported with what their clinicians reported to the federal government, and found that clinician-reported discussions about transplantation did not increase a patient's likelihood of being waitlisted, which led its authors to suggest that better methods of informing patients may be needed (Healio, 2018). The finding is a quiet rebuke to checkbox compliance. A box that says "discussed" can be ticked without anyone having been understood.

Poverty and place leave measurable marks on the path. A cohort study published in 2026 found that more than one in ten maintenance dialysis patients had at least one documented social risk factor, such as transportation insecurity, and that such patients had higher odds of being neither transplanted nor actively waitlisted, with Black and American Indian patients showing higher odds than White patients (Social risk factors and kidney transplant waitlisting among patients receiving dialysis, 2026†). A second 2026 paper found that more than half of adults who began dialysis did so without a prepared vascular access or without any pre-dialysis kidney care, and that these unprepared starts were more common at for-profit and smaller facilities and among patients who were younger, Black or Hispanic, uninsured, not informed of transplantation, or living in high-poverty neighborhoods (Pre-dialysis care and access type, 2026†). A third analysis reported that Black candidates who lived in the most disadvantaged neighborhoods had an adjusted hazard of receiving a transplant of 0.60, with a confidence interval of 0.58 to 0.62, compared with White candidates in the least disadvantaged neighborhoods (Residential neighborhood disadvantage, n.d.†). In plain language, after adjustment the rate of receiving a transplant was about forty percent lower. Staff interviewed across four regions of the country described non-medical barriers, from transportation to documentation to the sheer complexity of the process, as obstacles that clinical eligibility criteria do not capture (Non-medical barriers, 2026†).

## The record: ownership and incentives

The most contested piece of evidence in this territory concerns who owns the dialysis chair. A large retrospective study published in 2019 followed 1,478,564 patients across 6,511 facilities between 2000 and 2016 and found that patients treated at for-profit facilities were significantly less likely to be waitlisted than patients at nonprofit facilities (Gander et al., 2019). The senior author attributed the pattern to incentives, noting that for-profit facilities earn higher margins when more patients remain on dialysis. That attribution is an interpretation, and a plausible one. It is not an observed motive, and the study's ownership categories are heterogeneous. I use it as a hypothesis the data are consistent with, and the fact that the study is seven years old reminds me to say so.

The federal government has tried to move that incentive. Under the ESRD Treatment Choices model, which began in January 2021 and was randomly assigned to dialysis facilities and clinicians in 31% of hospital referral regions, payments are adjusted according to a facility's rates of home dialysis, waitlisting, and living-donor transplantation (CMS, 2025). The first-year evaluation reported that 663 patients were added to the waiting list among participating facilities, a 4% increase over the pre-model rate. After three years, the evaluators' summary concluded that the model had no impact on waitlisting or on living-donor transplant rates, the two outcomes it was explicitly designed to move (CMS, 2025). I classify this as Grade B, since I read a summary and not the full evaluation.

## The reading: when a modest incentive fails, what does that teach?

If a payment adjustment that targets the waitlist does not change waitlisting, there are at least three explanations. The first is that the adjustments were too small to alter behavior against a competing incentive that is larger. The second is that the bottleneck does not lie with the dialysis facility at all but with the downstream capacity of transplant centers to evaluate and list additional patients. The third is that the barriers lie with patients, in transportation, documentation, and distrust, which a facility's payment cannot reach. The evidence in this chapter is consistent with all three, and a well-designed project would look to separate them by comparing facilities with similar incentives and different downstream capacity.

What the findings do not support is a comfortable conclusion that the referral gap is a problem of attitude that will yield to exhortation. Telling dialysis staff to care more has been tried. The result, as measured, is a 15% rate of patients who were not informed at the start (Villani et al., 2024†), a gap between clinician-reported discussion and actual waitlisting (Healio, 2018), and a persistent shadow of poverty over the likelihood of receiving a kidney (Residential neighborhood disadvantage, n.d.†).

## A conservative argument for publishing facility-level numbers

This is the first place where I will put an argument on the table that readers of different political leanings may weigh differently. Facility-level waitlisting rates, adjusted for patient mix and published in an accessible, searchable form, would give patients and referring physicians information that no payment model can supply. This is not a regulatory burden of the heavy kind. It is a disclosure. It is also the kind of reform that a market-minded observer should favor, since markets work only when buyers can compare, and a patient choosing a dialysis facility is, at present, comparing blind. If the incentive to keep a patient on dialysis is real, the antidote is not a new bureaucracy. It is daylight.

# Chapter 6: The Clock
EPIGRAPH: "Life is short, and Art long; the crisis fleeting; experience perilous, and decision difficult."
BY: Hippocrates, Aphorisms I.1 (Adams translation, 1849)

## Why the cooler matters

A kidney removed from a donor is cooled and flushed with a preservation solution, and from that moment it lives on borrowed time. The interval between the cessation of its blood supply and the restoration of circulation in the recipient is called the cold ischemia time, and it is the namesake of the foundation that sponsors this book. Cold slows the metabolism of the organ, buying hours. It does not stop injury. The longer the interval, the more cellular damage accumulates before the kidney is reconnected to a living circulation.

## The record: what an hour costs

The most widely cited estimate of the price of an extra hour comes from a French cohort of 3,839 deceased-donor transplant recipients followed from 2000 to 2011. It found that each additional hour of cold ischemia raised the hazard of graft failure by a factor of 1.013 and the hazard of death by a factor of 1.018 (Debout et al., 2015†). The authors illustrated the effect with an example, noting that a recipient of a kidney with 30 hours of cold ischemia faced a risk of graft failure near 40% higher than a recipient of a kidney with 6 hours. My own extrapolation, compounding the per-hour ratio across the 24 intervening hours, gives about 36% for graft failure and about 53% for mortality. The difference between my number and the authors' is the difference between a compounded estimate and a rounded statement, and I mention both so that no reader mistakes my arithmetic for theirs.

A recent study offers a threshold perspective. Reported in June 2026, an analysis of 74 pairs of kidneys from older donors, in which the two kidneys of each donor were compared, found that cold ischemia beyond 12 hours, compared with less than 8 hours, was associated with a markedly higher risk of delayed graft function, with an adjusted odds ratio of 6.30 (95% confidence interval 1.52 to 26.06; p = 0.011), and with a tripling of the risk of death, even though death-censored graft survival was not affected (Transplant International, 2026†). The sample is small and comes from a single center, and the confidence interval is wide enough to drive a truck through. The result should be read as a signal worth replicating and not as a rule.

Another analysis estimated that regional sharing added about 2.9 hours to cold ischemia compared with local allocation (Appendix A, item E5). Studies of logistics add texture to the picture. In an analysis of 206 transplant centers, the median cold ischemia time for kidneys transplanted in the same hospital where they were recovered ranged from 3.3 to 29 hours (Appendix A, item E5). Weekend procurement has been associated with a higher chance that a kidney will be discarded and not transplanted, which the authors attributed to factors other than organ quality (Appendix A, item E5). A network analysis of 106,160 kidneys found that more sharing between organ procurement organizations lowered the odds of discard for high-volume organizations and raised them for low-volume ones (Appendix A, item E5). I place these in Grade B because I read them in a single literature summary. Taken together they describe a clock that does not run at one speed.

## The record: the case that perfusion technique matters

If time is the enemy, the main weapon on offer is machine perfusion, the practice of continuously pumping cold preservation solution through the organ in place of storing it still on ice. A landmark randomized trial of 672 deceased-donor recipients, in which one kidney from each donor went to each arm, found that machine perfusion reduced delayed graft function and improved one-year graft survival (Moers et al., 2009). A ten-year follow-up was announced in November 2025 by the manufacturer of the perfusion device, which reported a 27% lower risk of graft failure over the full decade, with complete ten-year data for 92% of recipients (Organ Recovery Systems, 2025). I classify the follow-up as Grade C. The announcing party sells the device, the underlying publication carries a designation that may mark it as a letter and not a full trial report, and one summary describes the analysis as post hoc. The result may well be sound. It deserves confirmation from the paper itself before anyone repeats it as settled.

## The reading: a continuous variable treated as a cliff

Here is the observation I find most useful in this chapter. The evidence describes the harm of cold ischemia as a slope. Each hour adds a little risk, and the slope may steepen beyond roughly half a day. Yet the behavior of clinicians, when they are offered a kidney, looks like a cliff. A national study of offers made between 2021 and 2023 found that centers varied sharply in their willingness to accept offers for suboptimal kidneys, and that the widest variation, with a median odds ratio of 4.86, concerned kidneys with cold ischemia beyond 36 hours (Quantifying center-level aggressiveness, n.d.†). The same study reported that the 193 adult centers accepted a median of 12.5% of offers for standard kidneys and only 7.2% for suboptimal ones.

When a continuous harm is converted into a binary rule, two kinds of error multiply. Some kidneys that would have served a patient well are declined because they crossed an arbitrary line. Others that crossed no line are accepted without adequate attention to the compounding costs. Quality engineers solve this class of problem with a decision aid that translates a continuous risk into a graded recommendation, one that tells the clinician what the expected benefit and the expected hazard are for this recipient and this kidney. I am not aware that such a tool is standard at the bedside, and I would argue that its absence is a design omission and not a fact of nature.

## Make the cold chain visible

A final proposal for this chapter is the simplest in the book. Every kidney should carry a time-stamped record of its journey: clamp, packaging, first offer, each decline, acceptance, departure, arrival, and reperfusion. These events are already observed by someone. What is missing is the routine publication of the intervals, by organization and by center, so that the 3.3-to-29-hour spread can be understood, challenged, and narrowed. A hospital that wishes to explain why its median is high should be given the chance. A hospital that cannot explain it should be given the encouragement. In both cases the patients who are waiting should be able to see the clock that governs their lives.
