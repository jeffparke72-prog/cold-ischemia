# Prologue: The Hum of the Corridor
EPIGRAPH: "Depend upon it, sir, when a man knows he is to be hanged in a fortnight, it concentrates his mind wonderfully."
BY: Samuel Johnson, as recorded by James Boswell (1791)

Every institution has a sound, and the sound of a transplant system is a hum. It lives in the fluorescent tubes above a corridor that smells of citrus disinfectant. It lives in the refrigerator compressor of a pharmacy, in the monitor that keeps its patient count of seconds, in a telephone that is expected to ring and does not. Anyone who has spent real time inside this world learns the hum the way sailors learn weather. You learn it without choosing to, and afterward you cannot stop hearing it.

I have lived long enough in these corridors, in more than one role and through more than one season, to be fluent in their dialect. I can tell you what a nurse's pause means before the sentence arrives. I know the arithmetic that families perform on the backs of envelopes: mileage, missed shifts, copayments, the cost of a motel near the hospital. I know that a waiting list is not a list at all. It is a held breath, stretched across a nation, divided into zip codes.

This book is not a memoir, and it will not behave like one. My own story belongs to me and to the few people who carried me through it. What I owe the reader is something harder to write and, I hope, more useful: an audit. I intend to look at the American kidney transplant system the way an engineer looks at a bridge that people are crossing at this very moment. I want to know where the load is carried, where the metal is fatigued, and which warning lights were wired to nothing.

Consider the shape of the problem. In 2024 the national registry counted 143,982 adults listed for a kidney, a number the official annual report described as approaching the peak recorded before the pandemic (Scientific Registry of Transplant Recipients [SRTR] & Organ Procurement and Transplantation Network [OPTN], 2026a). In that same year, 29.3% of the kidneys that surgeons and coordinators had managed to recover were never transplanted (SRTR & OPTN, 2026b). Read those two facts together and let them settle. A country that cannot find enough kidneys is declining nearly three in every ten that it finds.

Here is the unsettling part. Nobody involved is a villain in the cartoon sense. The surgeons I have met are exhausted and exacting. The coordinators drive through the night. The regulators, whatever else may be said about them, are reading the same tables that you and I can read. Yet the aggregate behavior of this well-meaning crowd produces waste on an industrial scale, and it does so reliably, year after year, with the unglamorous consistency of a machine. That consistency is the clue. Systems that fail by accident fail randomly. Systems that fail by design fail the same way every time.

Design is the operative word, because design is where accountability lives. A defect that is random can be mourned. A defect that is built in can be fixed, and the people who refuse to fix it can be named. This is why I have chosen, for the scaffolding of this book, a discipline that was born on factory floors and that has since traveled into hospitals: Lean Six Sigma. It is a method for people who are tired of anecdotes. It asks four unglamorous questions. What does the customer actually need? How are we really performing against that need? Why is the gap what it is? And what, specifically, will keep it closed once it has been closed?

Some readers will bristle at the vocabulary of manufacturing applied to the most intimate act in medicine. I understand the instinct and I share its reverence. But reverence has a poor record as a management technique. A kidney does not care how tenderly we speak of it. It cares about the hours it spends in a cooler, the number of phone calls that precede an acceptance, and whether someone on a Saturday night had the authority to say yes. Lean Six Sigma insists that these hours and calls and authorities be counted. Counting, I have come to believe, is a form of love that has been disciplined into usefulness.

You will find in these pages arguments that conservative readers will recognize and arguments that progressive readers will recognize, and I make no apology for borrowing from both. I believe in transparency as a public good and in accountability as an engine. I believe that institutions should be measured by outcomes and not intentions, that incentives should be aligned before they are lamented, and that any organization granted a monopoly over a human organ supply has accepted an obligation to be examined without flattery. I also believe that the people whom this system serves are owed plain language. Where a claim is a finding, I will call it a finding. Where it is an allegation, an anecdote, or a rumor, I will say so, and I will show my reasoning when I decline to repeat it.

Some of what follows will be uncomfortable for persons and organizations I respect. I would ask them to read the footnotes before the headlines, because the footnotes are where the evidence is. I would ask patients and care partners to read the headlines before the footnotes, because the headlines are where your lives are. And I would ask everyone to hold in mind a simple fact that no policy memo can soften: while we deliberate, the clock keeps running, and somewhere tonight a cooler is being carried down a hallway toward a decision.

Let us go and look at the cooler.

# Author's Note: How This Book Was Checked
EPIGRAPH: "Felix qui potuit rerum cognoscere causas."
BY: Virgil, Georgics, II.490 ("Fortunate is the one who has been able to learn the causes of things.")

A book that accuses systems of failure must first accuse itself of the same possibility. Here, in plain terms, is how this manuscript was built and where its limits lie.

**The six-month standard.** I set a rule for myself: the developments that carry this book's argument should be no more than about six months old as of the date of writing, 9 October 2026. Where the newest reliable account of a subject is older, I say so in the text. A handful of landmark studies, such as the classic comparison of mortality among patients on dialysis and those who receive transplants (Wolfe et al., 1999), are older than that by decades. They appear only as background, they are labeled as foundational, and nothing in my argument rests on them alone.

**What counts as evidence.** Every factual claim carries an inline citation in the style of the American Psychological Association. I graded each source on a three-step ladder. Grade A means a primary document or registry, such as a federal rule, an agency report, a registry data report, or a peer-reviewed paper, whose key figures I could confirm. Grade B means a credible secondary account, such as a law-firm analysis or trade press report, of a primary document. Grade C means a single-source figure, or one that originates with an organization that has a stake in the result, such as a device manufacturer or an advocacy campaign. Grade C material is used sparingly and is always marked as preliminary in the text. The complete ladder, source by source, appears in Appendix A.

**How the book is organized.** The five parts follow the five phases of the Lean Six Sigma method: define the customer and the process, measure the defects, analyze their causes, improve, and control. Each chapter separates *the record*, which is what the sources document, from *the reading*, which is my interpretation of it. Where I argue for a policy, I say so, and I say what evidence would change my mind.

**Fact, finding, allegation, rumor.** These four words are not synonyms, and the book uses them with discipline. A fact is a measurement that anyone can reproduce. A finding is a conclusion reached by a body with authority and a method, such as a federal investigation. An allegation is a claim made by a party in a dispute and not yet adjudicated. A rumor is a claim with no traceable source. You will meet all four in Chapter 10, and you will see how easily the first three can be mistaken for one another.

**What I could not verify.** The research tools used for this manuscript returned summaries and the bibliographic details of many journal articles, but not the complete text of every paper or agency page. For that reason, a few citations are marked with a dagger (†). A dagger means that the central figures were confirmed against more than one summary, but that some bibliographic detail, such as a full author list or a final volume number, should be completed from the original before any edition is published. I would rather show you the gap than hide it. A foundation that conceals its own gaps has no business asking federal agencies to close theirs.

**Where the evidence is thin.** Several subjects in this book are not yet supported by strong evidence. The first year of the Increasing Organ Transplant Access model has not been reported. The recertification outcomes for organ procurement organizations have not been announced. No outcome data from the first clinical trial of pig kidney transplantation have been published. In those places, I analyze the design and the incentives, and I decline to pretend that results exist.

**Disclosure.** The Cold Ischemia Foundation accepts no donations, grants, or sponsorships, and it charges posted fees only for services that individuals request. The Foundation publishes educational material about oxygen-carrier technology for organ preservation, including the work of a company called BHOC Therapeutics. The author reports no financial interest in that company or in any other organization discussed in this book. The Foundation's own published position is that this technology remains a research concept whose benefits are unproven, and this book repeats that position without softening it.

**A word on the numbers.** Registry counts differ from one source to another because populations and definitions differ. A candidate who is listed at two centers may be counted twice in one table and once in another. When two figures from different sources appear in the same chapter, I explain why they differ. Where I have calculated a figure myself, such as an extrapolation of a published hazard ratio or an estimated sigma level, the text and the figure caption say so.

**Not medical or legal advice.** This book analyzes systems and policy. It is not clinical guidance and does not replace the counsel of your own medical team. If you are in a medical emergency, call 911. If you or someone you love is in emotional crisis, call or text 988.

# PART ONE: DEFINE
EPIGRAPH: "That which is not good for the swarm, neither is it good for the bee."
BY: Marcus Aurelius, Meditations, VI.54 (Long translation, 1862)

Every quality discipline begins in the same place, which is with a definition. Before one measures a process, one must say what the process is for and who it is for. Part One does that work. It describes the customer who was never surveyed, and it draws the map of a process that most participants experience only as a fragment.
