# Chapter 3: A Field Guide to Reading Transplant Numbers
EPIGRAPH: "There is something fascinating about science. One gets such wholesale returns of conjecture out of such a trifling investment of fact."
BY: Mark Twain, Life on the Mississippi (1883)

## Why the numbers disagree

Anyone who spends time with transplant statistics will eventually experience a small crisis. Two reputable sources report different figures for what seems like the same quantity, and the reader wonders which one is wrong. In my experience the answer is usually that neither is wrong. They are answering slightly different questions. Because this book rests on numbers, and because many readers will want to repeat them, I will devote a chapter to the grammar of the numbers themselves. The material is dry, and I ask for patience. A reader who finishes it will be able to detect a bad statistic in a press release, a campaign flyer, or a hallway conversation.

Begin with the disagreements that appeared in the preceding chapters. The annual data report counted 143,982 adults listed for a kidney in 2024, and in the same publication family an overview counted 147,091 listings (SRTR & OPTN, 2026a, 2026b). The difference comes from counting. A candidate registered at two centers appears twice in one table, because the unit of analysis is the listing, and once in the other, because the unit is the person. Neither table is mistaken. They measure different things, and a careful writer states which one is being used.

A second disagreement concerns deaths on the list. In the registry's removal table, 4,448 kidney candidates were removed for death in 2022, whereas the annual report cites 4,734 for the same year (OPTN, n.d.; SRTR & OPTN, 2026a). I cannot determine from the summaries available to me exactly why the two differ. The usual suspects are differences in the population included, in the way the categories are defined, and in the date on which the data were extracted, since registry records are revised as late information arrives. The lesson is practical: never splice figures from two tables into one trend line without confirming that they share a definition.

A third disagreement concerns nonuse. The report that counted 27.9% in 2023 explains that kidneys recovered together as a pair are counted once while kidneys recovered separately are counted twice (SRTR & OPTN, 2025). Another analysis reports a "discard" rate of 25% in 2021 using a different method and source, and the authors of the summary I read cautioned that it should not be compared directly with the registry's nonuse figures. When someone tells you that the discard rate is "about a quarter," the intelligent response is to ask which measure, which year, and which denominator.

## Relative numbers hide absolute ones

The most common trick in health statistics requires no dishonesty at all, only a choice of which number to print. A relative change describes a ratio, and an absolute change describes a difference. They can differ enormously in their emotional force.

I have already used an example. Nonuse of hepatitis C-positive kidneys fell from 49.5% to 32.2%. The absolute drop is 17.3 percentage points. The relative drop is about 35%. Both are true, and both belong in an honest account, but only the first tells you how many kidneys were affected.

Now consider a figure that appeared in Chapter 6: a 27% lower risk of graft failure with machine perfusion over ten years, according to the device manufacturer (Organ Recovery Systems, 2025). That is a relative statement, and it cannot be translated into a benefit for a patient without knowing the baseline. Suppose, purely for illustration, that 30% of kidneys in a comparison group had failed by ten years. A 27% relative reduction would lower that to about 21.9%, an absolute difference of 8.1 percentage points, and about twelve patients would need to be perfused for one additional graft to survive. If the baseline were 10%, the same relative reduction would yield an absolute difference of 2.7 points, and about thirty-seven patients would need perfusion for one graft to benefit. The relative number is the same. The meaning is not. The hypothetical baselines are mine and are not data, and I use them only to show why a headline that gives only the relative figure cannot be evaluated.

## A ratio per hour is a compounding machine

The French analysis in Chapter 6 reported that each added hour of cold ischemia raised the hazard of graft failure by a factor of 1.013 (Debout et al., 2015†). An increase of 1.3% sounds negligible, and a reader may dismiss it. But a hazard ratio per unit applies repeatedly across many units. Multiplying 1.013 by itself twenty-four times, which covers the difference between six hours and thirty, yields about 1.36. The small number turns into a large effect through nothing more exotic than arithmetic. I flag this because the opposite error is common. A speaker who wishes to downplay a risk will quote the per-hour figure, and a speaker who wishes to inflate it will quote the cumulative one, and in both cases the number is accurate.

## Uncertainty is information, not decoration

A study reports a point estimate, such as an odds ratio of 0.68, and a confidence interval around it, such as 0.28 to 1.64. The temptation is to read only the first number. The interval is the more honest half of the report. It describes the range of effects that the data cannot rule out, and a wide interval means that the study knew little. The 2026 trial of normothermic perfusion reported a p-value of 0.38 (Normothermic versus hypothermic machine perfusion in kidney transplantation, 2026†). A careless summary would state that normothermic perfusion had no effect. A careful one would say that the trial was too small to tell, because its interval stretched from a large benefit to a substantial harm.

The same discipline applies to agreement statistics. When researchers measure how often clinicians agree on a decision, they typically report a coefficient that corrects for the agreement expected by chance. By the widely used benchmarks, values from 0 to 0.20 indicate slight agreement, 0.21 to 0.40 fair, 0.41 to 0.60 moderate, 0.61 to 0.80 substantial, and 0.81 to 1.00 almost perfect agreement (Landis & Koch, 1977). The survey result I cited in Chapter 7, 0.13, sits in the lowest band. Remember that the bands are conventions and not laws of nature, and the result came from a single small survey. It is a warning light and not a verdict.

## Selection, confounding, and the healthy-patient problem

A great deal of misleading transplant commentary rests on a single fallacy, which is comparing groups that were different before the comparison began. Patients who receive transplants are healthier on average than patients who do not, because selection for transplantation favors the healthier. A crude comparison of survival between transplanted and non-transplanted patients therefore overstates the effect of the operation. Kidneys that undergo biopsy are more likely to be marginal, so a comparison of nonuse between biopsied and unbiopsied kidneys overstates the effect of the biopsy. Facilities that serve poorer communities differ from facilities that serve richer ones in dozens of ways, so a comparison of their waitlisting rates may reflect the patients and not the facility. The best studies address these problems with statistical adjustment, with matching, or, when possible, with randomization. When a study does not, the reader should adjust their confidence downward.

A related trap is the ecological fallacy, in which a property of a place is attributed to every person who lives there. A neighborhood disadvantage score describes a district. It does not describe the individual patient standing in a clinic, and conclusions drawn from the first should be applied to the second with humility.

## Who is speaking?

The source of a statistic is part of its meaning. A government registry reports counts because the law requires it. A manufacturer announces a result because the result helps sell a product. An advocacy organization reports a tally of supporters because supporters are what advocacy is for. None of this makes the figure false. It makes the figure interesting for a particular reason, and the reader should know the reason. I have tried throughout this book to give each of the sources a grade for this purpose, and I commend the practice.

It also helps to know the ladder of publication on which a result sits. A tweet is not a press release. A press release is not an abstract at a conference. An abstract is not a peer-reviewed paper. A peer-reviewed paper from a single center is not a multicenter randomized trial, and a single trial is not a replicated finding. A great many claims that circulate in the transplant world are standing on the lowest rungs and are being described as though they stood on the highest.

## The lag in the data

Registry reports describe the past. The annual data report that I cite for 2024 was published in 2026, which is typical, and the federal tier assignments for organ procurement organizations rest on data from 2024 as well (Holland & Knight, 2026). A policy discussed in October 2026 is therefore frequently argued with numbers from a year that ended nearly two years earlier. This does not make the numbers wrong. It does mean that a reader should always ask what year a number describes before asking what it implies about today.

## Ten questions to ask of any number

:::note The field guide in ten questions
1. Who counted this, and what was the unit: a person, a listing, a kidney, a hospital?
2. Which years does it cover, and when was it published?
3. What is the denominator?
4. What is it being compared with, and were the groups alike at the start?
5. Is the change reported in relative or absolute terms, and can I see both?
6. How uncertain is it? Is there an interval, and how wide?
7. Who is announcing it, and what do they gain?
8. Has anyone independent confirmed it?
9. Where does it sit on the ladder: tweet, press release, abstract, paper, trial, replication?
10. What would I expect to see if it were wrong?
:::

Readers who adopt this habit will find, as I have, that most arguments about the transplant system are not really arguments about facts. They are arguments about which facts to emphasize. Knowing the grammar will not end such arguments, but it will make the reader an unusually difficult person to mislead.
