# Chapter 10: Trust Is a Process Variable
EPIGRAPH: "It may seem a strange principle to enunciate as the very first requirement in a Hospital that it should do the sick no harm."
BY: Florence Nightingale, Notes on Hospitals (1863)

## Why a quality book needs a chapter on trust

Engineers sometimes treat trust as a soft variable that lives in the marketing department. In the donation system that classification is a category error. Every deceased donation begins with a decision made by a grieving family, and that decision depends on a belief that the process which follows will be honest. Every registration on a state donor list is an act of advance trust. If belief erodes, supply contracts, and no allocation algorithm in the world can distribute organs that were never offered. Trust is therefore an input to the process in precisely the way a raw material is, and it can be depleted by the same carelessness.

This chapter examines the most consequential trust failure of recent years, together with the federal response and the regulatory action that followed. It is also the chapter where the discipline of separating fact from finding, allegation, and rumor matters most, because the subject is emotionally combustible and the temptation to repeat the most frightening version of every story is intense. I will therefore begin with a map of the vocabulary.

:::note Fact, finding, allegation, rumor
**Fact:** a measurement that anyone can reproduce, such as the date on which an agency issued a report.
**Finding:** a conclusion reached by a body with authority and a stated method, such as a federal investigation. A finding is not a verdict in a court of law, and its precise wording matters.
**Allegation:** a claim made by a party to a dispute that has not been adjudicated, such as a complaint by a family or a defense by an organization.
**Rumor:** a claim with no traceable origin, or one whose origin is a retelling of something stronger than the original said.
:::

## The record: what the federal investigators reported

In March 2025, the Division of Transplantation within HRSA completed a report on its investigation into Kentucky Organ Donor Affiliates, an organ procurement organization that had since merged with a neighboring Ohio organization and now operates as Network for Hope (HRSA Division of Transplantation, 2025). The investigators reviewed roughly 350 cases in which a planned organ recovery had been authorized but was ultimately not completed. In 73 of those cases, they concluded that officials should have considered halting the process sooner because the patients had high or improving levels of consciousness. In 20.8% of the cases, the report found features that were not conducive to donation after circulatory death in the patient's initial or subsequent neurological status. In at least 28 cases, representing 8.0% of the total, the records showed no documented time of cardiac death, which the investigators described as suggesting potential survival to hospital discharge (HRSA Division of Transplantation, 2025).

A summary issued by the Department of Health and Human Services in July 2025 stated that concerns were identified in 103 of 351 cases (Appendix A, item B5). The Energy and Commerce Committee of the House of Representatives held an oversight hearing on 22 July 2025 and afterward sent a bipartisan letter to HRSA (U.S. House Committee on Energy and Commerce, 2025). The agency ordered corrective actions that included a root-cause analysis and a policy under which any staff member may halt a donation. It directed the national transplant network to report every instance in which donation is paused over a safety concern, and to propose, for public comment, safeguards for patients who are candidates for donation after circulatory death. HRSA also stated that it had received reports of similar patterns at other organizations (HRSA Division of Transplantation, 2025).

The procedural history is part of the record. A review committee of the national network had closed a related case on 24 September 2024 without further action, and on 1 October of that year HRSA directed the network to reopen the matter (HRSA Division of Transplantation, 2025). The organization has disputed characterizations of its practices, and it has stated that it obtained a notice of full compliance following a separate audit by the Centers for Medicare & Medicaid Services (Appendix A, item B5). A widely reported individual account, concerning a man whose family says he showed signs of life as removal was prepared, has been contested by the organization, and I treat it as an allegation (Appendix A, item B5). A 2026 Kentucky bill, House Bill 510, titled an act relating to organ donation safety, noted that existing state law does not expressly require a pause in procurement when new indications of life arise and sought to address that gap; I could not confirm whether it passed (Kentucky General Assembly, 2026†).

## The record: what the board and the regulator did next

By late summer 2026, the network's board had adopted new rules for donation after circulatory death, which the oversight agency described as strengthening protections for potential donors and promoting greater consistency and accountability (HRSA, 2026c–2026f). The agency also reported the launch of standardized allocation compliance reports for all 55 organizations and the beginning of work on comprehensive safety and quality standards for organizations in the network, starting with the procurement organizations (HRSA, 2026a). Beginning on 1 October 2026, organizations moved to a new ventilated patient form designed to replace two legacy reporting mechanisms (HRSA, 2026c–2026f).

On the regulatory side, in September 2025 the federal government decertified a Miami-based procurement organization, Life Alliance Organ Recovery Agency, a division of the University of Miami Health System. The Secretary of Health and Human Services described the step as the first mid-cycle decertification of such an organization, and cited unsafe practices, inadequate training, chronic underperformance, understaffing, and paperwork errors (Fierce Healthcare, 2025; United Press International, 2025). The organization stated that it would not appeal and would cooperate with the transition. Separately, leaders of two House committees asked the organization for documents connected with reports of a Medicare fraud scheme. That is an allegation, and no finding has been announced in the accounts available to me.

## The record: what the public did

The clearest measurement of trust in this entire story comes from the national registry of donors. In the week after news coverage of the Kentucky allegations in October 2024, an average of 170 people per day removed themselves from the national registry, which was ten times the rate in the same week of 2023. That count did not include requests sent by email or removals from state registries. The head of the national association of procurement organizations observed that organ donation is based on public trust, and that when it erodes, it takes years to regain (Associated Press, 2024). I found no published data showing whether registrations recovered in 2025 or 2026. That absence is itself worth noting. A system that measures its trust only when a crisis forces it to has chosen not to measure it.

## Rumor control

The vocabulary introduced at the beginning of this chapter can now be applied to some of the sentences that have circulated about these events.

| Claim as commonly repeated | Where it stands |
|---|---|
| "Dozens of patients were not dead when removal began." | Overstated. The federal report found no documented time of cardiac death in at least 28 cases and described this as suggesting potential survival. That is a documentation finding with a worrying implication, and it is not a finding that 28 living patients underwent removal. |
| "A patient woke up on the table." | An allegation from a family, disputed by the organization. It is not, in the sources I reviewed, a federal finding. |
| "The problem is limited to one organization." | Not established. The agency stated that it had received reports of similar patterns elsewhere, but it did not publish the details. |
| "The decertified Miami organization committed Medicare fraud." | Unsupported as stated. Document requests were reported; no finding was announced. |
| "Registrations collapsed." | Partly true and incompletely measured. A tenfold spike in removals occurred in one week of 2024; longer-term effects are unreported. |

The table is useful for a reason beyond its content. Notice how little distance separates a careful sentence from an inflammatory one. The change from "no documented time of cardiac death" to "not dead" consists of a few words, and the change from "alleged" to "proved" consists of one. Anyone who intends to advocate for patients must learn to resist the second kind of sentence, even when it would help the cause, because a cause built on an overstatement will sooner or later be discredited by the first reader who checks.

## The reading: safety and supply are not opponents

A superficial reading of this episode sets safety against supply, as if every additional safeguard costs a kidney. I reject the framing for a reason that is both ethical and operational. The ethical reason is the one Florence Nightingale stated in the passage that opens this chapter: a medical institution must first avoid harm. The operational reason is that donation depends on belief, so a process that damages belief destroys the very resource it was built to harvest. Stronger safeguards at the moment of death determination are not a tax on supply. They are its insurance.

I would go further and argue that the Kentucky episode exposes a classic quality failure, which is the absence of a stop-the-line right. In the Toyota production system that gave Lean its name, any worker who sees a defect may halt the line without asking permission, because the cost of a halt is small beside the cost of a defect that reaches the customer (Liker, 2004). The corrective action that now allows any staff member to halt a donation is that principle transplanted into medicine. It is welcome. It would have been more welcome a decade earlier.

I would also argue that organizations granted exclusive franchises must expect to be examined without flattery, and that a regulator who examines them publicly is serving the donors and not betraying them. If the system is as good as its advocates say, it has nothing to fear from the light. If it is not, the light is the only disinfectant that has ever worked.

# Chapter 11: The Living Donor Paradox
EPIGRAPH: "The only gift is a portion of thyself."
BY: Ralph Waldo Emerson, "Gifts," Essays: Second Series (1844)

## The route around the shortage

If the deceased-donor pathway is a long chain with many seams, the living-donor pathway is a short one. A living donor can enter the process on their own initiative, the operation can be scheduled in advance, the cold ischemia time is measured in minutes and not in hours, and the transplanted kidney tends to last longer. In the old cohort data cited in Chapter 1, five-year survival was about 87.0% after a living-donor transplant against 73.7% after a deceased-donor transplant (USRDS, 2014). A system serious about shortening the list would treat living donation as its most efficient lever.

## The record: where living donation stands

The numbers are modest and the sources are mixed. One report, drawing on registry data, states that 6,522 living people donated a kidney in 2025, up from 6,419 in 2024 and 6,290 in 2023, and notes that these totals were the highest since before the pandemic, since 6,867 living donors gave kidneys in 2019 (PhillyVoice, 2026†). I classify that as Grade C, because I could trace the figure to a single news article and not to the registry itself, and I recommend that anyone quoting it verify it against the official registry. If it is accurate, the trend is encouraging and the level is still below the pre-pandemic benchmark.

The longer arc of living donation is less flattering. An older analysis reported a steady decline in living kidney donation after a peak in 2004, most pronounced among men, Black adults, and younger and lower-income adults (Appendix A, item G2). The pattern is not uniquely American. A 2026 article reports that Australia performed 253 living-donor kidney transplants in 2024, down from 354 in 2008, with people from lower socio-economic groups disadvantaged (Appendix A, item G5). When a decline appears in different countries and concentrates among those with the least financial cushion, a reasonable inference is that the cause is structural and not cultural.

## The record: the price of generosity

A consensus conference of transplant professionals published in 2015 identified the financial impact on donors as a potential source of the long decline, and recommended standardized reimbursement for lost wages and incidental costs, together with legal protections for employment and insurability (Tushla et al., 2015†). The medical costs of donation are generally covered. Many incidental costs are not: travel, lodging, child care, and weeks of lost income. The 2015 consensus is a decade old, and I cite it as background. What matters for the present book is that Congress acted in 2026.

The Honor Our Living Donors Act became law on 3 February 2026 as part of the Consolidated Appropriations Act, 2026 (Public Law 119-75). It prohibits the federal reimbursement program from considering the recipient's household income when deciding whether a donor is eligible for help, which had been the heart of the previous rule, in which donors were reimbursed only if the recipient's income fell below roughly 350% of the federal poverty guidelines (Appendix A, item G3). A donor's own household income may still determine priority. Eligible donors can receive up to $6,000 per organ for travel, meals, lost wages, and child and elder care, and a physicians' group has reported that 21% of donors in the program reach that cap. The act requires the program to report annually to Congress on how funds were spent, how many donors were reimbursed, and how many applied without being reimbursed. In July 2026, HRSA published a notice requesting public comment, and a later notice described the act's effect on eligibility (HRSA, 2026g†, 2026h†).

State law is an uneven patchwork. The American Kidney Fund's May 2026 report card found notable gains in several states and little or no progress in many others, and reported that 28 states have adopted laws addressing insurance discrimination against donors, though protection can be lost if a donor moves out of a covered state (American Kidney Fund, 2026). Private programs, such as wage-reimbursement and life-insurance protections offered through a national paired-donation registry, fill some gaps for those who happen to enter through them (Appendix A, item G4).

## The reading: the paradox

The paradox is plain once the pieces are assembled. The most efficient pathway to a kidney is the one most sensitive to household finances. It asks a donor to absorb costs that the recipient's insurance does not cover, in a country where the typical family has little slack. It should therefore surprise no one that the pathway is smaller than the need and that its decline is concentrated among lower-income donors. A well-intentioned society can honor donors in speeches and still deter them in practice.

The recent law narrows the paradox. It does not erase it. A $6,000 cap is a number, and a donor whose recovery requires eight weeks away from an hourly job may exhaust it quickly. That 21% of participants reach the cap suggests that the ceiling is binding for a meaningful minority. A conservative argument, and I mean the word in its most serious sense, is that a society that asks an individual to give an organ to a stranger or a relative should not also ask that person to pay for the privilege. Removing out-of-pocket cost for donors is an investment with a measurable return, because every successful living donation moves a candidate off the list and relieves the system of a stretch of dialysis spending.

## The contentious proposal: should donors be paid?

The boldest proposal now before Congress goes further than reimbursement. The End Kidney Deaths Act, introduced in April 2025 as H.R. 2687, would create a refundable federal tax credit for people who make non-directed living kidney donations, which are gifts to a stranger and not to a particular recipient. The donor would receive $10,000 for the year of donation and each of the following four years, totaling $50,000. It would apply to kidneys removed after 31 December 2026 and would sunset after 31 December 2036 (End Kidney Deaths Act, 2025). The bill was in committee as of spring 2026. Its sponsors' campaign reports some sixty cosponsors in the House, a figure I classify as Grade C, and a policy-tracking site assigns the bill a low chance of passage (Appendix A, item G6).

The law that stands in the way is the National Organ Transplant Act of 1984, which prohibits the transfer of human organs for valuable consideration (National Organ Transplant Act, 1984). The bill's designers have chosen a tax credit and not a payment in an attempt to respect that principle while changing the incentive. Reasonable people differ on whether the distinction is meaningful.

The case against is serious and deserves its strongest statement. Payment may induce poor people to take medical risks they would otherwise decline, converting economic desperation into a supply chain. It may crowd out altruistic donors, whose gifts carry a moral meaning that a transaction does not. It may erode public trust in a system whose legitimacy rests on the perception that organs are given and not sold, a perception that Chapter 10 showed to be fragile.

The case for is also serious. The status quo already imposes real costs on donors, most of whom are not wealthy. The waiting list is a graveyard of preventable deaths. A bounded, time-limited credit available only for gifts to strangers and paired with independent evaluation could generate the evidence that argument alone cannot. In my judgment, the strongest version of the conservative position is neither enthusiasm nor refusal. It is a demand for a pilot designed in advance to answer the right questions: whether the credit increases donations, who responds, whether outcomes and donor well-being are protected, and whether public trust survives. Policy without evidence is a prayer. Policy with a pre-registered evaluation is an experiment, and an experiment is the form of boldness that a free society can afford.

## What the paradox asks of the system

Whatever one thinks of payment, three things would be uncontroversial improvements. The reimbursement ceiling should be indexed to actual costs and reviewed annually. Eligibility should rest on a simple, published test that donors can apply to themselves in minutes. And the program's reporting to Congress, now required, should include the one number that matters most to a prospective donor: how many applied and how many were denied. A donor deserves to know the odds before the incision.
