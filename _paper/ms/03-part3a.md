# PART THREE: ANALYZE
EPIGRAPH: "He who knows only his own side of the case, knows little of that."
BY: John Stuart Mill, On Liberty (1859)

Measurement tells you how large the problem is. Analysis tells you what is holding it in place. The method of Part Three is the discipline's most venerable one: ask why, and then ask why again, and refuse to be satisfied by the first answer that blames somebody's character. The four chapters that follow examine decision-making, payment, trust, and the one pathway that bypasses most of the machinery, the living donor.

# Chapter 7: A Fishbone for a Wasted Kidney
EPIGRAPH: "The one great principle of the English law is, to make business for itself."
BY: Charles Dickens, Bleak House (1853)

## The diagram that shaped postwar quality

Across the middle of the twentieth century, the Japanese engineer Kaoru Ishikawa codified a simple drawing that has since traveled to every industry on earth. A horizontal spine runs toward a box that names the problem, and diagonal bones branch off the spine, each labeled with a category of cause. Ishikawa's contribution was not mathematical. It was a way of forcing a room full of experts to put their theories on one page, where they could be examined together and discarded or kept on the strength of evidence (Ishikawa, 1976). The diagram earned its nickname, the fishbone, from its shape.

I have drawn one for the problem of Chapter 4, the kidney that is recovered but never transplanted. Traditional manufacturing categories, which are usually remembered as the six M's, do not translate neatly to organ transplantation, so I adapted them into six categories of my own: people, policy, process, preservation, payment, and patient data.

## People: the variance that lives inside one human mind

The record on decision-making is the most striking in the whole analysis. A national study of kidney offers made between 2021 and 2023 found that the 193 adult centers accepted a median of 12.5% of offers for standard-risk kidneys and 7.2% of offers for suboptimal ones, with the widest center-to-center variation concentrated in kidneys that had endured more than 36 hours of cold ischemia (Quantifying center-level aggressiveness, n.d.†). Variation among centers is not by itself damning, since centers serve different populations and have different surgical teams. The deeper question is whether the variation is explained by anything a patient would recognize as a reason.

Earlier work suggests that much of it is not. A 2017 randomized experiment in which 68 physicians were shown identical kidney offers found that the variation within a single center was roughly ten times greater than the variation among centers (Appendix A, item E2). A 2023 survey of transplant nephrologists reported that agreement among them on whether to accept a specific offer was poor, with a Fleiss kappa of 0.13 (Appendix A, item E2). By the benchmarks that statisticians customarily apply, a kappa between zero and 0.20 indicates only slight agreement (Landis & Koch, 1977). I classify these two findings as Grade B because I read them in a summary, and I treat them as strong hints and not as proof.

## Measurement system analysis, applied to a human decision

Manufacturing has a technique for exactly this situation, called measurement system analysis, and its most famous application is the gauge repeatability and reproducibility study, or gauge R&R. The logic is as follows. Before you trust an inspector to reject defective parts, you check whether two inspectors, shown the same parts, reach the same verdicts, and whether one inspector reaches the same verdict on two occasions. If they do not, the inspection system is declared incapable, and no further conclusions are drawn from its output until it is repaired. A factory that discovered kappa of 0.13 among its inspectors would stop the line.

Kidney acceptance is an inspection. A human being examines a limited information packet about a donor and an organ, compares it with a prospective recipient, and issues a verdict that is irreversible for that organ. I do not claim that any individual decision is wrong. I claim that the system, taken as a whole, may be working with an inspection process whose reproducibility has never been publicly established. If that is true, the unused kidney is the visible product of an invisible variance.

## Policy and process: sharing, sequencing, and the weekend

The officially published verdict on policy is worth quoting at the level of paraphrase. The 2024 annual data report observes that progress has been offset by rising nonuse, longer cold ischemia times, and higher rates of delayed graft function under broader geographic organ-sharing policies (SRTR & OPTN, 2026a). That sentence is an unusually candid one for a federal publication, and it should be read as a design review. A policy changed the geometry of the system, expanding the territory over which a kidney can be offered, and the geometry's side effects are now appearing in the numbers.

The process around the policy contributes its own friction. An organ is offered to one candidate after another in a defined order, and a decline sends the offer to the next in line. Earlier registry analyses found that a deceased-donor kidney was offered a median of seven times before it was finally accepted (Appendix A, item E2). Every extra offer consumes minutes and, more often, hours. The rate at which kidneys procured on weekends are discarded, compared with those procured on weekdays, has also been reported as higher, which the authors attributed to factors other than organ quality (Appendix A, item E5). If that is true, the cause is not the kidney. It is the calendar.

## Patient data: when information is the missing organ

The two details from the registry that I highlighted in Chapter 4, the biopsy rate and the hepatitis C trajectory, belong on this bone. A kidney's fate is conditioned by what clinicians think they know about it, and what they think they know depends on tests whose results are imperfect and interpreted by tired people. Where a decision aid does not exist, intuition fills the vacuum, and intuition is where a kappa of 0.13 comes from.

## Preservation and payment

Two bones remain, and I address them at greater length in later chapters. Preservation technology, the subject of Chapter 12, can in principle widen the margin of time and function that a kidney has. Payment, the subject of Chapter 8, determines whom the system rewards for taking a chance. I mention them here only to complete the diagram and to note that, in a well-formed analysis, no category is allowed to monopolize the blame.

## A five-whys example

The final tool of this chapter is the humblest. The five whys technique asks a question and answers it, then asks why about the answer, and repeats until the chain reaches a cause that someone has the power to change. I offer an illustration, built from the elements assembled so far. It is a thought experiment and not a case study.

Why was this kidney unused? Because no recipient was located in time. Why was no recipient located? Because the offers were declined in sequence until the clock ran out. Why were they declined? Because each center's clinician judged the kidney marginal for that center's candidates. Why did the clinicians reach that judgment? Because the information available was limited and the decision was made under time pressure without a structured aid. Why was there no structured aid? Because no authority owns the quality of the acceptance decision, and no public measure exposes its variance.

Notice where the chain ends. It does not end at a wicked clinician. It ends at an unowned decision and an unpublished measure. That is a sign that the fish has been correctly filleted.

## An incentive map

Economists argue about almost everything, but they agree that behavior follows reward, and the reward structure of the kidney system is among the most intricate in American medicine. ## The record: the dialysis side

The Medicare Payment Advisory Commission reported in July 2026 that fee-for-service Medicare spent $7.6 billion in 2024 on dialysis, dialysis drugs, and related laboratory tests, a decline of 6% in nominal terms from the year before, a fall it attributed in large part to the growing enrollment of dialysis beneficiaries in Medicare Advantage plans since 2021 (Medicare Payment Advisory Commission [MedPAC], 2026). The same source reports that the number of patients with kidney failure grew by about one percent a year between 2019 and 2023, and that nearly 44% of them were over 65 in 2023.

The figure is noteworthy because it understates the magnitude of the system's economic weight. It excludes Medicare Advantage spending, and it excludes the care of patients covered by Medicaid and private insurers. What it illustrates is the scale of a revenue stream attached to the continuation of treatment. As I noted in Chapter 5, the senior author of the 2019 ownership study explained the lower waitlisting rates at for-profit facilities by observing that margins rise with the number of patients on dialysis (Gander et al., 2019). I do not allege that any provider acts on that incentive deliberately. I allege only that the incentive exists, that it points away from transplantation, and that a prudent designer would not rely on goodwill to offset it.

## The record: the hospital side

On 1 July 2025, the Centers for Medicare & Medicaid Services launched the Increasing Organ Transplant Access model, a mandatory payment model that runs until 30 June 2031. In its first performance year the model covered 103 kidney transplant hospitals in roughly half of the country's donation service areas, with the remaining areas serving as a comparison group, and 95 hospitals were selected for the second year (CMS, n.d.). The first year carried upside risk only, so hospitals could earn bonuses but could not be penalized. The second year adds downside risk.

Performance is scored out of 100 points across three domains. Achievement, measured by transplant volume, carries 60 points. Efficiency, measured by the rate at which a hospital accepts organ offers, carries 20. Quality, measured by graft survival, carries the final 20. A hospital can earn up to $15,000 for each Medicare kidney transplant it performs, and can be charged at most $2,000 (CMS, n.d.). A 2026 final rule, as I understand it from a secondary account, raised the minimum volume required for inclusion from 11 kidney transplants to 15 (Appendix A, item C1). The model's first-year results have not been published, and the performance year closed only on 30 June 2026.

## The reading: the first price on an acceptance decision

I want to draw attention to the 20-point efficiency domain, because it is the first mechanism I have encountered in federal policy that attaches money to the very decision I examined in Chapter 7. For decades the offer-acceptance decision was regulated by norms and peer expectation, with no explicit price. The new model creates one, and it creates a counterweight in the quality domain, since a hospital that accepts more marginal kidneys will see its graft survival suffer if the judgment was wrong. That is an elegant piece of design, in the sense that it addresses both sides of a trade-off.

Yet the model also tells us what its designers believe. A payment structure encodes a theory of the problem, and this one encodes the theory that hospitals decline too many usable offers and perform too few transplants. The asymmetry between a $15,000 maximum bonus and a $2,000 maximum penalty, a ratio of seven and a half to one, encodes a second belief: that the program should recruit participation before it demands sacrifice. Whether those theories hold is an empirical matter that the first year's data will begin to answer.

## The record: the organ procurement side

The 55 organ procurement organizations that recover organs from deceased donors operate as designated monopolies within defined service areas. Since 2020 they have been measured by two rates, a donation rate and a transplantation rate, and sorted into three tiers. The top quarter are placed in Tier 1 and are automatically recertified. Organizations that fall below the median on either rate are placed in Tier 3 and face decertification. Tier 2 organizations in between must compete to retain their service areas (Holland & Knight, 2026). The first recertification cycle ran from 1 August 2022 to 31 July 2026, and federal officials stated that Tier 3 organizations' contracts would remain in effect through 31 January 2027 to allow time for appeals and to prevent disruption (Holland & Knight, 2026).

The rule that governs the transition remains in motion. A proposed rule published in the Federal Register on 30 January 2026 would decertify an organization that places in Tier 3 in all of its service areas, would grant a right to appeal the removal of any Tier 3 service area, and would clarify the administrative appeal process (CMS, 2026). Federal officials have said they expect a final rule in late 2026, with recertification and decertification actions to follow sixty days after it takes effect (Holland & Knight, 2026). I searched for a published roster of the organizations that would fall into Tier 3 in the 2026 cycle and did not find one. In an authoritative sense, the outcome of the first great competition among monopolists has not yet been announced.

A peer-reviewed analysis published in the journal *Transplantation* reached a finding that deserves to be quoted carefully. It reported that the organizations projected to fall into Tier 3, and so face automatic decertification, serve disproportionately high shares of non-White populations across the 55 organizations (Centers for Medicare and Medicaid Services performance metrics and the disproportionate impact of decertifying organ procurement organizations on minority populations, n.d.†). One reading of that finding is that the metrics, by failing to adjust for community characteristics, penalize organizations that serve disadvantaged communities. A second reading is that persistent underperformance in those communities is exactly what the tiers were designed to expose. I have not found, in the sources available to me, a rigorous attempt to adjudicate between the readings, and I regard the absence as a notable gap.

## The reading: a contestable monopoly

A conservative economist will find something familiar in the tier system. Natural monopolies cannot be eliminated, since a donation service area is a geographic franchise, but they can be made contestable. The theory of contestable markets holds that a monopolist who knows a rival may take its place behaves more like a competitor than one who does not (Baumol et al., 1982). The tier system is an attempt to create that threat. Tier 2 organizations must compete, and Tier 3 organizations face loss of their franchise. If the threat is credible, the incentive to improve is real.

The threat is credible only if it is carried out. A decertification rule that is written but not executed teaches the regulated industry that the rule is theater. The nation witnessed one real act of enforcement in September 2025, when HHS decertified an organ procurement organization in Miami mid-cycle, a step the Secretary described as the first of its kind (Fierce Healthcare, 2025). I take up that episode in the next chapter, where the subject is trust.

## The danger of a good metric

No analysis of incentives would be responsible without a warning, and the warning has a name. The anthropologist Marilyn Strathern put it in a form that has become canon: when a measure becomes a target, it ceases to be a good measure (Strathern, 1997). Every metric described in this chapter is vulnerable to the pattern. A transplant-volume score can be inflated by transplanting patients who would have done well anyway. A graft-survival score can be protected by declining difficult cases. An offer-acceptance rate can be improved by accepting offers that should be declined. A donation rate can be raised in ways that endanger a potential donor.

For that reason I would argue that metrics must be published in constellations and not in isolation. A hospital's transplant volume should be shown beside its graft survival and its acceptance rate, adjusted for case mix, so that anyone who games one number reveals themselves in another. This is not a radical idea. It is the central insight of balanced measurement, and it is overdue.
