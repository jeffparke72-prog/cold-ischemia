# Chapter 14: Awareness Without Evidence
EPIGRAPH: "No man will take counsel, but every man will take money: therefore money is better than counsel."
BY: Jonathan Swift, Thoughts on Various Subjects (1711)

## What awareness is for

Few ideas in public health are as generously funded and as poorly measured as awareness. Campaigns are launched, ribbons are tied, proclamations are signed, and the activity is counted by impressions and events. Yet awareness is only a means. The ends are behaviors: a person registers as a donor, a patient asks about transplantation, a family says yes in the hospital. A quality engineer would ask of every awareness program what a clinician asks of every medication: does it work, in whom, and by how much?

## The record: how many people are registered?

National registration figures are surprisingly hard to pin down. A presidential proclamation in 2020 put the share of adults who are registered donors at approximately 60% (Appendix A, item I2). An undated health-system article cited 58% (Appendix A, item I2). A donation-industry report from 2013 had reported 45% (Appendix A, item I2). The figures reflect different definitions and different years, and I found no national percentage for 2025 or 2026. What a reader can responsibly conclude is that roughly half to three-fifths of American adults have registered at some point, and that the nation does not appear to publish an up-to-date figure.

A registration is, moreover, an input and not a transplant. The great majority of registrants will never die in circumstances that allow donation, so the connection between a registration and an organ is probabilistic and mediated by family consent, hospital practice, and organ procurement organization performance. That is a reason to measure awareness campaigns by their effect on registrations that stick and, ultimately, by the donation and transplant rates that follow, not merely by the number of people who say they approve.

## The record: what the experiments show

The cleanest evidence I found is a field experiment published in the *Journal of Marketing* in 2021. Researchers intercepted customers at service locations in Ontario, 3,330 of them, with messages targeting information and altruistic motives at the moment of service, and streamlined the registration process. Registrations, which were actual behavior and not stated intention, increased significantly (Appendix A, item I1). An online experiment in Australia with 762 young adults found that a message emphasizing the ease of registration was most effective at producing intentions to register, and the finding informed the 2021 national campaign (Appendix A, item I1).

By contrast, a campaign in a Riyadh shopping mall reported that 90.9% of people surveyed decided to register as donors. That figure measures a stated decision in a short, subjective survey, and the authors themselves noted that the effect on actual registrations was unexamined (Appendix A, item I1). I mention it as an example of the weak outcomes against which stronger designs should be compared.

I also encountered, in the course of this research, a web page that claimed a 2026 campaign had produced a 12% rise in registrations across fourteen countries, citing the World Health Organization. It named no verifiable source and its own text broke off in mid-sentence. I did not use it, and I mention it for a reason. Awareness is a field in which weak statistics circulate easily, and an advocate who repeats them borrows against the credibility of the cause.

## The reading: three lessons from the strong designs

The better experiments share three features. First, they target the moment of decision, not the abstract season of awareness: a message delivered where registration is possible outperforms a message delivered in a vacuum. Second, they reduce friction, since the Australian result suggests that people respond when they are told it is easy. Third, they measure actual behavior. The first two are design lessons. The third is a discipline of honesty.

What the experiments do not address is the problem that the Kentucky episode revealed. Awareness is built on trust, and trust, as Chapter 10 argued, can be lost in a week. A campaign that urges registration while the process behind it is visibly in doubt is pouring water into a bucket with a hole. For that reason I would urge that every donation-awareness program include a transparency component, a plain account of how death is determined, who is empowered to halt a donation, and what safeguards exist, together with a public monthly count of registry removals so that erosion is seen early.

## The other awareness problem: patients do not hear about the options

A less discussed failure concerns patients and not donors. As Chapter 5 documented, 15% of patients starting dialysis in a large national sample had not been informed about transplantation (Villani et al., 2024†), and clinician-reported discussions of transplant did not translate into waitlisting (Healio, 2018). The same gap applies, with more force, to emerging science. A patient who has never heard of machine perfusion or oxygen carriers cannot ask a center whether it uses them. I do not suggest that every patient needs a lecture on preservation technology. I suggest that a patient who asks should be answered plainly, and that patients should be given enough plain-language material to know what to ask.

This is the sense in which the Cold Ischemia Foundation understands its own work, and I hold it to the standard I have just stated. An awareness effort should be judged by what it changes. Did a patient ask a question they would not have asked? Did a care partner find a form they would not have found? Did a family understand a decision they would otherwise have faced in fog? If the answer is a count of page views, the work is unfinished.

## A design brief for the next campaign

Suppose a state donor registry or a national organization asked for a design brief for its next awareness campaign. Drawing on the experiments above, I would write six lines. *Name the behavior*: not "raise awareness" but "increase the number of registrations that are still active twelve months later." *Choose the moment*: place the message where registration can happen in the next minute, whether at a service counter, a license renewal, or a clinic waiting room. *Use the two messages that the evidence favors*: a motive that is altruistic and specific, and a promise that the act is easy. *Remove friction*: if registration takes more than a minute and a half, the campaign has already failed, whatever the posters say. *Include the transparency component*: a plain statement of who determines death, who may halt a donation, and how concerns are reported. And *pre-register the evaluation*: state before launch what will be counted, over what period, against what comparison, and publish the result whether or not it flatters the campaign.

The last line is the one most often declined, because an evaluation can embarrass. That is the reason to insist on it. A campaign that cannot survive a measurement was not a campaign. It was an event.

## Proposals

Pre-register the evaluation of every national campaign, naming in advance the behavior to be measured. Publish monthly registry registrations and removals. Replace the exhortation to "have the conversation" with the specific act that makes the conversation possible: a one-page plain-language map handed to every patient on the day they begin dialysis, and a navigation call that is free. And finally, measure what the campaign cost per additional registration that persisted for a year, because charity that does not know its own cost per outcome is only a mood.
