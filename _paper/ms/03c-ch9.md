# Chapter 9: The Interregnum
EPIGRAPH: "Quis custodiet ipsos custodes?" ("Who will guard the guards themselves?")
BY: Juvenal, Satires, VI.347–348 (c. 120 CE)

## Governance is also a process

Most of this book treats the transplant system as a pipeline that moves patients and organs. A second pipeline runs alongside it, and it moves decisions. A policy begins as an idea, passes through a committee, goes out for public comment, is voted on by a board, is approved by a federal agency, is implemented by hundreds of organizations, and is then, in theory, monitored for its effects. Each stage takes time, each handoff can lose information, and the cycle time of the whole determines how quickly the system can correct itself. If the pipeline of organs is measured so poorly, it is worth asking how well the pipeline of decisions is measured.

The past six months have made that pipeline unusually visible, because it has been under repair. The national transplant network, which writes the rules for allocation, has been in the midst of a multiyear reconstruction directed by the federal oversight agency. For the purposes of this chapter I call the present period an interregnum, a stretch in which the old arrangements have been dismantled and the new ones are not yet proven. What follows is a close reading of what the record shows.

Several items in the table deserve elaboration. The April decision to extend the terms of the board's president and vice president came with an admission. The board stated that recent disruptions, including contractor transitions and funding interruptions, had impaired its ability to move initiatives forward (HRSA, 2026b). In plain language, a body charged with improving the system reported that the machinery of the system itself had interrupted its work. Few organizations in any sector would publish such a sentence without discomfort, and I take its candor as a point in the agency's favor.

The oversight agency awarded a task order for policy and committee support, which covers policy development, committee operations, implementation, education, and post-implementation monitoring, and a separate task order for patient safety and compliance, awarded in May 2026 to a technology contractor in partnership with a research organization (HRSA, 2026c–2026f). A separate research organization holds a five-year contract for governance and transparency work, including the nomination and election of an independent board (American Institutes for Research, n.d.). The board approved a patient registration fee of $1,268 for fiscal year 2027, up from $1,036, a rise of about 22%, by a vote of 26 to 1 (HRSA, 2026c–2026f). To give a sense of scale, and only as an arithmetic illustration, if the new fee applied to each of the 50,481 patients added to the kidney list in 2024, it would total roughly $64.0 million, against roughly $52.3 million at the old rate (SRTR & OPTN, 2026a). That calculation is not an accounting of actual revenue, since the fee may not apply to every registration and covers organs other than the kidney.

## The reading: four patterns worth naming

*Key-person and contractor risk.* A governance structure that depends on one-year extensions of its leaders and on sequential contracts to vendors is fragile in the engineering sense. Reliability engineers call a component whose failure disables the whole a single point of failure. The April admission suggests that at least some points of failure were reached.

*The dispersion of control.* In September the board ended a standing committee that had overseen network operations, stating that technology and network oversight would continue through other committees and workgroups (HRSA, 2026c–2026f). Consolidation can be efficient. From the standpoint of a control plan, however, replacing a body whose single job is to watch a function with a diffuse responsibility shared among several bodies carries a risk. A function that everyone is partly responsible for may be, in practice, no one's. I do not claim that this has happened. I claim that it is the pattern a control engineer would watch.

*The value of daylight.* The board met in person in late September with its open sessions livestreamed (HRSA, 2026c–2026f). That is a meaningful transparency measure, and it should be recognized as one. A public meeting is the beginning of accountability and not its end, because the audience matters, and a livestream with an audience of a few dozen insiders is less a window than a skylight.

*The absence of independent observers.* The sources I reviewed for this chapter were overwhelmingly the agency's own monthly updates. I searched for independent press coverage or academic commentary on these decisions and did not find it. That does not mean it does not exist. It does mean that a patient trying to understand how the rules of allocation are changing must rely very heavily on the regulator's description of its own work. The remedy is for independent organizations, patient groups, universities, and foundations such as this one to attend, summarize, and challenge. A system watched only by its operators is a system that has not yet been audited.

## Two decisions I could not evaluate

I want to be candid about two decisions that bear directly on this book's themes and whose texts I could not retrieve. The first is the board's new set of requirements for donation after circulatory death, which the agency described as strengthening protections for potential donors and promoting greater consistency and accountability (HRSA, 2026c–2026f). Having devoted Chapter 10 to the events that provoked them, I would like to say whether the requirements are adequate. I cannot, because the agency's summary does not state their content. Readers who care should read the text, and I would urge the board to publish a plain-language explanation with each new requirement.

The second is the new ventilated patient form, which replaces two legacy reporting mechanisms with what the agency calls a standardized approach intended to improve data quality while reducing the burden of reporting (HRSA, 2026c–2026f). It is exactly the kind of change that might improve the measurement problems described in Chapter 3, or might merely reshuffle them. Whether it does will be visible only if the data it produces are published in a form that allows comparison with the past.

## A process-improvement question for the policy pipeline

A Lean practitioner would map the policy pipeline as a value stream and time every stage. How many days elapse between a problem being identified and a rule taking effect? How many proposals die in committee, and why? How long does public comment take, and how often does it change the outcome? What fraction of approved policies are evaluated afterward, and what happens when the evaluation is unfavorable? I have not found published answers to these questions, and I suspect the answers do not exist in compiled form. The stated scope of the policy-support task order includes post-implementation monitoring (HRSA, 2026c–2026f), and that is a hopeful sign. If it produces a published record of the cycle times and the results, the nation will have, for the first time, a measure of how quickly its transplant system learns.

## What the interregnum asks of patients

It is easy to treat governance as remote. A patient waiting for a kidney has more urgent matters. But the rules written in these committees decide who is offered an organ and in what order, how a potential donor's death is determined, what a transplant program is paid, and whether a fee is raised. A body that makes decisions of that weight deserves a public that is watching, and the public includes patients and care partners, who have more at stake than anyone. A modest practice would help: choose one of the board's open meetings each year, read the agenda beforehand, and listen. The system is easier to improve when the people it serves are in the room, even if the room is a livestream.
