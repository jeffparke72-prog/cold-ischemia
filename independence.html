About · What Independence Means

“Independent” is a word.
Independence is a structure.

Almost every patient advocacy organization says it speaks for patients. Far fewer can show that no one with a financial stake in patients’ care is paying for the microphone. This page shows the difference with public facts, so you can judge for yourself.

The Landscape

Industry money in patient advocacy is the rule, not the exception.

Patient advocacy groups are not required to report the money they receive from drug and device companies the way doctors’ payments are reported. Researchers and journalists had to dig to find it.

83%of the 104 largest U.S. patient advocacy organizations received money from drug, device, or biotech companies.NEJM, 2017 [1]
36%had at least one current industry executive on their governing board.NEJM, 2017 [1]
1 of 104stated explicitly that it does not accept industry money.NEJM, 2017 [1]
$116Mgiven by drugmakers to patient groups in a single year, across 594 groups.KFF Health News, 2018 [2]
Follow the Money

Two ways to build an advocacy organization.

Where the money comes from decides whose interests sit at the table, often without anyone saying a word.

The common model

Funding flows in from companies that profit from patients’ care. The organization’s voice flows out to patients and policymakers.

The Cold Ischemia Foundation model

No money flows in from anyone: no donations, gifts, grants, or sponsorships. Lived experience, evidence, and free tools flow out.

Ten Organizations, One Question

Who pays for the voice that speaks for patients?

Ten well-known U.S. advocacy organizations in kidney care, caregiving, and patient advocacy. Every one presents itself as a voice for patients or caregivers. Here is what is publicly documented about who funds them.

These organizations do real and often valuable work, and accepting industry funding is legal and common. We list them because their funding relationships are a matter of public record, from their own websites and disclosures or from published reporting, so you can weigh what each voice may be unable to say. Facts as of the dates of the sources cited below.

American Kidney Fund

Kidney · financial assistance

Major donations from the two largest dialysis companies, DaVita and Fresenius, reported at roughly 80% of the Fund’s donor base. A whistleblower lawsuit alleged those donations amounted to illegal kickbacks.

Dialysis-industry fundedSources [3]

National Kidney Foundation

Kidney · patient education & policy

Its 2026 corporate membership program lists drugmakers including Boehringer Ingelheim, Novo Nordisk, Vertex, AstraZeneca, Novartis, and GlaxoSmithKline. Dialysis provider Fresenius has publicized its fundraising support.

Pharma & dialysis supportedSources [4]

American Association of Kidney Patients

Kidney · patient advocacy

Its corporate membership program lists industry partners including Amgen, AstraZeneca, Baxter, Bayer, DaVita, and Fresenius Medical Care.

Industry corporate partnersSources [5]

Dialysis Patient Citizens

Kidney · dialysis patient advocacy

Founded in 2004 with an educational grant from dialysis provider DaVita, and originally named “DaVita Patient Citizens.” Its own fact sheet says the later name change better represents its “independent spirit of governance.”

Founded with dialysis fundingSources [6]

Caregiver Action Network

Family caregivers

Its corporate partners page lists drugmakers including Pfizer, Astellas, Eisai, Teva, Viatris, and Novartis as members of its Corporate Leadership Council.

Pharma corporate partnersSources [7]

National Alliance on Mental Illness

Mental health · families

A U.S. Senate Finance Committee inquiry found drugmakers gave the alliance nearly $23 million from 2006 to 2008, about three-quarters of its donations.

Pharma funded (documented)Sources [8]

American Diabetes Association

Diabetes · patients & research

Named by KFF Health News among the well-known recipients in its database of drugmaker donations to patient groups.

Pharma donations recordedSources [2]

American Heart Association

Heart health · research & policy

To its credit, it publishes its own disclosure: drug, biotech, and device companies provided about 5% of its total revenue in fiscal 2024–2025.

Pharma & device funded (disclosed)Sources [9]

Alliance for Patient Access

Patient access policy

The drug industry trade group PhRMA gave it more than $1.8 million from 2009 to 2016, according to Associated Press reporting. Its executive director led an affiliated Washington lobbying and PR firm.

Pharma trade-group fundedSources [10]

Patients Rising

Patient access advocacy

Described by KFF Health News as having multiple pharmaceutical sponsors while advocating for access to new drugs.

Pharma sponsoredSources [11]

Kidney · transplant · care partners

Accepts no funding, gifts, grants, sponsorships, or donations of any kind: not from drugmakers, dialysis companies, device makers, insurers, hospital systems, or individuals. There is no sponsor to protect, so every institution can be named.

Check Any Group Yourself

Don’t take anyone’s word for it. Including ours.

  1. Read their Form 990. Every U.S. nonprofit files one with the IRS. Search the organization by name on ProPublica Nonprofit Explorer to see revenue, top salaries, and grants.
  2. Find their sponsor page. Look for “corporate partners,” “corporate members,” or “supporters” on their website, often at the bottom of the page.
  3. Read the board list. Search each board member’s current and past employers.
  4. Ask what they won’t say. Have they ever publicly criticized a company that funds them? If not, ask why.
  5. Use our free tool. Our Advocacy Assessment walks you through these structural questions step by step.
