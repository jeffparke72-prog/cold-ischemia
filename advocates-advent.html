The Advocate's Advent

Twenty-four doors. Twenty-four traditions from around the world. One honest question behind every one of them: what does it actually mean to stand up for a patient when nobody is paying you to?

A letter about the word "advocate"

Every December the mailbox fills with glossy holiday cards from companies that bill patients all year. They wish you peace and joy in a font that cost more than your copay, and they sign it from "your care team." This calendar is not that card.

Somewhere along the way, "patient advocate" became a title you could buy. Industry-funded groups hand out lanyards. Paid "ambassadors" smile in commercials. Survivors are flown to Capitol Hill carrying talking points drafted by the same people who profit from the illness. Some of them mean well. They are still on someone's payroll, and a payroll always has an opinion.

A true advocate is the person who picks up the phone when there is no grant attached to the call. Who reads the forty-page form so you do not sign it blind. Who walks into a room full of executives and says the thing that gets them uninvited next year, then shows up again anyway. We do this work for free, funded only by people who choose to give and by the work we produce ourselves, and we would not trade that independence for any sponsor's check.

For twenty-four days, one door opens each morning. Behind each one is a tradition from somewhere on this planet: Christian, Jewish, Muslim, Buddhist, Sikh, Hindu, Persian, Filipino, Mexican, Lakota, Chinese, Scandinavian, African and African American, Norse, Roman, and more. Every one of them understood something about courage, dignity, and showing up for the vulnerable long before anyone printed a brochure about "patient engagement." We chose them deliberately. Kidney disease does not check anyone's faith, language, or zip code before it moves into a home, and neither do we. Every belief and every perspective that brings someone to the bedside of another person is welcome at this table.

Open one a day. Turn the card over. Then do the part that matters most: send it to someone. A care partner running on fumes. A nurse who still remembers why she started. A neighbor three years into the wait. These cards were made to be passed hand to hand, and then passed again.

Jeff A. ParkeFounder & Executive Director · Cold Ischemia Foundation

Why many traditions

Advocacy that only speaks one cultural language leaves people behind. A patient who prays five times a day, a family who keeps Shabbat, and a grandmother who lights a candle to the Virgin all deserve care that respects them. Honoring their traditions is where respect starts.

Why rights on every card

Inspiration without a tool is just a nice feeling. Each card ends with something you can do this week and a right you already hold, so the lesson does not evaporate when the holiday ends.

Why share it

Nobody learns their rights from the companies that benefit when they don't. They learn them from each other. Every share puts this information in front of someone the system would rather keep uninformed.

Pass it on. Then pass it on again.

This calendar was built to travel. Send it to your family group chat, your support group, your church, mosque, temple, or gurdwara, the nurses' station, or the one person you know who is carrying more than anyone realizes.
