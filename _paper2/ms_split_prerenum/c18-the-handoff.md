# Chapter 18: The Handoff
EPIGRAPH: "Rejoice, O young man, in thy youth; and let thy heart cheer thee in the days of thy youth."
BY: Ecclesiastes 11:9 (King James Version, 1611)

Picture a sixteen-year-old in a clinic room, sitting on the paper-covered table with her sneakers dangling. She has had her kidney since she was nine. She has been in this clinic so long that the nurses have watched her grow, and the children's hospital has a mural on the wall that she painted a corner of when she was twelve. She knows her medication names the way she knows her phone number. And she is, this month, being told that at eighteen she will move to the adult clinic, across town, with different doctors, in a building with no mural.

She does not say anything. She nods. She will pay attention to the new place, she tells herself. But she is sixteen, and she has a boyfriend, and a part-time job, and a way of forgetting what she would rather not remember, and the pills are the one thing in her life she has always been told she must not forget.

The scene is a composite. The danger it describes is among the best documented in transplant medicine, and among the least discussed outside it.

## The age at which kidneys are lost

A large analysis of United States registry data on first kidney transplants from 1987 to 2010, published in JAMA Internal Medicine in 2013, found that recipients aged 14 to 16 had the highest risk of graft loss of any age group, starting a year after transplant and widening at three, five, and ten years. Black adolescents were at disproportionate risk. The authors recommended structured transition programs (Source Notes S53). The data are old now, ending in 2010, but the pattern has been confirmed in other places. A study of national data in England found that young adults lose their transplants and return to dialysis at a far higher rate than people of other ages, and linked this to missed medication and missed appointments (Source Notes S53).

The mechanism is not a mystery, though it is easy to moralize about it. Teenagers forget things, push against authority, want to be like their friends, and sometimes stop taking drugs that make them feel unlike their friends. The drugs carry side effects that matter when you are fifteen: changes in appearance, in weight, in hair. And the transplant itself, being invisible and not painful, is easy to forget until it fails.

## A question about systems, not about teenagers

What interests me is where the risk concentrates: not in the teen years as such but at the point when care changes hands. It is the handoff. In pediatric centers a family is surrounded by a team that knows everyone's name and checks in by phone. In adult centers, a young person is expected to manage her own appointments, her own refills, and her own relationship with a physician she has met twice. In the space between those worlds, a kidney can be lost.

The obvious remedy, a structured program that eases the transition, has been tested, and the result humbles anyone who thought the problem simple. A randomized trial in Germany, called TransNephro, enrolled 102 adolescents and young adults and assigned them to a structured program, which included a central case manager, a communication app, and joint transition rounds for a year before and after transfer, or to standard care. The primary outcome was adherence, measured by the variability of drug levels in the blood. There was no difference between the groups (Source Notes S54). Graft survival was a secondary outcome, and the results I could reach did not report it. A single-center program at Stanford that begins preparing patients for transition at age twelve reported preserving low rates of graft loss while transferring about twice as many patients, though that was a before-and-after comparison and is weaker evidence (Source Notes S54).

## The shortage behind the handoff

There is a second, less visible problem at the handoff. Pediatric nephrology is a small and fragile specialty. A report in the trade press said that between 2014 and 2022 the fellowship fill rate in pediatric nephrology was 66 percent, the lowest of all fifteen pediatric subspecialties (The pediatric nephrology workforce is in crisis, 2025). When there are few pediatric kidney specialists, each handoff is also a handoff between scarce hands.

## What would a gentler handoff look like?

I do not know. The trial in Germany did not find that a more intensive program improved adherence, and I would not claim that any specific program works. But the right response to a negative result is not to give up. It is to ask what was missing. The program tested was structured and professional. What it may not have supplied is the thing that Chapter 4 found at the heart of the best interventions: the near people. A teenager who has a friend, an older cousin, a young adult who has been through the same thing, someone who is not a clinician and not a parent, might behave differently from one who has a case manager. That is a hypothesis, and not a finding, and it deserves a trial.

Ecclesiastes tells the young man to rejoice in his youth. I do not read it as an instruction to be careless. I read it as a recognition that a young person is entitled to a life that is not defined by her disease, and that a system that asks her to carry her kidney alone, at precisely the age when she most wants to be carried, is asking something that few adults could manage. A better handoff would not ask her to be mature earlier than she is. It would let her be sixteen, and keep her kidney anyway.
