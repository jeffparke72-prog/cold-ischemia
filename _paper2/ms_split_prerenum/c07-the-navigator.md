# Chapter 7: The Navigator
EPIGRAPH: "Midway upon the journey of our life / I found myself within a forest dark, / For the straightforward pathway had been lost."
BY: Dante Alighieri, The Divine Comedy, Inferno, Canto I (Longfellow translation, 1867)

Picture a woman of fifty-six who has just been told that she may be a candidate for a transplant. The sentence arrives in a conversation lasting nine minutes, and she walks out of the clinic holding a folder. In the folder are six forms, a list of tests, three phone numbers, and a page of instructions written in the dialect of people who work in hospitals. She is told she needs a colonoscopy, a dental clearance, a cardiac stress test, a mammogram, and a social-work evaluation. None of these appointments can be made by the people who requested them. All of them must be completed, in some order, before a committee will consider her.

She is not unintelligent. She kept a small business afloat for twenty years. But she is tired, she lives forty minutes from the center, she works part-time, and the folder sits on the kitchen table, and then on the counter, and then in a drawer.

This is the stretch of the journey that the registries cannot see, because it happens before anyone appears on a list. It is a forest in the poet's sense: the straightforward pathway has been lost. And a number of people have tried to send in guides.

## The navigator, tested

The most natural idea is a person whose job is to walk beside the patient through the forest. At Emory University, investigators led by a transplant researcher named Rachel Patzer put that idea to a randomized test. They enrolled 401 patients referred for transplant evaluation and assigned half to a trained navigator, who followed each patient from referral to the decision about waitlisting.

The result was not what the idea's admirers hoped. Overall, waitlisting was not significantly different between the navigated patients, 32 percent, and the controls, 26 percent, and the time from referral to waitlisting was actually 126 days longer for the navigated group. The authors suggested a gentler reading: the navigated patients may have been those with the greatest needs, who required more time for testing and follow-up. In one subgroup, a social-work navigator who helped patients complete tests and attend appointments seemed to increase waitlisting among those who took more than 500 days to get through the process (Source Notes S47).

I find that result more instructive than a victory would have been. It says that a human guide, however well trained, is not a magic key, and that the difficulty of the forest may exceed what one person can supply. It also says something about trial design: a navigator may bring forward the patients who would otherwise have vanished, and in doing so lengthen the average time, which looks like failure and may be the opposite.

## Starting at the dialysis unit instead

A different approach asked a different question. Instead of guiding patients after referral, why not change what happens in the dialysis unit, where most of them spend three afternoons a week? The study called RaDIANT randomized 134 dialysis facilities in Georgia, serving more than 9,000 patients, to receive or not receive an education and engagement program aimed at facility leaders, staff, and patients. Its primary outcome was referral for transplant evaluation within a year. The program substantially improved referral and narrowed racial disparities in it, though the investigators noted that longer follow-up would be needed to know whether the gains carried through to the waiting list. The intervention was later extended to North Carolina and South Carolina (Source Notes S47).

Notice the shape of the success. It did not rely on a single heroic person. It changed an environment: the facility's culture of talking about transplant, the staff's knowledge, the patients' expectations. That fits the pattern I found in Chapter 4, where the most promising programs treated the people near a patient as the unit of change.

## Someone who has been there

A third idea is peer mentorship: pairing a patient with someone who has walked the same road. The appeal is obvious. A person who has had a transplant can say things no professional can say. The evidence, however, is mixed. A hospital-based peer mentoring program for transplant candidates found no significant differences in time to listing or quality of life between those who had mentors and those who did not, and the authors noted that willingness to accept a mentor varied by race (Source Notes S48). A pragmatic randomized trial of 200 hemodialysis patients, reported in the Journal of the American Society of Nephrology in April 2025, measured unplanned hospitalizations and emergency visits over three months; the mentored group's adjusted incidence rate ratio was 0.60 against 0.85 for usual care, but the overall result did not reach statistical significance, and benefit appeared in certain subgroups (Source Notes S48). A feasibility study of 57 patients at a dialysis provider and a trial pairing house-call transplant education with peer mentorship are registered, and I found no outcomes for either.

## What this teaches

If you have a tidy mind, this chapter will frustrate you. Navigators did not clearly work, mentors did not clearly work, and the best-supported approach was an educational program that changed the culture of dialysis units. A hasty reader might conclude that the human touch is overrated. I draw nearly the opposite lesson.

What the results suggest is that the human touch is not a single thing. A guide who accompanies a patient through paperwork, a mentor who shares an experience, and a clinic that changes how it speaks are all different interventions, with different mechanisms and different populations, and that none of them has yet been shown to be the key does not mean that none will. It means that this field has not yet done the patient work of learning what kind of help helps whom. I would want these tested again, with larger samples, with outcomes that include what patients themselves value, and with willingness to publish the disappointments, as Emory's team did.

The woman with the folder deserves more than a folder. She deserves a first conversation that is longer than nine minutes, a single list instead of six, someone who can make the appointments she cannot, a clinic that is not forty minutes away, and, if she wishes it, a person who has been through the forest and is willing to say what the trees look like. Whether any of that will work is a question we can answer. Whether it is worth asking is not.
