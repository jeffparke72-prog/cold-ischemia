# Prologue: Four Hours
EPIGRAPH: "I have discovered that all the unhappiness of men arises from one single fact, that they cannot stay quietly in their own chamber."
BY: Blaise Pascal, Pensées (Trotter translation, 1908)

Pascal believed the trouble with people was that they could not sit still. He never met anyone whose treatment required it.

Picture a room with a row of recliners and a window nobody looks out of. The chairs are vinyl, the color of weak tea. Each one faces a machine the size of a bar refrigerator, and each machine hums at a slightly different pitch, so the room has a kind of chord in it. A television is bolted high in a corner, tuned to a program no one chose. It is one o'clock on a Tuesday. Nobody here is sick in the way the movies show sickness. They are reading, dozing, doing crosswords, answering email, watching their own blood travel in a loop through clear tubing and come back clean. They are, for the next four hours, exactly where they are supposed to be.

That scene is a composite, built from what patients, nurses, and researchers have described. No single person stands in it. I begin with it because I have come to believe that the most important fact about kidney failure is also the one least often counted. It is not the number of people waiting for an organ, though that number is large. It is not the cost, though the cost is larger. It is the hours.

Let me do the arithmetic, slowly, because slowness is the point. A common schedule for in-center hemodialysis is three sessions a week of about four hours each. Over fifty-two weeks that is 156 sessions and 624 hours in the chair. Now add what comes afterward. In an online survey of 359 patients who dialyze in centers, the median time to recover from a session was three hours, and four in ten said it took four hours or longer. The most common complaint was feeling washed out, reported by 62 percent, followed by cramps at 44 percent and symptoms of low blood pressure at 42 percent (Source Notes S1). Three hours of recovery across 156 sessions is another 468 hours.

Together, 1,092 hours. A full-time job occupies about 2,080 hours in a year. By this reckoning, kidney failure treated in a center takes something like half of a full-time job, and the person holding it is not paid, cannot quit, and is often too tired to do any other job at all. I need to be honest about the limits of that figure. The schedule is typical and not universal, the recovery number comes from one modest survey, and recovery time overlaps with sleep and rest in ways no survey can untangle. Travel to and from the clinic is not counted at all. The point is not that the sum is exact. The point is that it is the right kind of sum, and that almost nobody does it.

I have lived long enough inside this world, in more than one role, to know the sound that rooms like this make, and the sound that a family makes when it is doing a sum like this one in the parking lot. I will not tell you my own story. It belongs to me and to the few people who carried me through it. What I can offer instead is a way of looking, which is to ask of every part of the American kidney ecosystem a question so plain it sounds naive. What is this doing to people's time?

The question has an old pedigree. The psalmist asked God to teach us to number our days. The Stoics made an entire discipline of the observation that life is not short so much as spent carelessly. In our era the question has been reduced to productivity, which is a shrunken version of it. But in a dialysis chair, the old question returns at full size. If you were given a certain number of afternoons, what would you want them for? And what do the rest of us owe a person whose afternoons we have, by accident of biology, quietly claimed?

This book is my attempt to take that question seriously, in two registers at once. One is existential, in the plain sense of the word: it concerns finitude, choice, and the strange dignity of deciding how to spend a limited life. The other is conservative, in the older sense of the word: it concerns what is worth preserving, the humility to ask why a thing stands before tearing it down, and a stubborn faith in the small institutions, a family, a congregation, a town's clinic, a hospital's coordinator, that do most of the real work of caring while the grand systems get the credit.

You will find that the chapters do not follow one another like a case being made. They are more like rooms in a house, each with a different window. One looks onto the dialysis afternoon. One looks onto the quiet decision some people make to decline dialysis altogether. One looks onto the person who drives, the person who gives, the family in a hallway, the surgeon's telephone at three in the morning, the machines that may someday set the afternoons free. I have tried to bring current evidence into every room, drawn from the last year wherever it exists, and to say plainly where the evidence ends.

I will make you one promise and one request. The promise is that I will not use a statistic to frighten you when a sentence will do, and I will not use a story to persuade you of something the data do not support. The request is that you read slowly. The people in these chairs have been taught patience whether they wanted the lesson or not. We might at least match it.
