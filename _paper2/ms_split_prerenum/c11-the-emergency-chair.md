# Chapter 11: The Emergency Chair
EPIGRAPH: "I will use treatment to help the sick according to my ability and judgment, but never with a view to injury and wrong-doing."
BY: The Hippocratic Oath (Jones translation, 1923)

Picture a man of thirty-eight who does not have a telephone number he can give a doctor. He came to the United States as a young adult and has worked since then in jobs that do not offer insurance. A year ago his kidneys began to fail, and he did not know, because he did not go to a doctor, because doctors cost money he did not have. Now he is in an emergency room, short of breath, his potassium dangerously high. They will dialyze him tonight. They will send him home in a day or two, and he will not be offered a chair again until he becomes sick enough to qualify.

This is a composite, but the arrangement it describes is real, and it is one of the least discussed corners of American kidney care. In some states, people without legal immigration status who have kidney failure can receive dialysis only when they arrive at an emergency department critically ill. It is called emergency-only hemodialysis.

I want to approach it with care, because the subject is politically charged and I do not intend to settle any argument about immigration. My subject is narrower and, I think, one on which people of very different convictions can agree: how well this arrangement works as medicine, and what it costs.

## What the comparison shows

The most careful comparison I found followed 211 undocumented patients who began dialysis between 2007 and 2014 at three hospitals. Two of them, in Denver and Houston, offered emergency-only dialysis. The third, in San Francisco, offered standard scheduled dialysis. After adjustment, patients receiving emergency-only treatment had a higher risk of death, with a three-year hazard ratio of 4.96 whose interval was wide enough to include no difference (0.93 to 26.45), and a five-year hazard ratio of 14.13, with an interval wider still (1.24 to 161). They also spent about ten times as many days in acute care as expected (Source Notes S50). The study is observational and the patients differed in ways that adjustment could not fully remove: those who got standard care were more likely to start with a fistula or graft and had better blood counts. The intervals show how little the data can say about the size of the difference. They do not erase its direction.

A second cohort of 181 adults compared scheduled dialysis with emergency-only dialysis. One-year mortality was 3 percent for scheduled treatment and 17 percent for emergency-only. Emergency-only patients made six more emergency-department visits per month, had 1.5 more hospitalizations, and spent ten more days in the hospital over six months, and their care cost $5,768 more per month (Source Notes S51).

## What Colorado changed

In 2019, Colorado decided to count kidney failure as a qualifying condition for emergency Medicaid, which extended coverage to scheduled outpatient dialysis three times a week for those who qualify. A published account reports that before the change, the state's emergency Medicaid dialysis spending for this population was close to $20,000 a patient per month in 2017 and 2018, and that after the change, the average monthly expenditure per patient was $5,574 even as the number of patients served rose to about 135 a month (Source Notes S52). That account comes from state data reported in a secondary publication, and the totals it gives are not easy to reconcile with one another, so I rely only on the direction: scheduled care cost less per patient than emergency care.

## An argument from prudence

I make no claim about who deserves what. I make a claim about foresight. The conservative tradition prizes prudence, which means attending to consequences, and the evidence I have described points to an arrangement that is deadlier and, per patient, more expensive than the alternative. A system that waits until a person is nearly dead to give him the treatment that would have kept him well has chosen the costliest door.

There is also a plain moral intuition that runs under the practical one, and it is older than any health policy. The oath that physicians have sworn for twenty-five centuries binds them to use treatment for the benefit of the sick. It does not say which sick people. A profession that sends a man home after a rescue knowing he will return in worse condition has not quite honored its oath, though it may have obeyed its rules. Many nephrologists feel this tension acutely. In the qualitative literature I read, some describe the moral distress of providing emergency-only care as among the hardest parts of their work.

## Not the whole story

I should say what I do not know. I found no 2025 study of emergency-only dialysis, and the main cohort studies are from 2018 and rely on data that are now a decade old. I found no outcome data from Texas beyond the early cohort. Policies have shifted in some states since, and some of them may have changed the picture. The evidence is not unanimous about the size of the differences, and the largest estimates carry wide uncertainty.

But the shape is consistent, and it fits what the chapters on time and distance have shown. People who cannot reach the chair on a schedule are not saved money. They are charged later, in worse form, and at a higher price.

I think of the man in the emergency room. He does not know about hazard ratios. He knows that the last time, he was breathless, and that they helped, and that he will be told to come back when it gets bad again. He is waiting in a different kind of line, one whose only way forward is to become sicker. If there is a principle of kidney care in this book, it is that no one should have to earn a chair by nearly dying.
