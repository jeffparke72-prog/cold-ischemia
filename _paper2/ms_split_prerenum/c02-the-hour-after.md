# Chapter 2: The Hour After
EPIGRAPH: "It is vain for you to rise up early, to sit up late, to eat the bread of sorrows: for so he giveth his beloved sleep."
BY: Psalm 127:2 (King James Version, 1611)

Picture the first hour after a dialysis session, which is the hour no one writes down. Ruth is in the passenger seat with a warm blanket over her knees and a small paper cup of juice she did not want. The nurse has pressed gauze to her arm and asked whether she is all right, and she has said yes, because that is the answer that gets you out the door. The light outside is the color of an old photograph. She feels, as she always does, like a cloth that has been wrung out and hung up wet.

She will not describe it that way to her doctor. Doctors ask about blood pressure, about the numbers on the printout, about whether she is "tolerating treatment," and she is, in the narrow sense. She is not being rushed to a hospital. She is not cramping so badly she needs the session cut short. She is merely a person who has spent four hours having her blood cleaned and has been returned to her own life with the batteries drained.

Patients have a word for this and clinicians, until recently, mostly did not. They say they are washed out.

## A symptom with a clock

When researchers finally asked how long the hour after lasts, the answers were longer than anyone had put on a chart. In the online survey of 359 in-center patients described in the Prologue, the median time to recover from a session was three hours, with a range from none to a full day, and 40 percent of patients said it took four hours or longer. Fatigue, or feeling washed out, was the most common symptom at 62 percent, followed by cramps at 44 percent and the signs of low blood pressure at 42 percent (Source Notes S1). A general review puts the typical median at two to four hours and says about a quarter of patients report recovery taking more than six (Source Notes S40).

What interests me is how little that figure resembles any measure the system tracks. A recovery time of three hours is not reported on a quality dashboard. It is not required by a payment model. It does not appear in the federal data. Yet the patients who took part in the international survey described in Chapter 1 put exactly this experience, feeling washed out after dialysis, on the list of things that mattered more to them than they did to the professionals.

## What shortens it

If the hour after is a real harm, the obvious question is whether anyone has found a way to shorten it. The evidence is thinner than the question deserves, but it is not empty.

A French cohort study of 536 patients found that 15 percent said the washed-out feeling lasted more than twelve hours. Of all the features of the dialysis prescription the researchers examined, only two were associated with longer recovery: a bigger drop in the blood's sodium during the session, and a lower frequency of treatment, two sessions a week against three or more (Source Notes S40). It is an observational finding and cannot prove that adding a session would shorten the recovery, but it points in a direction.

Another lever is the temperature of the dialysis fluid. In a 2024 quasi-experimental study of 92 patients in Pakistan, fluid at 36 degrees Celsius was associated with less frequent and shorter post-dialysis fatigue than fluid at 37 degrees, without a difference in low blood pressure (Source Notes S41). An older non-randomized crossover study of 50 patients compared 35 and 37 degrees, and 76 percent reported feeling more energetic with the cooler fluid (Source Notes S41). A study that changed patients' prescriptions reported that median recovery time fell from 210 minutes to 60 minutes after six months and 105 minutes after twelve (Source Notes S42). Registered trials of cooler fluid exist, including one in Iran with 46 patients and one in Turkey, and I found no published results from either.

The best-known trials of more frequent dialysis asked different questions. The FHN Daily Trial randomized 245 patients to dialysis six times a week or three, and found that frequent dialysis significantly reduced the mass of the heart's left ventricle by 13.1 grams, with an interval of 5.0 to 21.3 (Source Notes S43). The companion nocturnal trial showed similar trends that did not reach statistical significance, though patients on nocturnal dialysis had better control of blood phosphate and blood pressure. Neither trial, as far as I could determine, made recovery time or fatigue its main outcome.

## The gap

That last observation is the point of this chapter. I could not find a randomized trial in which the length of the hour after, or the fatigue that fills it, was the primary outcome of changing the schedule. The things that have been tested are the things that fit the traditional vocabulary of nephrology: heart mass, phosphate, blood pressure, survival. The thing that patients named, in the rare moments they were asked, has hardly been tested at all.

I do not think this is an accident or a scandal. It is an ordinary consequence of what a field decides to count. Trials chase outcomes that are easy to measure and that regulators accept, and recovery time is neither. But it means that a person like Ruth, wondering whether a cooler bath or a longer session or a home machine might give her back her evenings, has almost no evidence to consult. She is being asked to choose in the dark about the one outcome she cares about most.

## A vital sign for the evening

There is a small, almost embarrassingly simple remedy. At every visit, a nurse or doctor could ask a patient the question the survey asked: how long does it take you to feel like yourself after treatment? And then write down the answer, in hours, in the same place as the blood pressure. A recovery time recorded each month would reveal at once whether a change in prescription, a different fluid temperature, or a move to home treatment made any difference. It would turn an invisible symptom into a variable, which is the first step toward doing something about it.

This is, in the language of the older conservative tradition, an argument from attention. The small, unglamorous things that make daily life bearable are exactly the things that grand systems overlook, and exactly what wise institutions take care to notice. The hour after is the hour in which a person returns from the machine to the world. A system that wished to treat her as a whole person would want to know how that return goes.
