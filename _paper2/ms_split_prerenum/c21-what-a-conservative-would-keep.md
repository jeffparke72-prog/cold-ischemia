# Chapter 21: What a Conservative Would Keep
EPIGRAPH: "If you don't see the use of it, I certainly won't let you clear it away. Go away and think. Then, when you can come back and tell me that you do see the use of it, I may allow you to destroy it."
BY: G. K. Chesterton, The Thing (1929)

I have called this book's second register conservative, and I owe the reader an account of what I mean, because the word has drifted a long way from its roots. A conservative, in the tradition I have in mind, is not someone who opposes change. Burke insisted that a state without the means of some change is without the means of its own conservation. A conservative is someone who asks, before changing a thing, what it is for.

Seen that way, the American kidney ecosystem looks different than it does to either its defenders or its critics. It is an assembly of institutions, most of them remarkable, some of them decaying, a few of them invisible until they vanish. The conservative's first duty is to inventory what is worth keeping.

## What I would keep

**The gift.** The entire edifice rests on the free decision of families to give, and of living people to part with a piece of themselves. Chesterton, in his book *Orthodoxy*, described tradition as giving a vote to the most obscure of all classes, our ancestors, and called it the democracy of the dead (Chesterton, 1908). In transplantation the dead do vote, in a literal way. Every deceased donor is a person whose last act was a decision, honored by a family. Any reform that makes that decision feel less safe or less honored has spent capital that cannot easily be earned back. That is the lesson of Chapter 14.

**The near.** The hospital coordinator who knows the unit. The rural dialysis unit that holds a county in place. The nurse who knows the names of a patient's children. The friend trained to ask on someone's behalf. Wherever I found success in this book, it came from institutions small enough to be recognized. A policy that cannot see them will tend to wear them down.

**The household.** The kitchen table, the care partner, the home machine. Policy should treat the family as the central unit of care it already is, and stop pretending that unpaid labor is free.

**The right to say no.** The conservative tradition honors conscience and the individual's authority over her own life. A system that cannot offer, and honestly describe, the choice to forgo dialysis has failed to honor it.

## What I would change

A conservative is not a defender of everything. The things I would change are mostly the things nobody can see.

*Count the hours.* Put the patient's time, travel, recovery, and care partner strain on the dashboard next to the metrics already there. Chapters 1, 8, and 20.

*Say it early.* Tell people about kidney disease while choices are still wide, and about every option, including transplantation, home therapy, and conservative care. Chapters 5 and 6.

*End the receipt.* No living donor should pay to give. Chapter 13.

*Keep the unit open.* Treat the closure of a local dialysis unit as a failure to be investigated and not an outcome to be shrugged at. Chapter 9.

*Give the decider a map.* Standard information, a travel-time projection, and a second look for the kidney declined again and again. Chapter 15.

*Count what is promised.* Require independent replication of what manufacturers announce, and report what each new machine gives back in time. Chapter 16.

*Be honest about the graft.* Tell recipients how long a kidney lasts, plan for the second chapter, and measure whether the drugs are being skipped for cost. Chapters 17 and 19.

*Make the family's hallway legible.* Explain who determines death, who can halt a donation, and who will call in a month. Chapter 14.

## Three tests for any new rule

Chesterton's parable suggests a way of questioning proposals, mine included, before adopting them. I offer three tests, in the form of questions that anyone can ask.

*What is the fence?* Before changing a practice, can its defenders and its critics both say what it was meant to do? If no one can, the first task is to find out. Many of the rules in transplantation were written to prevent specific harms, and the harms are not always visible to the people who inherit the rules.

*Who is nearest?* Is the decision being made by the person or the institution closest to those affected, or has it been lifted to a distance without a reason? The coordinator in the Spanish hospital, the friend in the champion program, the nurse in the rural unit, and the family in the hallway are each the nearest competent party. A rule that overrides them should say why.

*What does it cost the near?* Every reform has a price, and the price often falls on the small institutions that were never consulted: a family that will now have to do the paperwork, a clinic that will lose its late shift, a coordinator who will have a new form to fill. A reform that lowers the cost of the grand design by raising the cost of the household has not saved anything. It has moved the bill.

None of these tests prevents change. They discipline it, which is all that the conservative tradition at its best has ever asked.
## What would change my mind

A person who argues for reform should say what would make her retreat. If patients, asked what they want measured, named things I have not imagined, I would change the dashboard to match. If a well-designed trial showed that a technology I have praised gives back no time, I would say so. If a pilot of home dialysis showed that it merely moved burden onto families who could not bear it, I would withdraw my enthusiasm for it. And if the people who actually sit in the chairs told me that the whole frame of this book, the arithmetic of hours, misses what they need, I would ask them to tell me what it is.

## The last conservative argument

Burke's phrase about the little platoon is often taken to mean that the small is good and the large is bad. I think it means something more exact. The large things in life, a nation, a health system, a profession, are made of small things, and they are only as trustworthy as the small things are. The kidney ecosystem is not broken because it lacks a grand design. It is strained because the small things it depends on, the household, the coordinator, the unit, the volunteer, the family in the hallway, have been asked to carry more than they were ever built to carry, with less recognition than they deserve.

The fence, in Chesterton's story, is still standing. The wise reformer goes away and thinks, and when she comes back she can say what the fence is for. What is it for? It is for keeping the people on the road from harm. That is a good thing for a fence to be for. Our task is to make sure it still does.
