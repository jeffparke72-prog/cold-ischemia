# Chapter 12: The Long Chain
EPIGRAPH: "The only gift is a portion of thyself."
BY: Ralph Waldo Emerson, "Gifts," Essays: Second Series (1844)

Picture two couples who have never met. In the first, a husband needs a kidney and his wife wants to give him one, but their blood types do not match. In the second, the same story, the same love, the same incompatibility, in a different state. Each pair is stuck. Each has a willing donor who cannot help the person she most wants to help.

Then someone does something clever. The first wife's kidney goes to the second husband. The second wife's kidney goes to the first husband. Two transplants happen where none could. Nobody has been asked to give to a stranger, and everyone has, without quite noticing.

That is the simplest form of kidney paired donation, and it has become one of the quiet marvels of modern medicine. It is also a puzzle with an almost unbearably human structure, because the longer you let it run, the more it begins to resemble something older than medicine.

## The chain that starts with a stranger

Now add a person who walks in with no one in particular to help. A non-directed donor, sometimes called an altruistic donor, gives a kidney to a stranger. That first gift can unlock a pair that had been stuck, whose donor then gives to the next pair, whose donor gives to the next, and on it goes, each link a donor who gives only because someone else gave first. The chain can end by sending its last kidney to a person on the deceased-donor waiting list, so that nobody is left with a debt unpaid.

In 2009, the New England Journal of Medicine described a ten-transplant chain begun by a single altruistic donor in July 2007, coordinated over eight months by two registries, across six hospitals in five states. The authors reported something touching in its plainness: no one reneged (Rees et al., 2009). Every donor, having seen her loved one saved, went through with her own operation, though she had nothing left to gain. Think about that. The chain depends on each link doing a hard thing after the incentive is gone.

Chains have grown longer since. The National Kidney Registry has described one that began with a non-directed donor and ran across 17 hospitals in 11 states over four months, ending with a thirtieth transplant, a record at the time (Source Notes S23). A chain at the University of Alabama at Birmingham that has been reported as the longest at a single center has been described in different announcements as 74 donors and 74 recipients and as reaching 100 or more recipients (Nation's longest single-site kidney chain reaches 100, n.d.; Source Notes S24). The sources disagree on the current total because they count different things, completed versus ongoing chains, single-center versus multicenter, transplants versus surgeries. I will not pretend to a precision the record lacks.

## Scale

What began as a curiosity is now a meaningful share of the field. The National Kidney Registry announced its 10,000th living-donor transplant, with 1,744 facilitated in 2024 alone, and projected that it would account for roughly 30 percent of all living-donor kidney transplants in the United States in 2025. In one recent month, its 104 member centers together facilitated a record 179 transplants, surpassing the 157 set in July 2024 (National Kidney Registry facilitates 10,000th living donor kidney transplant, n.d.). These are the organization's own figures, and they should be read as such, but the trend is not in doubt.

## Why bigger pools help

There is a piece of logic underneath all of this that does not depend on any particular statistic. A pair that cannot match each other has a limited chance of matching with a small group of other pairs. Add more pairs and the chance rises, not by a little but by a lot, because the number of possible combinations grows much faster than the number of participants. This is the reason that a national registry can do what a single hospital cannot, and the reason that the National Kidney Registry's growth matters. Every added pair is a possible key to a lock that someone else has been waiting to open. The advantage compounds, which is why a field built on voluntary gifts can behave, in this one corner, like a network whose value rises with each person who joins.

It is also the reason that every refusal to travel has a cost. A donor who cannot or will not go to another center, a hospital that does not participate, a patient who is never told that this option exists, each subtracts from the pool, and the loss is borne by strangers they will never meet. The conservative instinct to keep things local is right in a great many parts of this book. Here it meets its limit, and the chain's strength comes from asking local institutions to participate in something larger than themselves.

## The promise nobody enforces

One detail of the 2009 chain stays with me more than the transplant counts. Nobody reneged. There is no contract that compels a donor to go through with surgery after her own relative has received a kidney, and in a chain of a few dozen links, the odds that some link will break are not small. The registry's rules can encourage and its coordinators can encourage, but ultimately the chain holds because people keep their word when no one can make them. If you wanted a single example of what a free society runs on, you could do worse. We tend to describe such societies in terms of rights and markets. They also run on promises kept in the dark.
## What the chain teaches

I find three lessons in this machinery, and none of them is technical.

The first is that generosity can be organized without being cheapened. The registry's software matches blood types and antibodies, but the heart of the thing is a promise kept by strangers. Emerson wrote that "the only gift is a portion of thyself," and he meant that a gift worthy of the name costs the giver something of her own. A living kidney donor gives a literal portion of herself. A system that honors that must resist treating her as a supply.

The second is that reciprocity, at its best, travels forward and not back. The old moral economy of favor owed and favor repaid was a dyad. The chain makes it a line. You cannot repay the person who gave to your husband, because she gave because someone gave before her. You can only pass it on, and the person you pass it to cannot repay you either. A line of debts that nobody collects is the clearest definition of grace I know.

The third is a conservative one. The chain is built from voluntary acts, and its strength comes from that. Remove the voluntariness, by compulsion or by bribery, and something essential would be lost, though reasonable people differ about where the line falls, as the next chapter will show. What the chain needs from policy is not control but care: reliable logistics, clear information, protection against financial harm to the donors, and a refusal to let the whole thing be turned into a marketplace.
