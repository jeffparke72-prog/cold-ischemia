# Chapter 10: The Storm
EPIGRAPH: "A prudent man foreseeth the evil, and hideth himself: but the simple pass on, and are punished."
BY: Proverbs 22:3 (King James Version, 1611)

Picture a house on the Gulf Coast in late August. A storm has been named, tracked, argued about on television, and has swerved. A woman who is on dialysis three times a week looks at her calendar and does what she has trained herself to do. She counts forward. Her next session is Thursday. The storm will arrive Wednesday night. The clinic has called, a recorded voice announcing that it will close if the power fails. She has a packet in a folder, a diet sheet for emergencies, a bag by the door. She also has a daughter two states away who has told her, firmly, to come.

What the storm threatens is not only the roof. A dialysis machine needs three things the way a lung needs air: electricity, clean water in large volumes, and a road that a person can drive on. Take any one away and the hours that Chapter 1 counted stop being a schedule and become a countdown.

## What happens when the lights go out

The research on disasters and dialysis is substantial, partly because dialysis patients are among the first people in a disaster whose illness cannot wait. A 2019 systematic review of studies in the Americas found that the loss of electricity, drinkable water, and transportation, and the closure of dialysis facilities, can disrupt care and lead to missed sessions and to more hospitalizations and emergency visits (Source Notes S49).

After Hurricane Katrina in 2005, a survey of patients from nine New Orleans dialysis units found that 16.8 percent missed three or more treatments and 23 percent were hospitalized in the following month. A national registry analysis linked the storm to higher mortality and hospitalization, though the authors cautioned that the makeup of the patient population changed before and after, which can distort comparisons (Source Notes S49).

The most thorough single study came after Hurricane Sandy in 2012. Researchers followed 13,264 in-center dialysis patients in the affected areas and compared them with patients in comparison regions. The rate of emergency visits was 4.1 percent in the storm group against 2.6 and 1.7 percent in the two comparison groups, hospitalization was 4.5 percent against 3.2 and 3.8, and the 30-day death rate was 1.83 percent against 1.47 and 1.60 (Dialysis care and death following Hurricane Sandy, n.d.†). It is a modest difference in absolute terms and a meaningful one in a vulnerable population.

## The thing that helped

What the Sandy study showed next is the part that every policymaker should memorize. Fifty-nine percent of the patients received early dialysis, an extra treatment before the storm hit, and those who did were less likely to go to an emergency room or be hospitalized within a week, with odds ratios of 0.75 and 0.77. The commentary on the study made the obvious observation that about 40 percent of patients did not receive early dialysis, which means that the preparedness was good and incomplete (What nephrologists learned from Hurricane Sandy, 2015†).

Puerto Rico after Hurricane Maria offers a counterpoint worth taking seriously. A study of mortality there found that deaths among people on dialysis were not higher afterward than they would otherwise have been, and the authors credited adherence to disaster-preparedness guidelines and early government response (Source Notes S49). In a hurricane that devastated the island's infrastructure, a population dependent on electricity and water emerged without excess death. It is not proof that preparation works, but it is an example of what it looks like when it does.

## The conservative virtue of foresight

The proverb at the head of this chapter praises the prudent man who sees the evil coming and takes shelter, and condemns the simple, who press on and are punished. It is a counsel of foresight, and it is the least glamorous virtue in public life. Nobody wins an award for the disaster that did not happen.

I think this chapter belongs in a book about time for a reason. Preparedness is the discipline of spending a few hours now so that a person is not stripped of many hours later. A dialysis unit that keeps a generator, that has a protocol for extra treatments before a forecast storm, that tells its patients in advance where to go and whom to call, is spending a modest amount of the system's time to protect a great deal of the patient's. The people who run such units rarely get credit for it.

It also belongs among the questions about distance. The woman on the Gulf Coast may have a daughter two states away. Many people do not. For them the storm is not a matter of choosing the best option but of finding any option, and a system that wishes to take seriously the principle of caring for the near would ask who is within reach of each person on its list when the road is under water.

## What the disaster literature leaves open

I did not find recent data on winter storms or the grid failures that now accompany them, though I would expect the same three needs, power, water, and a road, to govern. Nor did I find evidence on how many dialysis facilities in the country have a tested emergency plan. What I found suggests that early dialysis works, that about four in ten patients in one major storm did not get it, and that the cost of preparing is small. That is not a mystery needing more research. It is a gap needing a decision.
