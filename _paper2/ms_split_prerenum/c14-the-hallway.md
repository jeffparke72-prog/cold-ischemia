# Chapter 14: The Hallway
EPIGRAPH: "It is better to go to the house of mourning, than to go to the house of feasting: for that is the end of all men; and the living will lay it to his heart."
BY: Ecclesiastes 7:2 (King James Version, 1611)

Picture a hallway outside an intensive-care unit, late at night. The lights have been dimmed to the amber of a hotel corridor. A family stands at the end of it, close together, not speaking much. Behind a door a person they love is being kept alive by machines, and a doctor has just explained, as gently as such things can be explained, that the person will not recover. A second person has now appeared, quieter and softer-spoken, wearing a lanyard. She is the one who will ask the question.

I have spent much of this book on people whose lives depend on a gift. Here I want to spend time with the people who give it, who are not the recipients and not the donors in any operating-room sense, but the families of those who have died. They are, in the end, the ones on whom the entire deceased-donor system rests, and we know remarkably little about how they fare.

## What the research shows, and how thin it is

An interview study of 49 potential donor families in Australia found that no family who agreed to donation regretted the decision, while some who declined did. The process was often long and stressful, but not enough to reduce satisfaction with the outcome (Source Notes S30). A review of the literature reached a subtler conclusion: dissatisfaction with how hospital staff behaved was more likely than the donation itself to worsen families' grief, and the aggravating factors included lack of privacy, a feeling of being pressured, and fear of not honoring the wishes of the person who had died (Source Notes S30).

Support after the fact is uneven. A 15-country European inventory found that about half of respondents thought aftercare for donor families could be improved, and that anonymous written contact between donor families and recipients is possible in almost all countries, with direct contact permitted in only a few (Source Notes S31). An analysis of 1,613 letters over seven years, presented as a conference abstract, found that recipients wrote more often than donor families and usually wrote first, that recipients' letters were mostly about gratitude, and that donor families' letters were mostly about the person who had died (Source Notes S31). A Southern California procurement organization reported that consent rose after it created a family services and aftercare department in 2002, though it is an abstract and cannot establish that the department caused the rise (Source Notes S31).

What strikes me about this literature is how small it is. For a practice that touches thousands of families a year and whose entire legitimacy depends on their trust, there is surprisingly little research on how those families fare over time. The reviews say as much.

## What happened to trust

The trust on which all of this depends has been tested. In the autumn of 2024, news coverage of a Kentucky case, in which a patient was described by his family as showing signs of life as procurement was prepared, prompted a surge of people removing themselves from donor registries. The national registry reported an average of 170 removals a day in the following week, ten times the rate of the same week a year earlier, a count that did not include emailed requests or state registries. The head of the national association of procurement organizations said that organ donation is based on public trust, and that once eroded, it takes years to regain (Associated Press, 2024).

In March 2025 the federal oversight agency completed a report on its investigation of the Kentucky organization. It reviewed about 350 cases in which a planned recovery had been authorized but not completed. In 73 of them it concluded that officials should have reconsidered sooner, because the patients' level of consciousness was high or improving. In at least 28 cases, 8.0 percent, the records showed no documented time of cardiac death, which the agency described as suggesting potential survival to hospital discharge (Health Resources and Services Administration, Division of Transplantation, 2025). Let me be careful in how I say this, because the wording matters. It is a finding about documentation and judgment, serious in its implications, and not a finding that twenty-eight living people underwent removal. The organization has disputed characterizations of its practices, and I treat the most publicized individual account as an allegation. A summary issued by the Department of Health and Human Services in July 2025 stated that concerns were identified in 103 of 351 cases (Source Notes S32).

The response was substantial. The agency required a root-cause analysis and a policy allowing any staff member to halt a donation, directed the national network to report every pause for a safety concern, and noted that it had received reports of similar patterns elsewhere (Health Resources and Services Administration, Division of Transplantation, 2025). In September 2025 federal officials decertified a Miami procurement organization in mid-cycle, citing unsafe practices, inadequate training, chronic underperformance, understaffing, and paperwork errors, the first action of its kind (Fierce Healthcare, 2025). In 2026 the national network's board adopted new requirements for donation after circulatory death, which the oversight agency described as strengthening protections for potential donors (Health Resources and Services Administration, 2026b).

## The fence

G. K. Chesterton left a parable that conservatives are fond of citing, and this seems the right place to cite it. A man comes upon a fence across a road, and a reformer says he does not see the use of it and proposes to clear it away. The wiser reformer answers that if you do not see the use of it, you may not clear it away. Go away and think. When you can come back and tell me you do see the use, I may allow you to destroy it (Chesterton, 1929).

The voluntary donation of organs after death is such a fence. It rests on a delicate, unwritten understanding that those who give are not being used. Every reform, whether a new metric, a new tier, or a new payment model, is a hand on that fence. The events of 2024 and 2025 are best read as a reminder of why the fence was built. A donation system survives on its reputation for being, first, a system that does no harm.

I do not think the answer is to make donation harder to carry out. I think the answer is to make the process legible to the family in the hallway: who determines death, who is empowered to stop, what happens next, and who will call in a month. The people in the hallway are keeping a promise on behalf of someone who can no longer keep it himself. They deserve a system that behaves as though it knows that.
