# Chapter 1: The Half-Time Job
EPIGRAPH: "So teach us to number our days, that we may apply our hearts unto wisdom."
BY: Psalm 90:12 (King James Version, 1611)

Picture a woman I will call Ruth. She does not exist, and her afternoons are real. She retired from bookkeeping a few years ago, and she is exceptional at one thing, which is arriving. Her ride comes at 12:15. By 12:40 she is weighed, wrapped in a warm blanket she brought from home, and plugged into the chair. By 1:00 the machine has begun to do what her kidneys no longer can. She has brought a paperback and a thermos. She will read perhaps forty pages. She knows the names of the nurses' children. She knows who sleeps, who talks, and who gets quiet around hour three.

At 5:15 she is unhooked. She will not go to dinner with her sister, because she will not feel like it. She will sit in the passenger seat and watch the town go by in a way that is a little like watching a film of herself, and then she will be home, and she will be tired in a bone-deep, bargain-basement way that rest does not quite fix. On Wednesday she will feel better. On Thursday she will start counting backward toward Friday's chair.

If you asked Ruth to rank what matters most about her treatment, you might expect her to say staying alive. She would, of course, say that. But researchers have asked patients, and the answer gets more interesting after the obvious.

## What people asked for

An international project called SONG-HD set out to discover which outcomes ought to be measured in trials of hemodialysis. It did not simply ask experts. It surveyed patients and caregivers alongside clinicians, in three rounds, across 73 countries. More than 1,100 people took part in the first round, including 202 patients and caregivers and 979 health professionals, and 838 stayed through the third (Developing a set of core outcomes for trials in hemodialysis, 2017†).

The results were quietly startling. Where the two groups disagreed, they disagreed in a pattern. Patients and caregivers rated ability to travel higher than clinicians did, by nearly a full point on the scale. They rated time free from dialysis higher, by half a point. They gave higher marks as well to the adequacy of dialysis and, perhaps most tenderly, to a symptom the clinical literature barely names: feeling washed out after a session. Clinicians, for their part, rated mortality higher, along with hospitalization, drops in blood pressure, complications of vascular access, depression, and cardiovascular disease.

Neither list is wrong. A doctor is right to worry about death, and a patient is right to want to see her grandchildren in another state. But look at what the patients' list has in common. Travel, free time, feeling like oneself in the evening: these are not measures of the body's chemistry. They are measures of a life that has room in it. I should say that I could not confirm from the summaries I could reach which outcomes made the project's final core set, and I would not want to put words in its authors' mouths. The shape of the disagreement is enough. Those who live with the machine and those who tend it agree that it matters, and differ on what it is for.

## What it does to the mind

Hours do not only pass. They leave residue. A 2025 meta-analysis pooled 137 studies covering 21,608 hemodialysis patients and estimated that 45.8 percent of them had depression, with a confidence interval of 42.0 to 49.6 percent. Prevalence was lowest in high-income countries, at 38.9 percent, which is still more than one in three (Prevalence of depression in hemodialysis patients, 2025†). The studies differ in method, and they were mostly cross-sectional snapshots, so no one should read the figure as a precise census. But the authors' conclusion is hard to argue with: screening and identifying those at risk is valuable, and I found no sign that it is routine.

Loneliness belongs in the same conversation. A large population-based cohort of people with chronic kidney disease, not dialysis patients specifically, found that those in the most socially isolated group had a 33 percent higher risk of death, and that isolation was associated with a loss of about two years of life expectancy for women and nearly three for men. Those who reported loneliness had a 10 percent higher risk (Association of social isolation and loneliness with the incidence and life expectancy of chronic kidney disease, 2026†). Reviews of the qualitative literature add the texture: the schedule of treatment dominates the calendar, which makes it hard to plan a holiday or a family dinner, and social support appears to blunt the effect.

Here a conservative sensibility has something to say that a purely clinical one might miss. We tend to treat loneliness as a feeling and dialysis as a procedure, and to place them in different departments. But they are one thing in one body in one chair. A person whose treatment consumes the afternoons that friendship usually occupies is not being treated for kidney failure alone. She is being treated for the loss of her ordinary life, and the ordinary life is where the little platoons live, the neighbors, the congregation, the card game. The medicine that ignores them is incomplete in a way no lab value reveals.

## The question the chair asks

I do not mean to suggest that anything here can be solved by kindness. Machines are necessary, and the nurses who run them are, in my experience, among the most patient people in medicine. I mean something narrower. A system reveals its priorities by what it counts. Ours counts deaths, hospitalizations, and transplant volumes with admirable precision. It counts Ruth's Tuesday not at all.

So let me ask the question the chair asks, and let me ask it of every chapter ahead. If this person had a thousand and ninety-two hours back, what would she do with them? Whatever the answer, it is the measure of what we are really building toward. A better machine, a quicker transplant, a quieter way of living without either: each is a different answer to the same plea, which is simply to be given back the afternoon.
