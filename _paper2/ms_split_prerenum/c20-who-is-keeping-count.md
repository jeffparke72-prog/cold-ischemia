# Chapter 20: Who Is Keeping Count
EPIGRAPH: "One of the essential qualities of the clinician is interest in humanity, for the secret of the care of the patient is in caring for the patient."
BY: Francis W. Peabody, "The Care of the Patient," JAMA (1927)

Picture an administrator, late on a Thursday, scrolling through a dashboard with forty-one metrics. Door-to-needle times, readmission rates, graft survival at one year, transplant volumes, compliance with the latest policy. The numbers are carefully defined and the colors mostly green. Nothing on the screen records that on Tuesday a woman named in no database sat for four hours, felt washed out for three more, and missed her granddaughter's recital.

That is not a failure of the dashboard. It is a description of it. A measurement system is a theory of what matters, and it is worth reading a system's metrics the way you would read a person's calendar.

## What is counted

Consider the instruments that now govern American kidney care. A payment model for dialysis facilities, the one I described in Chapter 8, rewards home dialysis and transplantation. A newer model for transplant hospitals, which began on 1 July 2025 and runs to mid-2031, scores each participating hospital out of 100: 60 points for the number of transplants it performs, 20 for how readily it accepts organ offers, and 20 for how long its grafts survive. A hospital can earn up to $15,000 per Medicare kidney transplant and be charged at most $2,000, and in the first year there is no downside risk at all. As of this writing no first-year results have been published (Centers for Medicare & Medicaid Services, n.d.). A third instrument sorts the nation's 55 organ procurement organizations into tiers on the basis of two rates, a donation rate and a transplant rate, with the lowest tier facing loss of its territory. The first recertification cycle ended in July 2026, a final rule is expected in late 2026, and contracts of organizations in the lowest tier remain in force through 31 January 2027 (Holland & Knight, 2026). I found no published roster of which organizations those are.

The governing body of the whole network is in the middle of rebuilding itself. In April 2026 its board extended its president's and vice president's terms by a year, saying that contractor transitions and funding interruptions had impaired its ability to move initiatives forward (Health Resources and Services Administration, 2026c). It later raised the fee charged for each registered candidate to $1,268 for fiscal year 2027, about 22 percent more than the year before, and ended one of its standing committees (Health Resources and Services Administration, 2026d). All of this comes from the agency's own summaries, and I found no independent press coverage that tested them.

## What patients named

Now set that list beside the one in Chapter 1. When patients and caregivers were asked what mattered, they rated the ability to travel, time free from dialysis, the adequacy of treatment, and feeling washed out higher than clinicians did (Developing a set of core outcomes for trials in hemodialysis, 2017†). Read side by side, the two lists barely touch. The system counts volume, acceptance, survival, and compliance. The patients named travel, time, and how they feel in the evening.

I do not say that the system's measures are wrong. Volume matters, survival matters, and holding monopolies to account matters. I say that the instruments are incomplete in a specific way, and that the incompleteness lands on the people with the most at stake.

## The idea behind a core outcome set

The international survey in Chapter 1 belonged to a larger movement within nephrology, one that deserves more credit than it gets. The premise of a core outcome set is simple and slightly radical: before researchers run a trial, they should agree, with patients in the room, on the short list of outcomes that every trial in a field ought to measure. For decades each trial chose its own, usually the ones that were easiest to count, so that results could not be compared and the things patients cared about were often not measured at all. A core outcome set is an attempt to correct that, by writing the patient's priorities into the design of research itself.

I find this encouraging, because it shows that the problem described in this chapter has been recognized by people inside the field, and because it supplies a model for the dashboard I am proposing. If a research community can convene more than a thousand patients, caregivers, and clinicians in 73 countries to agree on what trials should count, a health system could convene a few hundred of its own patients to say what its dashboard should count. The hard part is not the method. It is the willingness to be surprised by the answer.
## The law of the good metric

The anthropologist Marilyn Strathern put a warning in one sentence that has since been widely repeated: when a measure becomes a target, it ceases to be a good measure (Strathern, 1997). Every metric in this chapter is vulnerable to it. A volume score can be raised by transplanting those who would have done well anyway. A graft-survival score can be protected by declining difficult cases. An acceptance score can be improved by accepting what should be declined. The remedy is not to abandon measurement, which would be a disaster for the people who most need it. It is to measure in groups, so that anyone who inflates one number reveals themselves in another, and to include in the group the things that cannot be gamed because they are reported by the patient.

## A plain way to tell noise from news

Industry has a small tool for exactly this problem, called a control chart. It plots a measure over time and draws bands around its ordinary range, so that you can tell a real change from the normal wobble. Imagine applying it to the share of recovered kidneys that go unused. That figure was 18.2 percent in 2013, 26.6 percent in 2022, 27.9 percent in 2023, and 29.3 percent in 2024 (Scientific Registry of Transplant Recipients & Organ Procurement and Transplantation Network, 2025, 2026a). No chart is needed to see that this is not wobble. But the nation publishes such numbers once a year, years late, and a number published once a year cannot reveal a drift that begins in March.

## A dashboard a patient could read

Suppose we began over. I would ask a few hundred patients and care partners to name the five things they most wanted measured, and I would measure those. Time in the chair and in recovery. Distance traveled. Days at home. How much the care partner is carrying. Whether medicine was ever skipped for cost. I would publish them monthly and plainly. Peabody wrote in 1927 that the secret of the care of the patient is in caring for the patient, and the sentence is easy to quote and hard to operationalize. A dashboard that begins with the patient's own list is as close as I know to a way of doing it.
