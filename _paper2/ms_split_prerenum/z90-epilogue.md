# Epilogue: A Tuesday Worth Wanting
EPIGRAPH: "How vain it is to sit down to write when you have not stood up to live."
BY: Henry David Thoreau, Journal (1851)

Picture Ruth again, a few years from now, in a story I cannot promise. It is Tuesday. She did not go to the chair this morning, because there is no chair. Perhaps a kidney arrived. Perhaps her treatment moved to her kitchen. Perhaps a friend she trained to ask for her did the asking. I do not know how it happened, and she would tell you the how matters less than the what. It is one o'clock in the afternoon, and she is doing something unimportant. She is deciding whether to make soup.

Nothing in the data in this book predicts that Tuesday for any individual. What the data do show is that the ingredients exist. Medicines that keep the silence from lasting. Machines that fit in a kitchen. Chains of strangers who give. Cells that may teach the body peace. Pig kidneys that have run for months. Programs that train friends. Laws that, belatedly, began to reimburse donors for their generosity. The ingredients are not yet a meal, and many of them will not survive a clinical trial. But they exist, which was not true a generation ago.

What remains is a decision about what we are building toward. I have argued that the answer is time, and I have tried to say so without sentiment, because sentiment is not what the person in the chair needs. She needs the afternoon back. She needs the care partner counted, the local unit kept open, the cost of the pill known, the choice of whether to dialyze at all offered honestly, and a kidney, when one comes, that lasts.

I have sat in rooms where the chord of the machines hums and no one talks. I have learned that silence is not emptiness. It is attention, spent on one another. The same attention, scaled up, is the only thing that has ever made a system humane. We have a great many instruments in this field. What we need is to be a little more present in the room.

I will leave you with a question, which I ask of myself as often as I ask it of anyone. If you were given a certain number of afternoons, what would you want them for? And when you know the answer, what would you ask of the people who look after you, to help you spend them?

Two things more, and then I will let you go. The first is a thank-you to the people this book is really about, who will never read it, and who are right now sitting in a chair with a book of their own. You taught me what attention is. The second is an invitation. If you are one of those people, or you love one, and you find something in these pages that is wrong, or incomplete, or that misses the thing you most wanted said, tell the Foundation. A book about being counted ought to be willing to be corrected.
