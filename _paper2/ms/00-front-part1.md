# Prologue: Four Hours
EPIGRAPH: "I have discovered that all the unhappiness of men arises from one single fact, that they cannot stay quietly in their own chamber."
BY: Blaise Pascal, Pensées (Trotter translation, 1908)

Pascal believed the trouble with people was that they could not sit still. He never met anyone whose treatment required it.

Picture a room with a row of recliners and a window nobody looks out of. The chairs are vinyl, the color of weak tea. Each one faces a machine the size of a bar refrigerator, and each machine hums at a slightly different pitch, so the room has a kind of chord in it. A television is bolted high in a corner, tuned to a program no one chose. It is one o'clock on a Tuesday. Nobody here is sick in the way the movies show sickness. They are reading, dozing, doing crosswords, answering email, watching their own blood travel in a loop through clear tubing and come back clean. They are, for the next four hours, exactly where they are supposed to be.

That scene is a composite, built from what patients, nurses, and researchers have described. No single person stands in it. I begin with it because I have come to believe that the most important fact about kidney failure is also the one least often counted. It is not the number of people waiting for an organ, though that number is large. It is not the cost, though the cost is larger. It is the hours.

Let me do the arithmetic, slowly, because slowness is the point. A common schedule for in-center hemodialysis is three sessions a week of about four hours each. Over fifty-two weeks that is 156 sessions and 624 hours in the chair. Now add what comes afterward. In an online survey of 359 patients who dialyze in centers, the median time to recover from a session was three hours, and four in ten said it took four hours or longer. The most common complaint was feeling washed out, reported by 62 percent, followed by cramps at 44 percent and symptoms of low blood pressure at 42 percent (Source Notes S1). Three hours of recovery across 156 sessions is another 468 hours.

Together, 1,092 hours. A full-time job occupies about 2,080 hours in a year. By this reckoning, kidney failure treated in a center takes something like half of a full-time job, and the person holding it is not paid, cannot quit, and is often too tired to do any other job at all. I need to be honest about the limits of that figure. The schedule is typical and not universal, the recovery number comes from one modest survey, and recovery time overlaps with sleep and rest in ways no survey can untangle. Travel to and from the clinic is not counted at all. The point is not that the sum is exact. The point is that it is the right kind of sum, and that almost nobody does it.

[[FIG:time|Figure 0.1. A year of in-center hemodialysis in hours, compared with a full-time work year. Illustrative arithmetic from a common three-session schedule and a published median recovery time; travel excluded.]]

I have lived long enough inside this world, in more than one role, to know the sound that rooms like this make, and the sound that a family makes when it is doing a sum like this one in the parking lot. I will not tell you my own story. It belongs to me and to the few people who carried me through it. What I can offer instead is a way of looking, which is to ask of every part of the American kidney ecosystem a question so plain it sounds naive. What is this doing to people's time?

The question has an old pedigree. The psalmist asked God to teach us to number our days. The Stoics made an entire discipline of the observation that life is not short so much as spent carelessly. In our era the question has been reduced to productivity, which is a shrunken version of it. But in a dialysis chair, the old question returns at full size. If you were given a certain number of afternoons, what would you want them for? And what do the rest of us owe a person whose afternoons we have, by accident of biology, quietly claimed?

This book is my attempt to take that question seriously, in two registers at once. One is existential, in the plain sense of the word: it concerns finitude, choice, and the strange dignity of deciding how to spend a limited life. The other is conservative, in the older sense of the word: it concerns what is worth preserving, the humility to ask why a thing stands before tearing it down, and a stubborn faith in the small institutions, a family, a congregation, a town's clinic, a hospital's coordinator, that do most of the real work of caring while the grand systems get the credit.

You will find that the chapters do not follow one another like a case being made. They are more like rooms in a house, each with a different window. One looks onto the dialysis afternoon. One looks onto the quiet decision some people make to decline dialysis altogether. One looks onto the person who drives, the person who gives, the family in a hallway, the surgeon's telephone at three in the morning, the machines that may someday set the afternoons free. I have tried to bring current evidence into every room, drawn from the last year wherever it exists, and to say plainly where the evidence ends.

I will make you one promise and one request. The promise is that I will not use a statistic to frighten you when a sentence will do, and I will not use a story to persuade you of something the data do not support. The request is that you read slowly. The people in these chairs have been taught patience whether they wanted the lesson or not. We might at least match it.

# A Note Before We Begin
EPIGRAPH: "Felix qui potuit rerum cognoscere causas."
BY: Virgil, Georgics II.490 ("Fortunate is the one who has been able to learn the causes of things.")

A few plain words about how this book was made, because a book that asks you to trust it owes you the details.

**The scenes are composites.** When a chapter opens with a picture of a person or a room, it is built from documented patterns, not from an individual. I have given no one a real name, and I have invented no statistics inside any scene. Whenever I describe an actual person or event, I cite the source.

**The facts are graded.** Every claim carries an inline citation. Behind each one is a rating I assigned in the source notes at the back. A means a primary document, a registry, or a peer-reviewed paper whose key figures I could confirm. B means a credible secondary account. C means a figure from a single source or from a party with a stake in the result, such as a manufacturer or an advocacy campaign. Material graded C is always labeled as preliminary where it appears.

**Where I could not see the whole page.** The research tools I used returned summaries and bibliographic details, not the full text of every article or agency page. A dagger (†) after a citation means the central figures were confirmed but some detail, such as a complete author list, should be completed from the original before any future edition. I would rather show you that gap than hide it.

**The date.** This was written in October 2026. I have leaned on developments from the preceding six months wherever they exist, and I say so when the newest figure is older. A few landmark studies appear as background and are labeled as such.

**Disclosure.** The Cold Ischemia Foundation accepts no donations, grants, or sponsorships, and charges posted fees only for services that individuals request. The Foundation publishes educational material about oxygen-carrier technology, including the work of a company called BHOC Therapeutics. The author reports no financial interest in that company or in any other organization discussed here. The Foundation's own position is that the technology is a research concept whose benefits are unproven, and this book repeats that position without softening it.

# Chapter 1: The Half-Time Job
EPIGRAPH: "So teach us to number our days, that we may apply our hearts unto wisdom."
BY: Psalm 90:12 (King James Version, 1611)

Picture a woman I will call Ruth. She does not exist, and her afternoons are real. She retired from bookkeeping a few years ago, and she is exceptional at one thing, which is arriving. Her ride comes at 12:15. By 12:40 she is weighed, wrapped in a warm blanket she brought from home, and plugged into the chair. By 1:00 the machine has begun to do what her kidneys no longer can. She has brought a paperback and a thermos. She will read perhaps forty pages. She knows the names of the nurses' children. She knows who sleeps, who talks, and who gets quiet around hour three.

At 5:15 she is unhooked. She will not go to dinner with her sister, because she will not feel like it. She will sit in the passenger seat and watch the town go by in a way that is a little like watching a film of herself, and then she will be home, and she will be tired in a bone-deep, bargain-basement way that rest does not quite fix. On Wednesday she will feel better. On Thursday she will start counting backward toward Friday's chair.

If you asked Ruth to rank what matters most about her treatment, you might expect her to say staying alive. She would, of course, say that. But researchers have asked patients, and the answer gets more interesting after the obvious.

## What people asked for

An international project called SONG-HD set out to discover which outcomes ought to be measured in trials of hemodialysis. It did not simply ask experts. It surveyed patients and caregivers alongside clinicians, in three rounds, across 73 countries. More than 1,100 people took part in the first round, including 202 patients and caregivers and 979 health professionals, and 838 stayed through the third (Developing a set of core outcomes for trials in hemodialysis, 2017†).

The results were quietly startling. Where the two groups disagreed, they disagreed in a pattern. Patients and caregivers rated ability to travel higher than clinicians did, by nearly a full point on the scale. They rated time free from dialysis higher, by half a point. They gave higher marks as well to the adequacy of dialysis and, perhaps most tenderly, to a symptom the clinical literature barely names: feeling washed out after a session. Clinicians, for their part, rated mortality higher, along with hospitalization, drops in blood pressure, complications of vascular access, depression, and cardiovascular disease.

Neither list is wrong. A doctor is right to worry about death, and a patient is right to want to see her grandchildren in another state. But look at what the patients' list has in common. Travel, free time, feeling like oneself in the evening: these are not measures of the body's chemistry. They are measures of a life that has room in it. I should say that I could not confirm from the summaries I could reach which outcomes made the project's final core set, and I would not want to put words in its authors' mouths. The shape of the disagreement is enough. Those who live with the machine and those who tend it agree that it matters, and differ on what it is for.

## What it does to the mind

Hours do not only pass. They leave residue. A 2025 meta-analysis pooled 137 studies covering 21,608 hemodialysis patients and estimated that 45.8 percent of them had depression, with a confidence interval of 42.0 to 49.6 percent. Prevalence was lowest in high-income countries, at 38.9 percent, which is still more than one in three (Prevalence of depression in hemodialysis patients, 2025†). The studies differ in method, and they were mostly cross-sectional snapshots, so no one should read the figure as a precise census. But the authors' conclusion is hard to argue with: screening and identifying those at risk is valuable, and I found no sign that it is routine.

Loneliness belongs in the same conversation. A large population-based cohort of people with chronic kidney disease, not dialysis patients specifically, found that those in the most socially isolated group had a 33 percent higher risk of death, and that isolation was associated with a loss of about two years of life expectancy for women and nearly three for men. Those who reported loneliness had a 10 percent higher risk (Association of social isolation and loneliness with the incidence and life expectancy of chronic kidney disease, 2026†). Reviews of the qualitative literature add the texture: the schedule of treatment dominates the calendar, which makes it hard to plan a holiday or a family dinner, and social support appears to blunt the effect.

Here a conservative sensibility has something to say that a purely clinical one might miss. We tend to treat loneliness as a feeling and dialysis as a procedure, and to place them in different departments. But they are one thing in one body in one chair. A person whose treatment consumes the afternoons that friendship usually occupies is not being treated for kidney failure alone. She is being treated for the loss of her ordinary life, and the ordinary life is where the little platoons live, the neighbors, the congregation, the card game. The medicine that ignores them is incomplete in a way no lab value reveals.

## The question the chair asks

I do not mean to suggest that anything here can be solved by kindness. Machines are necessary, and the nurses who run them are, in my experience, among the most patient people in medicine. I mean something narrower. A system reveals its priorities by what it counts. Ours counts deaths, hospitalizations, and transplant volumes with admirable precision. It counts Ruth's Tuesday not at all.

So let me ask the question the chair asks, and let me ask it of every chapter ahead. If this person had a thousand and ninety-two hours back, what would she do with them? Whatever the answer, it is the measure of what we are really building toward. A better machine, a quicker transplant, a quieter way of living without either: each is a different answer to the same plea, which is simply to be given back the afternoon.

# Chapter 2: The Little Platoon
EPIGRAPH: "...the growing good of the world is partly dependent on unhistoric acts; and that things are not so ill with you and me as they might have been, is half owing to the number who lived faithfully a hidden life."
BY: George Eliot, Middlemarch (1872)

Somebody drives Ruth. We rarely ask who.

In the vocabulary of the field he is a care partner, which is the sort of phrase that sounds like it was invented by a committee and, for once, earned its place. He might be her husband. He might be a son who moved back into his old bedroom, or a neighbor, or a niece. Whoever he is, he keeps the calendar, remembers the medication names, watches for the swelling in her ankles, sits through the appointments, and does the thing nobody else will do, which is to say "I'm worried about her" to a doctor with eleven minutes.

Edmund Burke wrote, in 1790, that "to be attached to the subdivision, to love the little platoon we belong to in society, is the first principle (the germ as it were) of public affections." He was not writing about dialysis. But I know no better description of the unit in which kidney care actually takes place. It is not a hospital. It is not a payer. It is two or three people at a kitchen table with a pill organizer.

## What the platoon pays

The research on caregivers of dialysis patients is mostly small, from many countries, and cross-sectional, so it shows association and not cause. It is consistent enough to take seriously. Across studies in several settings, the hours a person spends caring are linked to depression, anxiety, and strain, and the burden tends to rise with the length of the caregiving (Source Notes S2). I will not offer a single number for how many hours a week the average care partner gives, because the figures I found were reported in incompatible units and I do not trust them. I would ask instead that you notice the absence of the number. An enterprise that costs the public billions and rests on the unpaid labor of the patient's family has never, so far as I could find, made counting that labor a priority.

## The gift of asking

There is one place where the platoon has been put to deliberate use, and the results are instructive. Many people who need a living-donor kidney simply cannot bring themselves to ask. The request is intimate and awkward, and the person most in need of courage is the person with the least to spare. A program at Johns Hopkins, called the Live Donor Champion program, answered with a change of roles. The patient chooses a friend, relative, or community member to speak on her behalf, and the two attend six monthly sessions of about an hour each, learning how to talk about living donation and how to map the networks they belong to.

In an early pilot, 15 candidates with no prospective donor went through the program and were compared with 15 matched candidates who did not. Twenty-five potential donors contacted the center on behalf of the program group. Four participants went on to receive a living-donor transplant and three more had donors in evaluation. Among the matched controls the number was zero (Source Notes S3). A larger follow-up of 163 candidates reported 81 donor referrals, and participants had about five and a half times the odds of at least one referral compared with matched controls on the waiting list (Source Notes S3). That result was presented as a conference abstract and it is observational, and I found no randomized trial of the program. I treat it as promising, not proven.

What moves me is not the statistic but the design. The program did not hire a bigger institution. It trained a friend. It recognized that the strongest asset a patient has is the web of people who already love her, and that the web needs words, permission, and a little coaching to become useful.

A faith-based organization in Israel has taken the same insight a step further. Matnat Chaim, a nonprofit that arranges mostly altruistic donations between strangers, facilitated 494 living kidney donations from its founding in 2009 through the end of 2017, according to a retrospective review (Emergence of an Israel faith-based community organization facilitating live donor kidney transplantation, 2018†). A retrospective count shows scale and not cause, and nobody should read it as proof that a congregation can replace a hospital. It does suggest that a community with a shared moral language can do something a bureaucracy cannot, which is to make generosity feel ordinary.

## An institution built like a platoon

The most striking large-scale illustration of this principle may be Spain. In 2025 the country recorded 51.9 deceased donors per million people, one of the highest rates in the world, with 2,547 deceased donors and 408 living donors that year, a little below its own record of 52.6 per million set in 2024 (Ministerio de Sanidad, 2026; La Moncloa, 2025). I will not compare that rate with the American one, because definitions of a donor differ and the comparison invites more heat than light. What interests me is the structure. The Spanish system is organized in three tiers, national, regional, and hospital, and at the base of the pyramid sits a hospital coordinator, usually an anesthesiologist or intensive-care physician working part-time in the role, embedded in the hospital where donation happens (The Spanish model, 2025). The coordinator knows the unit, the staff, and often the family. This is an institution designed on the scale of a platoon.

Whether it can be transplanted to another country is unclear, and its admirers disagree about which feature does the work. But the lesson for a conservative reader seems plain. The nearer a decision sits to the people it affects, the better it tends to go. We have built a national apparatus of extraordinary technical power, and I suspect it would work better if more of its human contact were placed in the hands of people who can be recognized in a hallway.

## What we owe the platoon

If the little platoon is where care happens, then policy has a simple obligation, which is to stop treating it as free. That does not necessarily mean wages. It could mean respite, training, a named person to call, a plain-language map of what is coming, or a clinic that asks the care partner how she is. It would be a modest revolution in a field that has built an elaborate machinery of support for the patient and almost none for the person who stands behind her.

Eliot ended her great novel by honoring the hidden lives that keep the world from being worse than it is. That is the care partner, to the letter. The least we owe such people is to count them.

# Chapter 3: The Quiet Option
EPIGRAPH: "That to philosophize is to learn to die."
BY: Michel de Montaigne, essay title, Florio translation (1603)

Picture a woman of eighty-two, whom I will not name, sitting across from a nephrologist whose kindness is obvious and whose clock is ticking. Her kidney function has fallen to the point where dialysis is the next step. The doctor has laid out the plan with a good deal of skill: access surgery, a schedule, a chair. And the woman, who spent her working life as a seamstress and has outlived her husband and one of her children, asks a question that is not on the form. "What would it cost me," she says, "in afternoons?"

This scene is a composite, and I begin with it because the question she asks is one that American kidney care has barely learned to hear.

## An option almost nobody is offered

Conservative kidney management, sometimes called comprehensive conservative care, means choosing to treat kidney failure without dialysis or a transplant, with attention to symptoms, comfort, and the patient's priorities for the time that remains. It is not "doing nothing." It involves medication, diet, support, and often palliative care. It is also, in the United States, strikingly rare.

In a 2023 analysis of a large cohort of people with chronic kidney disease, only 7 percent of 1,018 participants were planning for conservative management. Among 407 who completed a survey, 18 percent reported ever having a conversation with a provider about forgoing dialysis. The providers, for their part, mostly said they felt comfortable raising the subject, and yet none worked in a setting with a dedicated conservative-management clinic or protocol (Source Notes S4). A qualitative study of twenty nephrologists at Veterans Affairs facilities found that national guidelines call for discussing conservative management with patients at advanced stages, that this rarely happens, and that clinicians feel real emotional strain when they care for older patients who are moving toward dialysis (Source Notes S5).

The reasons are not hard to guess. Dialysis is reimbursed, scheduled, and accompanied by an infrastructure. Conversation is none of those. And physicians trained to rescue find it hard to say that rescue may not be what this person wants.

## What the evidence suggests, and what it cannot

Observational studies of adults aged seventy-five and older who have significant illness or functional limitation suggest that survival and quality of life may not differ appreciably between those who start dialysis and those who choose a conservative path. Dialysis is associated with more hospital stays and a smaller chance of dying at home or in hospice. In studies of people approaching kidney failure, a substantial share said they would give up meaningful life expectancy to reduce the burden of treatment (Source Notes S6). A 2026 qualitative study in JAMA Network Open, drawing on two American randomized trials conducted between 2019 and 2024, examined why older adults choose this path. I could read only its abstract, which describes the approach as infrequently used despite the weight dialysis places on patients and caregivers (Older patients and conservative kidney management, 2026†).

Let me be careful here, because this is the most delicate subject in the book. Observational comparisons of this kind are vulnerable to bias, since the people who choose conservative care are, on average, frailer to begin with. I am not arguing that anyone should forgo dialysis, and I would be appalled if this chapter were read as an argument for fewer starts. I am arguing for something simpler and more demanding: that the choice be real. A person who has never been told there is a second path has not chosen the first.

## The quiet endings

The other side of this question is dialysis withdrawal, the decision to stop treatment after it has begun. It is more common than most people suppose. Withdrawal is reported as a cause of death in 20 to 30 percent of people with kidney failure in Western countries, and the authors of the reviews I read note that this is probably an underestimate because of inconsistent coding (Source Notes S7). At one Midwestern center that followed 1,226 patients from 2001 to 2015, 536 died, and 262 of those deaths, 49 percent, followed a decision to withdraw. The death-notification forms captured the withdrawal in only about 73 percent of sampled cases (Source Notes S8).

After withdrawal, the end usually comes quickly. In a three-year Italian program that joined nephrology and palliative care, most patients died within a week, and the program reported fewer hospital admissions and more deaths at home (Source Notes S9). An Australian abstract reported that people who stop dialysis typically survive about a week, and that most still die in acute-care hospitals, often against their wishes (Source Notes S10). A week is short. It is enough time to say what needs saying, if the setting allows, and not enough to repair a setting that does not.

I mention these figures not to be grim but because they describe a moment for which most families are wholly unprepared. They arrive at it in a hospital corridor, without a plan, because no one had the conversation months earlier when there was still time to have it well.

## The oldest question

Montaigne titled one of his essays with a sentence that has been quoted for four centuries: to philosophize is to learn to die. He did not mean that we should brood. He meant that a person who has looked squarely at the end is freer to choose what to do with the middle. That is the existential point beneath the clinical one. Dialysis is a method of buying time. The seamstress's question, what will it cost me in afternoons, is the right question to ask of any purchase. Some people, asking it, will buy the time gladly and use it well. Some will decide the price is too high. Both are exercising the same dignity.

A conservative reading adds a note of humility. Medicine's power tempts it toward the view that every decline is a problem to be solved. The older and wiser tradition holds that the person is the authority over her own life, that families are the proper witnesses of such decisions, and that the physician's role is to illuminate the choice and not to make it. A system that cannot say "there is another way, and here is how it goes" has not respected the person in front of it.

If you are weighing this decision for yourself or someone you love, please do so with your own clinical team, and with a palliative care specialist if one is available. If you are in distress, call or text 988.
