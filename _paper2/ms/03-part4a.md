# Chapter 10: Three in the Morning
EPIGRAPH: "The art of being wise is the art of knowing what to overlook."
BY: William James, The Principles of Psychology (1890)

Picture a surgeon asleep in her own bed when the telephone lights up the nightstand. It is 3:07 a.m. A coordinator from an organ procurement organization is on the line, speaking fast and clearly in the manner of people who make this call often. A kidney has become available. The donor is sixty-one. There was a biopsy, and the result is not wonderful and not terrible. The kidney is a few hours from her hospital by air. A patient on her list, a man she has known for three years, is sixth in line. Four centers ahead of her have already said no.

She has perhaps a few minutes to decide, and the decision has several layers. Is the kidney good enough for this man? Can it be here in time? Will his recovery be harder if it arrives late? If it works poorly, her program's outcome statistics will carry the mark. If she declines, the offer passes to the next center, and perhaps the kidney is used, or perhaps it is not.

The scene is a composite. The dilemma in it is exactly the one the data describe.

## What goes unused

In 2024, 29.3 percent of the kidneys recovered for transplant in the United States were not transplanted, up from 18.2 percent in 2013 (Scientific Registry of Transplant Recipients & Organ Procurement and Transplantation Network, 2026a). The registry's analysts say "nonuse," not "discard," and the choice is telling, because it concedes that the fate of a recovered kidney is often a decision and not a destiny.

Two details in that report say something about how decisions behave. Kidneys from donors who had a procurement biopsy went unused 40.8 percent of the time, against 6.4 percent for kidneys not biopsied. Selection explains much of that, since surgeons biopsy the kidneys they already doubt. But a biopsy samples a small piece of an organ and is read by a pathologist who may have been woken for the purpose, and a rate that high invites the question of how consistently such readings turn into verdicts (Scientific Registry of Transplant Recipients & Organ Procurement and Transplantation Network, 2026a). The other detail concerns hepatitis C. Nonuse of kidneys from donors who had been exposed but had no active virus fell from 49.5 percent in 2016 to 32.2 percent in 2024, which the report attributes to the spread of drugs that cure the infection. A drug invented elsewhere changed what surgeons were willing to do, and about a third fewer of those kidneys were wasted. Much of nonuse, in other words, is not a property of the organ. It is a property of what people believe they can safely do.

## One kidney, many answers

The decision at three in the morning is a human one, and humans vary. A national study of offers made between 2021 and 2023 found that the 193 adult centers accepted a median of 12.5 percent of offers for ordinary kidneys and 7.2 percent for suboptimal ones, with the widest variation among kidneys that had already endured more than 36 hours of cold storage (Quantifying center-level aggressiveness in transplanting suboptimal, deceased donor kidneys in the United States, n.d.†). Earlier studies, which I could read only in summary, suggest that variation within a single center can exceed variation between centers, that kidneys are offered a median of seven times before acceptance, and that agreement among transplant nephrologists on whether to accept a given offer was poor, with a statistic of 0.13 where 1.0 means perfect agreement (Source Notes S33).

I want to be fair to the surgeon in the story. Variation among skilled people deciding under uncertainty is not a sign of incompetence. It is a sign that the problem is hard and that the information is thin. The old teaching, put memorably by William James, is that wisdom is largely the art of knowing what to overlook. But to know what to overlook, one has to see everything first, and at three in the morning, nobody does.

## The hour as a hidden variable

Every hour on ice has a cost, and the cost compounds. A French cohort of 3,839 deceased-donor recipients found that each added hour of cold storage raised the hazard of graft failure by a factor of 1.013 and of death by 1.018 (Debout et al., 2015†). Compounded across the twenty-four hours between six and thirty, my own arithmetic gives a 36 percent higher hazard of graft failure and 53 percent higher hazard of death. The authors, rounding differently, wrote that a thirty-hour kidney carried a risk near forty percent higher than a six-hour kidney. A study reported in June 2026 of 74 kidney pairs from older donors found that cold storage beyond twelve hours raised the odds of delayed graft function more than sixfold, though it was small and from a single center (Source Notes S34).

The point is not that long journeys doom a kidney. The harm is a slope, not a cliff, and the surgeon in the story is weighing a slope. The deeper point is that the hours are a product of logistics as much as medicine. In 2025 a federal working group delivered a final report with 20 recommendations on moving organs, and the Federal Aviation Administration launched a website to help those who carry them by air, with updated guidance on keeping organs in the cabin (Health Resources and Services Administration, n.d.). A 2024 industry briefing noted that the contract then in force could not require organs to be tracked, which made it hard to learn what happened to those that arrived late or damaged, and the organization long contracted to run the network says it built its own tracking tool, now used by 16 procurement organizations (Source Notes S35). When the national kidney-allocation system was changed in 2021 to share organs more broadly, a federal monitoring report found that kidneys traveled farther, mostly within about 250 nautical miles, and that nonuse rose from 21 to 26 percent (Source Notes S35). A proposal for a more flexible system of ranking candidates, called continuous distribution, was listed as paused as of July 2025, and I found no newer status (Source Notes S35).

## What would help her

I do not think the answer is to take the decision away from the surgeon. The answer is to give her a better map than the one she has. A short, standard summary of the donor and the kidney that all centers see in the same format. A projection of what the expected cold time will be, updated as the plane takes off. A way to ask for a second look at a kidney that has been declined repeatedly. A published, plain-language record of how often decisions like hers turn out well, so that she is not carrying the full weight of uncertainty alone.

Above all I would give her a friend, a colleague or a system that tells her at three in the morning what the evidence says about kidneys like this one, as clearly as a pilot is told what the instruments say. Judgment is a human quality, and it improves with good instruments. That is not an argument against the surgeon. It is an argument for her.

# Chapter 11: The Odd Machines
EPIGRAPH: "Why should we be in such desperate haste to succeed, and in such desperate enterprises?"
BY: Henry David Thoreau, Walden (1854)

The oddest part of transplant medicine is how little of it looks like the future. Much of the daily work still depends on a cooler, a courier, and a telephone. And yet around the edges of that old routine, a set of strange new machines is being built, some of them so unlikely that when you first hear of them they sound like fiction. A pig kidney that works in a human being. A chemical that carries oxygen without a single red cell. A pump that keeps an organ alive outside the body.

Any honest tour of these has to begin by admitting that most promising technologies in medicine fail, and that hope is cheap. So I will do what I have tried to do throughout, which is to ask of each machine a single question: what would it give back, and how well do we know?

## The pump

Machine perfusion, the practice of pumping cold preservation fluid through a kidney in place of letting it sit on ice, is the senior member of the family. A randomized trial of 672 recipients, in which one kidney from each donor went to each approach, found that perfusion reduced delayed graft function and improved one-year graft survival (Moers et al., 2009). In November 2025 the manufacturer of one such device announced a ten-year follow-up reporting a 27 percent lower risk of graft failure, with ten-year data for 92 percent of recipients (Organ Recovery Systems, 2025). I classify that as preliminary until it is confirmed by the paper itself, since the announcer sells the device.

Warming the kidney to body temperature while perfusing it has been less persuasive. A randomized trial of kidneys from donors after circulatory death found the approach safe and feasible but no better at preventing delayed graft function, with rates of 60.7 and 58.5 percent (Hosgood et al., 2023†). A 2026 single-center trial that added two hours of warm perfusion to cold perfusion found immediate graft function in 39 percent of treated kidneys against 49 percent of controls, with an interval so wide, 0.28 to 1.64, that it could not rule out benefit or harm (Normothermic versus hypothermic machine perfusion in kidney transplantation: A randomized controlled trial, 2026†). A 2025 review concluded that adding oxygen during cold perfusion has produced mixed results, and that the benefit of warm perfusion remains unproven (Nature Reviews Nephrology, 2025†). Untested is not the same as useless. It is untested.

## The molecule

The idea behind oxygen carriers is to add to the perfusion fluid a molecule that does the job red cells do, carrying oxygen, so that an organ cut off from circulation is not starved. A French group tested one such product, extracted from a marine worm and known as HEMO2life, in 60 transplanted kidneys at six centers, comparing them with the paired kidneys from the same donors. The investigators reported higher patient survival at 48 months, 98.3 percent against 86 percent, and the manufacturer reports lower delayed graft function in some subgroups (Source Notes S36). I would treat these claims with unusual caution. It is surprising for a preservation additive to change how long a recipient lives four years later, and surprising results are usually the start of an investigation. The design was not randomized, and the most favorable figures come from interested parties. A randomized trial is registered, and I found no results (ClinicalTrials.gov, n.d.).

The Cold Ischemia Foundation has published educational material about one company in this space, BHOC Therapeutics, and I owe the reader the Foundation's own stated position, which is stricter than any critic's. The company describes a cell-free hemoglobin carrier designed to work across blood groups. Its transplant program says plainly that it is a research concept, not an approved treatment, and that it has not been shown to prevent rejection or to change transplant outcomes (Cold Ischemia Foundation, n.d.-a). The Foundation's evidence brief notes that no oxygen carrier has full regulatory approval for intravenous use in humans (Cold Ischemia Foundation, n.d.-b). Interest is warranted. Promotion is not.

## The pig

The most startling development of the past two years is that a gene-edited pig kidney has worked, for months, inside a living person. In January 2025, a 66-year-old man at Massachusetts General Hospital, Tim Andrews, received a kidney from a pig engineered by the company eGenesis. He lived without dialysis for 271 days. An early episode of rejection, driven by T cells, responded to treatment, and no pig virus was detected. The kidney was removed in October 2025 after progressive dysfunction. In January 2026 he received a donated human kidney, becoming the first patient to move from a pig kidney to a human one. The case was reported in The Lancet on 3 September 2026 (Patient who received pig kidney becomes first to progress to human kidney transplant, 2026; Immune profiling in a living human recipient of a gene-edited pig kidney, 2025).

The company reported in September 2026 that five patients had received its kidney under an expanded-access program, that three had maintained kidney function for more than eight months without dialysis, that one had passed nine months, a record, and that two had gone on to human kidneys (eGenesis, 2026†). That update is a company statement and not peer-reviewed. The first formal trials are underway: United Therapeutics' gene-edited kidney, with ten genetic changes, was cleared by the Food and Drug Administration in February 2025 and its first trial transplant took place at NYU Langone on 3 November 2025 (United Therapeutics Corporation, 2025). The eGenesis kidney was cleared for a formal trial in September 2025, designed for dialysis-dependent adults aged 50 and older who are on the waiting list. I found no patient outcome data from either formal trial. A recent review in the American Journal of Transplantation takes stock of where the field stands (Xenotransplantation in living human recipients: Where we stand in 2026, 2026).

## What each would give back

| Machine | What it might return | How well do we know |
|---|---|---|
| Cold perfusion pump | Better early function and, perhaps, longer graft life | One large trial; ten-year follow-up reported by the manufacturer |
| Warm perfusion | A longer, safer time window for the organ | Two randomized trials; no benefit shown on main endpoints |
| Oxygen carriers | Organs better protected during the journey | Early human data, mostly from interested parties; research stage |
| Pig kidney | An organ on demand for people unlikely to get a human one | First formal trials under way; a handful of patients |
| Implantable or wearable artificial kidney | Freedom from the schedule | Animal studies; one small human device trial stopped early |

## The patience a machine requires

Thoreau asked why we should be in such desperate haste to succeed, and in such desperate enterprises. I read the question not as a counsel of delay but as a caution about motive. The people waiting have every reason to be desperate, and the companies and clinicians who build these machines have every reason to promise. A desperate patient and an eager inventor, left alone together, can produce either a miracle or a disaster. What separates the two is the dull discipline of evidence: trials designed in advance, results reported whatever they show, independent replication of what manufacturers announce.

Each of these machines, if it works, would return something the person in the chair counts as precious. Time. A quicker graft, a longer one, a pig kidney that needs no wait, a device that frees the schedule. I would not slow any of them down. I would ask only that we measure what they give back, in afternoons, and say so honestly when the answer is not yet.

# Chapter 12: A Kidney Is Not Forever
EPIGRAPH: "Hope is itself a species of happiness, and, perhaps, the chief happiness which this world affords."
BY: Samuel Johnson, The Rambler, No. 2 (1750)

There is a sentence people say to one another at transplant celebrations, and it is mostly true and slightly dangerous. "You've got your life back." It is true that a working kidney returns something enormous. It is dangerous because it leaves out the next sentence, which is that the kidney has a lifespan, and so, in a sense, does the return.

Picture a thirty-four-year-old woman, two years past her transplant, who has never felt better. She runs on Saturdays now. She is planning a trip. And on some quiet nights, at the edge of sleep, she does a calculation she has told no one about. Her kidney came from a deceased donor. She knows, in the way you know a fact you have chosen not to look at, that it will not last her whole life.

## The arithmetic of a graft

An analysis of national registry data, published in the American Journal of Transplantation in 2021, estimated that median graft survival for deceased-donor kidneys rose from 8.2 years for transplants performed in 1995 to 1999 to about 11.7 years in the most recent era, and that for living-donor kidneys it rose from 12.1 years to about 19.2 years for transplants in 2014 to 2017 (Source Notes S37). Those are medians, which means half of grafts last less. A conference abstract using the same registry reported projections for living-donor kidneys of well over twenty years, and noted that five-year and ten-year survival had improved too (Source Notes S37). Both sources use projections for the newest cohorts, and they disagree somewhat with each other, so I treat the figures as approximate.

The progress is real, and it is a quiet triumph. But look at the implication for a young recipient. If a kidney lasts a dozen years and a person lives sixty more, she will need more than one. The transplant is not an ending. It is the first chapter of a long relationship with a kind of medicine that she will have to renew. A second transplant means a second wait, and the waiting list, as the registry reminds us, is long.

## The drugs that hold it in place

A transplant works because the recipient takes drugs that quiet the immune system's natural wish to destroy a foreign organ. They are lifesaving, and they are not free of cost. They raise the risk of infection and of certain cancers, they must be taken exactly and for life, and they must be paid for. The previous generation of recipients simply accepted that bargain. The next might not need to.

In a clinical trial of an experimental cell therapy called MDR-101, recipients of kidneys from HLA-matched living siblings received an infusion of cells from the donor along with a conditioning regimen, in an attempt to teach the immune system to accept the kidney as its own. In a phase 3 study with 20 treated patients and 10 controls, interim results in 2023 showed that 16 of 19 patients were off immunosuppression at two years. A report in the American Journal of Transplantation in July 2025 described about 95 percent of the treated patients stopping all immunosuppression around a year after transplant, and 75 percent remaining free of it for more than two years. Four patients resumed drugs, for reasons including recurrent kidney disease and rejection, and none developed graft-versus-host disease (Source Notes S38). The sample is small, restricted to matched siblings, and the figures come from different time points, and I found no 2026 news on regulatory approval.

A transplant physician interviewed in April 2026 described the broader field as moving toward cell therapies, with trials combining stem cells and regulatory T cells in living-donor recipients and a randomized study of whether patients can be safely moved to a single immunosuppressive drug (Transplant tolerance: The frontier of kidney care, 2026†). If it succeeds, the result would be something transplant medicine has chased for sixty years: a kidney that the body treats as its own, which is to say tolerance.

## What tolerance means

The word "tolerance" is a clinical term, and I want to take it back for a moment. To tolerate something is to live peacefully alongside it. A person with a tolerated kidney does not carry it as a visitor to be guarded against. She carries it as part of herself. There is a quiet existential difference between taking a drug every day to prevent a rejection and living without that daily reminder that the body is in a negotiation.

Samuel Johnson wrote that hope is itself a species of happiness. He also warned, in the sentence that follows the one most often quoted, that the excesses of hope must be paid for in pain. The woman running on Saturdays knows both halves. A transplant is a gift of years, not a gift of forever. The right response is not to dampen her hope but to meet it with honesty. Tell her the numbers. Tell her about the second chapter. Tell her what is being built for it. And let her go running.
