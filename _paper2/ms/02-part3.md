# Chapter 7: The Long Chain
EPIGRAPH: "The only gift is a portion of thyself."
BY: Ralph Waldo Emerson, "Gifts," Essays: Second Series (1844)

Picture two couples who have never met. In the first, a husband needs a kidney and his wife wants to give him one, but their blood types do not match. In the second, the same story, the same love, the same incompatibility, in a different state. Each pair is stuck. Each has a willing donor who cannot help the person she most wants to help.

Then someone does something clever. The first wife's kidney goes to the second husband. The second wife's kidney goes to the first husband. Two transplants happen where none could. Nobody has been asked to give to a stranger, and everyone has, without quite noticing.

That is the simplest form of kidney paired donation, and it has become one of the quiet marvels of modern medicine. It is also a puzzle with an almost unbearably human structure, because the longer you let it run, the more it begins to resemble something older than medicine.

## The chain that starts with a stranger

Now add a person who walks in with no one in particular to help. A non-directed donor, sometimes called an altruistic donor, gives a kidney to a stranger. That first gift can unlock a pair that had been stuck, whose donor then gives to the next pair, whose donor gives to the next, and on it goes, each link a donor who gives only because someone else gave first. The chain can end by sending its last kidney to a person on the deceased-donor waiting list, so that nobody is left with a debt unpaid.

In 2009, the New England Journal of Medicine described a ten-transplant chain begun by a single altruistic donor in July 2007, coordinated over eight months by two registries, across six hospitals in five states. The authors reported something touching in its plainness: no one reneged (Rees et al., 2009). Every donor, having seen her loved one saved, went through with her own operation, though she had nothing left to gain. Think about that. The chain depends on each link doing a hard thing after the incentive is gone.

Chains have grown longer since. The National Kidney Registry has described one that began with a non-directed donor and ran across 17 hospitals in 11 states over four months, ending with a thirtieth transplant, a record at the time (Source Notes S23). A chain at the University of Alabama at Birmingham that has been reported as the longest at a single center has been described in different announcements as 74 donors and 74 recipients and as reaching 100 or more recipients (Nation's longest single-site kidney chain reaches 100, n.d.; Source Notes S24). The sources disagree on the current total because they count different things, completed versus ongoing chains, single-center versus multicenter, transplants versus surgeries. I will not pretend to a precision the record lacks.

## Scale

What began as a curiosity is now a meaningful share of the field. The National Kidney Registry announced its 10,000th living-donor transplant, with 1,744 facilitated in 2024 alone, and projected that it would account for roughly 30 percent of all living-donor kidney transplants in the United States in 2025. In one recent month, its 104 member centers together facilitated a record 179 transplants, surpassing the 157 set in July 2024 (National Kidney Registry facilitates 10,000th living donor kidney transplant, n.d.). These are the organization's own figures, and they should be read as such, but the trend is not in doubt.

## What the chain teaches

I find three lessons in this machinery, and none of them is technical.

The first is that generosity can be organized without being cheapened. The registry's software matches blood types and antibodies, but the heart of the thing is a promise kept by strangers. Emerson wrote that "the only gift is a portion of thyself," and he meant that a gift worthy of the name costs the giver something of her own. A living kidney donor gives a literal portion of herself. A system that honors that must resist treating her as a supply.

The second is that reciprocity, at its best, travels forward and not back. The old moral economy of favor owed and favor repaid was a dyad. The chain makes it a line. You cannot repay the person who gave to your husband, because she gave because someone gave before her. You can only pass it on, and the person you pass it to cannot repay you either. A line of debts that nobody collects is the clearest definition of grace I know.

The third is a conservative one. The chain is built from voluntary acts, and its strength comes from that. Remove the voluntariness, by compulsion or by bribery, and something essential would be lost, though reasonable people differ about where the line falls, as the next chapter will show. What the chain needs from policy is not control but care: reliable logistics, clear information, protection against financial harm to the donors, and a refusal to let the whole thing be turned into a marketplace.

# Chapter 8: The Receipt
EPIGRAPH: "The cost of a thing is the amount of what I will call life which is required to be exchanged for it, immediately or in the long run."
BY: Henry David Thoreau, Walden (1854)

Imagine a woman who has decided to give a kidney to her brother-in-law. She has been evaluated, approved, and scheduled. Then she sits at her kitchen table with a pencil, the way people do before a big decision, and begins to write down what it will cost.

The medical bills are covered. That part she has been told, and it is true. But there are things the bills do not include. Gas and a motel for the evaluation trips, and for a second trip, and a third. The two weeks she will miss from work, unpaid, since her employer offers no leave she can use. The child care for the week she is in the hospital. The elder care for her mother. The friend who will take the dog. When she is done the list runs to the bottom of the page, and she looks at it for a long while. She will probably give anyway. Not everyone does.

This is a composite. It is also the situation of a large number of people who might otherwise give.

## A decline with a pattern

The count of living kidney donors in the United States has recovered somewhat from its pandemic dip, but it has not recovered to where it stood before. One news report, drawing on registry data, put the number of living kidney donors at 6,522 in 2025, up from 6,419 in 2024 and 6,290 in 2023, and still below the 6,867 of 2019 (PhillyVoice, 2026†). I classify that as a single-source figure and urge anyone who quotes it to check the registry. An older analysis described a longer decline after a peak in 2004, most pronounced among men, Black adults, and younger and lower-income adults (Source Notes S25). The pattern is not only American. A 2026 article reports that Australia performed 253 living-donor kidney transplants in 2024, down from 354 in 2008, with people from lower socioeconomic groups disadvantaged (Source Notes S26).

When a decline appears in more than one country and concentrates among those with the least cushion, it is reasonable to suspect that the cause is not a failure of kindness. A 2015 consensus conference of transplant professionals reached that conclusion a decade ago, naming the financial burden on donors as a possible source of the decline, and recommending standardized reimbursement for lost wages and incidental costs along with legal protection for employment and insurability (Tushla et al., 2015†).

## What changed in 2026

In February 2026 Congress acted. The Honor Our Living Donors Act, part of that year's appropriations law, took away a rule that had always seemed backward: the federal reimbursement program could previously consider the recipient's household income when deciding whether a donor qualified for help. Under the new law, the recipient's income no longer counts, and the donor's own circumstances determine priority. Eligible donors may receive up to $6,000 per organ for travel, meals, lost wages, and child and elder care, and the program is now required to report to Congress each year on how much it spent, how many donors it reimbursed, and how many applied without being reimbursed (Source Notes S27).

A physicians' group, in a funding letter, said that 21 percent of donors in the program reach the $6,000 cap (Source Notes S27). That detail deserves attention. If one in five donors exhausts the ceiling, the ceiling is binding for a meaningful minority, and the woman at the kitchen table with her list may be one of them. In July 2026 the federal health-resources agency requested public comment on the rules (Health Resources and Services Administration, 2026a†). The final shape of the program is still being written.

State law is a patchwork. The American Kidney Fund's report card in May 2026 found gains in several states and little or no progress in many others, and reported that 28 states have laws guarding living donors against insurance discrimination, though protection can be lost if a donor moves (American Kidney Fund, 2026). Private programs fill some gaps, such as wage reimbursement and life insurance offered through the paired-donation registry of the last chapter (Source Notes S28).

## The harder question

There is a bolder proposal before Congress, and it deserves a fair hearing. The End Kidney Deaths Act, introduced in 2025, would create a refundable federal tax credit of $10,000 a year for five years, a total of $50,000, for people who donate a kidney to a stranger, and it would sunset after ten years (End Kidney Deaths Act, 2025). It was still in committee as of spring 2026, and a policy tracker rated its chances as low (Source Notes S29).

Honest people are divided, and they are divided for good reasons. The case against is that payment could draw poor people into risks they would otherwise decline, that it might crowd out the altruism that gives a gift its meaning, and that a system built on the idea that organs are given, not sold, might lose public trust if that idea blurred. A federal law, the National Organ Transplant Act of 1984, prohibits the transfer of organs for valuable consideration, and the bill's designers chose a tax credit to respect that principle while changing the incentive (National Organ Transplant Act, 1984). The case for is that the status quo already asks donors to pay, and that the waiting list is a place where people die.

I have a modest view, and it is a conservative one in the sense of caution. Whatever one concludes about payment, no one should object to ending the receipt. A society that asks a person to give an organ to a family member or a stranger should not also ask her to subsidize the operation. Reimbursement that is complete, simple, and not means-tested for the recipient is a debt of ordinary decency. After that, if anyone wishes to test an incentive, it should be tested as an experiment, with outcomes defined in advance and independent evaluation. Policy without evidence is a wager made with other people's lives. Thoreau's accounting is the right one. The price of a thing is the life it costs. The donor deserves to know the price, and to be told it will not be charged to her twice.

# Chapter 9: The Hallway
EPIGRAPH: "It is better to go to the house of mourning, than to go to the house of feasting: for that is the end of all men; and the living will lay it to his heart."
BY: Ecclesiastes 7:2 (King James Version, 1611)

Picture a hallway outside an intensive-care unit, late at night. The lights have been dimmed to the amber of a hotel corridor. A family stands at the end of it, close together, not speaking much. Behind a door a person they love is being kept alive by machines, and a doctor has just explained, as gently as such things can be explained, that the person will not recover. A second person has now appeared, quieter and softer-spoken, wearing a lanyard. She is the one who will ask the question.

I have spent much of this book on people whose lives depend on a gift. Here I want to spend time with the people who give it, who are not the recipients and not the donors in any operating-room sense, but the families of those who have died. They are, in the end, the ones on whom the entire deceased-donor system rests, and we know remarkably little about how they fare.

## What the research shows, and how thin it is

An interview study of 49 potential donor families in Australia found that no family who agreed to donation regretted the decision, while some who declined did. The process was often long and stressful, but not enough to reduce satisfaction with the outcome (Source Notes S30). A review of the literature reached a subtler conclusion: dissatisfaction with how hospital staff behaved was more likely than the donation itself to worsen families' grief, and the aggravating factors included lack of privacy, a feeling of being pressured, and fear of not honoring the wishes of the person who had died (Source Notes S30).

Support after the fact is uneven. A 15-country European inventory found that about half of respondents thought aftercare for donor families could be improved, and that anonymous written contact between donor families and recipients is possible in almost all countries, with direct contact permitted in only a few (Source Notes S31). An analysis of 1,613 letters over seven years, presented as a conference abstract, found that recipients wrote more often than donor families and usually wrote first, that recipients' letters were mostly about gratitude, and that donor families' letters were mostly about the person who had died (Source Notes S31). A Southern California procurement organization reported that consent rose after it created a family services and aftercare department in 2002, though it is an abstract and cannot establish that the department caused the rise (Source Notes S31).

What strikes me about this literature is how small it is. For a practice that touches thousands of families a year and whose entire legitimacy depends on their trust, there is surprisingly little research on how those families fare over time. The reviews say as much.

## What happened to trust

The trust on which all of this depends has been tested. In the autumn of 2024, news coverage of a Kentucky case, in which a patient was described by his family as showing signs of life as procurement was prepared, prompted a surge of people removing themselves from donor registries. The national registry reported an average of 170 removals a day in the following week, ten times the rate of the same week a year earlier, a count that did not include emailed requests or state registries. The head of the national association of procurement organizations said that organ donation is based on public trust, and that once eroded, it takes years to regain (Associated Press, 2024).

In March 2025 the federal oversight agency completed a report on its investigation of the Kentucky organization. It reviewed about 350 cases in which a planned recovery had been authorized but not completed. In 73 of them it concluded that officials should have reconsidered sooner, because the patients' level of consciousness was high or improving. In at least 28 cases, 8.0 percent, the records showed no documented time of cardiac death, which the agency described as suggesting potential survival to hospital discharge (Health Resources and Services Administration, Division of Transplantation, 2025). Let me be careful in how I say this, because the wording matters. It is a finding about documentation and judgment, serious in its implications, and not a finding that twenty-eight living people underwent removal. The organization has disputed characterizations of its practices, and I treat the most publicized individual account as an allegation. A summary issued by the Department of Health and Human Services in July 2025 stated that concerns were identified in 103 of 351 cases (Source Notes S32).

The response was substantial. The agency required a root-cause analysis and a policy allowing any staff member to halt a donation, directed the national network to report every pause for a safety concern, and noted that it had received reports of similar patterns elsewhere (Health Resources and Services Administration, Division of Transplantation, 2025). In September 2025 federal officials decertified a Miami procurement organization in mid-cycle, citing unsafe practices, inadequate training, chronic underperformance, understaffing, and paperwork errors, the first action of its kind (Fierce Healthcare, 2025). In 2026 the national network's board adopted new requirements for donation after circulatory death, which the oversight agency described as strengthening protections for potential donors (Health Resources and Services Administration, 2026b).

## The fence

G. K. Chesterton left a parable that conservatives are fond of citing, and this seems the right place to cite it. A man comes upon a fence across a road, and a reformer says he does not see the use of it and proposes to clear it away. The wiser reformer answers that if you do not see the use of it, you may not clear it away. Go away and think. When you can come back and tell me you do see the use, I may allow you to destroy it (Chesterton, 1929).

The voluntary donation of organs after death is such a fence. It rests on a delicate, unwritten understanding that those who give are not being used. Every reform, whether a new metric, a new tier, or a new payment model, is a hand on that fence. The events of 2024 and 2025 are best read as a reminder of why the fence was built. A donation system survives on its reputation for being, first, a system that does no harm.

I do not think the answer is to make donation harder to carry out. I think the answer is to make the process legible to the family in the hallway: who determines death, who is empowered to stop, what happens next, and who will call in a month. The people in the hallway are keeping a promise on behalf of someone who can no longer keep it himself. They deserve a system that behaves as though it knows that.
