# Chapter 13: The Pharmacy Counter
EPIGRAPH: "The rich ruleth over the poor, and the borrower is servant to the lender."
BY: Proverbs 22:7 (King James Version, 1611)

Picture a man in a pharmacy line on the first of the month, holding a paper bag he has not yet been allowed to take home. The pharmacist is explaining, gently, that the price has changed. His coverage reset. He is not a careless man. He keeps a spreadsheet. He knows exactly how much he has left after rent, and he knows that the pills in the bag are the only thing standing between his transplanted kidney and the machine he left behind.

This scene is a composite. It describes a gate that no one thinks of as a gate, and that has ended more transplants than most surgical complications.

## The cliff that was repaired

For decades, a transplant recipient whose only qualifying condition was kidney failure lost Medicare coverage 36 months after surgery, unless disabled or over sixty-five. The anti-rejection drugs did not become cheaper on the thirty-seventh month. Since December 2022 a benefit called Medicare Part B-ID has covered FDA-approved immunosuppressive drugs indefinitely, for a premium that was $97.10 a month in 2023 (Source Notes S39). A federal analysis in 2019 had observed that, for patients who lost coverage and had no other source, the cost of continuing therapy could be prohibitive. Congressional testimony cited a report that nearly 70 percent of transplant programs had experienced a death or a lost transplant because a patient could not afford the drugs, a figure that reached me only through that testimony (Source Notes S39). A cost-effectiveness analysis found that extending coverage saved Medicare money while improving outcomes. It is one of those rare policies in which compassion and arithmetic agreed, and it took years to enact.

I could find no peer-reviewed study of how the new benefit has changed patients' out-of-pocket burden or their financial distress after the thirty-sixth month. A repair whose effect nobody has measured has not been finished.

## The new uncertainty

The next risk is not a cliff that has been built but one that has been forecast, and I want to describe it with restraint. The 2025 federal budget reconciliation law changed Medicaid in ways that advocacy organizations expect to matter to kidney patients. The National Kidney Foundation, citing the Congressional Budget Office, said that more than 11 million people would lose Medicaid coverage and that more than $700 billion in funding would be removed over the budget period. It reports that nearly half of dialysis patients rely on Medicaid (National Kidney Foundation, 2025). A nephrology society newsletter noted that a transplant can cost more than $400,000 and that immunosuppressive drugs afterward cost $10,000 to $14,000 a year (American Society of Nephrology, 2025). A fact brief in August 2026 said that work requirements of 80 hours a month are scheduled to begin in January 2027, with exceptions for some groups, and that eligibility must be rechecked every six months starting by December 2026 (Gigafact, 2026†).

What I do not know is how many dialysis or transplant patients will lose coverage, whether exceptions will protect them, or how immunosuppressant coverage will fare. These are forecasts from advocacy sources and one fact brief, and the enacted text should be read directly. I say so because it would be easy, and wrong, to write a sentence that sounds like a prophecy.

## Independence is a medical outcome

There is a conservative principle in this chapter that has nothing to do with the budget. Proverbs observes that the borrower is servant to the lender. A person who must ask for permission, every month, to take the medicine that keeps her kidney alive is not fully free, and she knows it. The dignity of self-reliance, which conservatives rightly honor, is poorly served by a system that sets a person up to depend on institutions she cannot influence. Whatever one's view of the proper size of public programs, an arrangement that makes the continuity of a transplant depend on a bureaucratic reset date is not a conservative arrangement. It is merely fragile.

The remedies are not exotic. Measure the problem, by asking recipients each year whether they have missed doses for cost. Offer a free navigation call before the thirty-sixth month and before any change in coverage. And design coverage rules with an explicit exception for people whose survival depends on a daily drug, because the cost of that exception is trivial beside the cost of a lost graft.

# Chapter 14: Who Is Keeping Count
EPIGRAPH: "One of the essential qualities of the clinician is interest in humanity, for the secret of the care of the patient is in caring for the patient."
BY: Francis W. Peabody, "The Care of the Patient," JAMA (1927)

Picture an administrator, late on a Thursday, scrolling through a dashboard with forty-one metrics. Door-to-needle times, readmission rates, graft survival at one year, transplant volumes, compliance with the latest policy. The numbers are carefully defined and the colors mostly green. Nothing on the screen records that on Tuesday a woman named in no database sat for four hours, felt washed out for three more, and missed her granddaughter's recital.

That is not a failure of the dashboard. It is a description of it. A measurement system is a theory of what matters, and it is worth reading a system's metrics the way you would read a person's calendar.

## What is counted

Consider the instruments that now govern American kidney care. A payment model for dialysis facilities, the one I described in Chapter 5, rewards home dialysis and transplantation. A newer model for transplant hospitals, which began on 1 July 2025 and runs to mid-2031, scores each participating hospital out of 100: 60 points for the number of transplants it performs, 20 for how readily it accepts organ offers, and 20 for how long its grafts survive. A hospital can earn up to $15,000 per Medicare kidney transplant and be charged at most $2,000, and in the first year there is no downside risk at all. As of this writing no first-year results have been published (Centers for Medicare & Medicaid Services, n.d.). A third instrument sorts the nation's 55 organ procurement organizations into tiers on the basis of two rates, a donation rate and a transplant rate, with the lowest tier facing loss of its territory. The first recertification cycle ended in July 2026, a final rule is expected in late 2026, and contracts of organizations in the lowest tier remain in force through 31 January 2027 (Holland & Knight, 2026). I found no published roster of which organizations those are.

The governing body of the whole network is in the middle of rebuilding itself. In April 2026 its board extended its president's and vice president's terms by a year, saying that contractor transitions and funding interruptions had impaired its ability to move initiatives forward (Health Resources and Services Administration, 2026c). It later raised the fee charged for each registered candidate to $1,268 for fiscal year 2027, about 22 percent more than the year before, and ended one of its standing committees (Health Resources and Services Administration, 2026d). All of this comes from the agency's own summaries, and I found no independent press coverage that tested them.

## What patients named

Now set that list beside the one in Chapter 1. When patients and caregivers were asked what mattered, they rated the ability to travel, time free from dialysis, the adequacy of treatment, and feeling washed out higher than clinicians did (Developing a set of core outcomes for trials in hemodialysis, 2017†). Read side by side, the two lists barely touch. The system counts volume, acceptance, survival, and compliance. The patients named travel, time, and how they feel in the evening.

I do not say that the system's measures are wrong. Volume matters, survival matters, and holding monopolies to account matters. I say that the instruments are incomplete in a specific way, and that the incompleteness lands on the people with the most at stake.

## The law of the good metric

The anthropologist Marilyn Strathern put a warning in one sentence that has since been widely repeated: when a measure becomes a target, it ceases to be a good measure (Strathern, 1997). Every metric in this chapter is vulnerable to it. A volume score can be raised by transplanting those who would have done well anyway. A graft-survival score can be protected by declining difficult cases. An acceptance score can be improved by accepting what should be declined. The remedy is not to abandon measurement, which would be a disaster for the people who most need it. It is to measure in groups, so that anyone who inflates one number reveals themselves in another, and to include in the group the things that cannot be gamed because they are reported by the patient.

## A plain way to tell noise from news

Industry has a small tool for exactly this problem, called a control chart. It plots a measure over time and draws bands around its ordinary range, so that you can tell a real change from the normal wobble. Imagine applying it to the share of recovered kidneys that go unused. That figure was 18.2 percent in 2013, 26.6 percent in 2022, 27.9 percent in 2023, and 29.3 percent in 2024 (Scientific Registry of Transplant Recipients & Organ Procurement and Transplantation Network, 2025, 2026a). No chart is needed to see that this is not wobble. But the nation publishes such numbers once a year, years late, and a number published once a year cannot reveal a drift that begins in March.

## A dashboard a patient could read

Suppose we began over. I would ask a few hundred patients and care partners to name the five things they most wanted measured, and I would measure those. Time in the chair and in recovery. Distance traveled. Days at home. How much the care partner is carrying. Whether medicine was ever skipped for cost. I would publish them monthly and plainly. Peabody wrote in 1927 that the secret of the care of the patient is in caring for the patient, and the sentence is easy to quote and hard to operationalize. A dashboard that begins with the patient's own list is as close as I know to a way of doing it.

# Chapter 15: What a Conservative Would Keep
EPIGRAPH: "If you don't see the use of it, I certainly won't let you clear it away. Go away and think. Then, when you can come back and tell me that you do see the use of it, I may allow you to destroy it."
BY: G. K. Chesterton, The Thing (1929)

I have called this book's second register conservative, and I owe the reader an account of what I mean, because the word has drifted a long way from its roots. A conservative, in the tradition I have in mind, is not someone who opposes change. Burke insisted that a state without the means of some change is without the means of its own conservation. A conservative is someone who asks, before changing a thing, what it is for.

Seen that way, the American kidney ecosystem looks different than it does to either its defenders or its critics. It is an assembly of institutions, most of them remarkable, some of them decaying, a few of them invisible until they vanish. The conservative's first duty is to inventory what is worth keeping.

## What I would keep

**The gift.** The entire edifice rests on the free decision of families to give, and of living people to part with a piece of themselves. Chesterton, in his book *Orthodoxy*, described tradition as giving a vote to the most obscure of all classes, our ancestors, and called it the democracy of the dead (Chesterton, 1908). In transplantation the dead do vote, in a literal way. Every deceased donor is a person whose last act was a decision, honored by a family. Any reform that makes that decision feel less safe or less honored has spent capital that cannot easily be earned back. That is the lesson of Chapter 9.

**The near.** The hospital coordinator who knows the unit. The rural dialysis unit that holds a county in place. The nurse who knows the names of a patient's children. The friend trained to ask on someone's behalf. Wherever I found success in this book, it came from institutions small enough to be recognized. A policy that cannot see them will tend to wear them down.

**The household.** The kitchen table, the care partner, the home machine. Policy should treat the family as the central unit of care it already is, and stop pretending that unpaid labor is free.

**The right to say no.** The conservative tradition honors conscience and the individual's authority over her own life. A system that cannot offer, and honestly describe, the choice to forgo dialysis has failed to honor it.

## What I would change

A conservative is not a defender of everything. The things I would change are mostly the things nobody can see.

*Count the hours.* Put the patient's time, travel, recovery, and care partner strain on the dashboard next to the metrics already there. Chapters 1, 5, and 14.

*Say it early.* Tell people about kidney disease while choices are still wide, and about every option, including transplantation, home therapy, and conservative care. Chapters 3 and 4.

*End the receipt.* No living donor should pay to give. Chapter 8.

*Keep the unit open.* Treat the closure of a local dialysis unit as a failure to be investigated and not an outcome to be shrugged at. Chapter 6.

*Give the decider a map.* Standard information, a travel-time projection, and a second look for the kidney declined again and again. Chapter 10.

*Count what is promised.* Require independent replication of what manufacturers announce, and report what each new machine gives back in time. Chapter 11.

*Be honest about the graft.* Tell recipients how long a kidney lasts, plan for the second chapter, and measure whether the drugs are being skipped for cost. Chapters 12 and 13.

*Make the family's hallway legible.* Explain who determines death, who can halt a donation, and who will call in a month. Chapter 9.

## What would change my mind

A person who argues for reform should say what would make her retreat. If patients, asked what they want measured, named things I have not imagined, I would change the dashboard to match. If a well-designed trial showed that a technology I have praised gives back no time, I would say so. If a pilot of home dialysis showed that it merely moved burden onto families who could not bear it, I would withdraw my enthusiasm for it. And if the people who actually sit in the chairs told me that the whole frame of this book, the arithmetic of hours, misses what they need, I would ask them to tell me what it is.

## The last conservative argument

Burke's phrase about the little platoon is often taken to mean that the small is good and the large is bad. I think it means something more exact. The large things in life, a nation, a health system, a profession, are made of small things, and they are only as trustworthy as the small things are. The kidney ecosystem is not broken because it lacks a grand design. It is strained because the small things it depends on, the household, the coordinator, the unit, the volunteer, the family in the hallway, have been asked to carry more than they were ever built to carry, with less recognition than they deserve.

The fence, in Chesterton's story, is still standing. The wise reformer goes away and thinks, and when she comes back she can say what the fence is for. What is it for? It is for keeping the people on the road from harm. That is a good thing for a fence to be for. Our task is to make sure it still does.

# Epilogue: A Tuesday Worth Wanting
EPIGRAPH: "How vain it is to sit down to write when you have not stood up to live."
BY: Henry David Thoreau, Journal (1851)

Picture Ruth again, a few years from now, in a story I cannot promise. It is Tuesday. She did not go to the chair this morning, because there is no chair. Perhaps a kidney arrived. Perhaps her treatment moved to her kitchen. Perhaps a friend she trained to ask for her did the asking. I do not know how it happened, and she would tell you the how matters less than the what. It is one o'clock in the afternoon, and she is doing something unimportant. She is deciding whether to make soup.

Nothing in the data in this book predicts that Tuesday for any individual. What the data do show is that the ingredients exist. Medicines that keep the silence from lasting. Machines that fit in a kitchen. Chains of strangers who give. Cells that may teach the body peace. Pig kidneys that have run for months. Programs that train friends. Laws that, belatedly, began to reimburse donors for their generosity. The ingredients are not yet a meal, and many of them will not survive a clinical trial. But they exist, which was not true a generation ago.

What remains is a decision about what we are building toward. I have argued that the answer is time, and I have tried to say so without sentiment, because sentiment is not what the person in the chair needs. She needs the afternoon back. She needs the care partner counted, the local unit kept open, the cost of the pill known, the choice of whether to dialyze at all offered honestly, and a kidney, when one comes, that lasts.

I have sat in rooms where the chord of the machines hums and no one talks. I have learned that silence is not emptiness. It is attention, spent on one another. The same attention, scaled up, is the only thing that has ever made a system humane. We have a great many instruments in this field. What we need is to be a little more present in the room.

I will leave you with a question, which I ask of myself as often as I ask it of anyone. If you were given a certain number of afternoons, what would you want them for? And when you know the answer, what would you ask of the people who look after you, to help you spend them?
