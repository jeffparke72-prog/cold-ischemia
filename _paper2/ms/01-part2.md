# Chapter 4: The Disease No One Feels
EPIGRAPH: "For in much wisdom is much grief: and he that increaseth knowledge increaseth sorrow."
BY: Ecclesiastes 1:18 (King James Version, 1611)

Kidney disease has an unusual talent for silence. A failing heart announces itself with breathlessness, a failing lung with every flight of stairs. A failing kidney can go about its decline for years without producing a single symptom a person would think to mention. By the time it begins to hurt, in the sense of fatigue and swelling and a taste like metal in the mouth, a great deal has already been lost. Nothing hurts until nearly everything does.

I think of this as the cruelest feature of the disease, and also the most hopeful, because silence is a problem that information can address.

## How many, and how few know

The Centers for Disease Control and Prevention estimated, in a national page updated in March 2026, that 14 percent of American adults, about 37 million people, have chronic kidney disease (Centers for Disease Control and Prevention, 2026). The numbers that follow are older, and I give them as context only. An earlier CDC analysis, reported at the time in the trade press, found that 96 percent of people with early-stage disease did not know they had it, and that 48 percent of those with severely reduced function were unaware and not yet on dialysis (Kidney disease affects 1 in 7 Americans, says CDC, n.d.). The American Kidney Fund cites 35.5 million Americans and says nine in ten are unaware, a figure that is widely repeated, though I could not find the calculation behind it (Source Notes S11). A study from the 2020s of patients with moderately to severely reduced function found that roughly half were unaware, and the authors observed that the gap has persisted for nearly two decades (Source Notes S12).

These figures disagree at the edges, and that is the honest state of knowledge. What they agree about is the shape: a large population, most of it unaware, discovering the disease late. A test that costs little exists. Measuring albumin in the urine is one of the key ways to detect early damage, and yet a study cited in commentary on one major trial found that among adults with type 2 diabetes receiving primary care at 24 U.S. health organizations, the median rate of such testing was only 52.9 percent (Diabetic kidney disease therapies keep flowing, n.d.). The people most likely to be harmed by diabetes were being checked about half the time.

## The most hopeful paragraph in the book

If the first half of this chapter is about silence, the second half is about something that has begun to change it. The FLOW trial, a large randomized study, was the first dedicated kidney-outcomes trial of a once-weekly injectable drug of the GLP-1 class, semaglutide, in people with type 2 diabetes and chronic kidney disease. A later analysis found that the benefit held whether or not participants were also taking an SGLT2 inhibitor, a different class of drug that professional guidelines now recommend as first-line treatment for most adults with diabetes and kidney disease (Effects of semaglutide with and without concomitant SGLT2 inhibitor use in participants with type 2 diabetes and chronic kidney disease in the FLOW trial, 2024†).

I will resist the temptation to promise what these drugs will do for the population. I found no study that measured how many eligible patients are actually receiving them, and a promise built on prescriptions nobody has counted is a rumor. But I will say what the pattern implies. For most of the history of kidney medicine, the options on offer began at the point of failure. Now, for the first time, there are tools aimed at the long quiet stretch before it. The kidney that never fails is the most patient-centered innovation imaginable, since it spares a person the entire apparatus of what comes after.

## The chair you never sit in

The second way of avoiding the chair is to skip it. A preemptive transplant takes place before dialysis ever begins. It is the goal many clinicians would choose if they could, and in practice it remains uncommon. One analysis of transplants from 2003 to 2012 found that only 17 percent of recipients overall, and 31 percent of recipients of living-donor kidneys, received one before dialysis. Another reported that only 2.5 percent of people whose kidneys failed in 2015 received a preemptive transplant as their first treatment (Source Notes S13). A 2025 study using national transplant data reminded readers that the survival benefit of very early preemptive transplantation remains debated.

One quiet policy story shows how a single number can decide whether a person gets to skip the chair. For years, a formula used to estimate kidney function included an adjustment based on race, which made the kidneys of Black patients look healthier than they were. Because a transplant candidate's waiting time could begin only when the estimate fell below a threshold, the formula had the effect of starting the clock later for Black candidates. In 2023 the national transplant network required centers to correct this by backdating waiting time for affected candidates, those who would have crossed the threshold on a race-neutral calculation (Organ Procurement and Transplantation Network, n.d.). By July of that year, 6,103 modifications had been completed, with a median of 1.7 years of additional time awarded, and the share of Black candidates transplanted before dialysis had risen from 11.7 to 13.9 percent, a preliminary finding the authors cautioned might reflect broader trends (6 month waiting time modification and 1 year race-neutral eGFR monitoring report, n.d.). A later quasi-experimental analysis of more than 181,000 adults, reported in March 2026, found that over 21,000 candidates had received added time between January 2023 and June 2025 and that the policy was associated with 5.3 additional transplants per 1,000 listings among Black candidates, in both the pre-dialysis and the on-dialysis groups (Source Notes S14).

I find this story moving for a reason that has nothing to do with politics. It shows that a measuring stick can quietly steal years, that the theft can be undone once someone looks at the stick, and that undoing it gave real people real kidneys sooner. It also illustrates something conservatives understand well: that an institution's rules deserve respect, and that respect includes the willingness to repair them when they turn out to be wrong.

## What knowing buys

The book of Ecclesiastes warns that increasing knowledge increases sorrow, and a person told at fifty that her kidneys are failing may well agree. But she is also handed a choice. She can act on the diabetes, the blood pressure, the medications, the diet, the family conversation about donation, the question of whether she would ever want the chair. The silence of kidney disease is not a kindness. It is a theft of time during which choices are still wide. The most patient-centered thing a health system can do is give people the news while they can still do something about it, and give it gently.

# Chapter 5: Dialysis at the Kitchen Table
EPIGRAPH: "Home is the place where, when you have to go there, / They have to take you in."
BY: Robert Frost, "The Death of the Hired Man" (1914)

Picture a man in his late fifties, standing in his own kitchen at six in the morning, doing something he would have thought impossible a year earlier. He is washing his hands for the third time. Beside the counter sits a machine about the size of a carry-on bag, and he has just threaded a pair of needles into the vein access in his forearm, a task he was taught over four weeks by a nurse who never once made him feel stupid. His wife is making coffee behind him. She has learned to watch the readout, and to hear a small change in the sound.

The scene is a composite. For a growing minority of people with kidney failure, it is also simply true.

## The slow rise of the house

Home dialysis, which includes peritoneal dialysis done with a catheter and a sterile fluid, and home hemodialysis done with a compact machine, gives people something the clinic cannot: control of the schedule. A person can dialyze at night while sleeping, or for shorter sessions more often, or in the afternoon after the grandchildren leave. Recall what patients named as most important in Chapter 1. Ability to travel, time free from dialysis, and not feeling washed out are the things a home schedule can sometimes give back.

The uptake has been slow. A study using national registry data found that the share of newly diagnosed patients starting on home dialysis rose from 6.8 percent in 2010 to 13.3 percent in 2020, a pace the authors judged too slow to reach the federal goal of having 80 percent of new patients on home dialysis or a transplant by 2025 (Source Notes S15). A 2025 market report counted about 433,400 patients treated in centers and about 78,400 treated at home as of March 31, 2025, which by my arithmetic puts the home share near 15 percent of that combined group, though that is not an official national figure (Matthews, 2025). For home hemodialysis specifically, a manufacturer announcement citing 2022 figures put the share at only 2.4 percent of roughly 550,000 people on dialysis (FDA clears Quanta home hemodialysis system, n.d.).

## What the incentives did, and did not do

The federal government tried to nudge the numbers. Under a payment model called ESRD Treatment Choices, dialysis facilities in randomly selected regions, 95 of them, covering 2,591 facilities and 141,890 Medicare fee-for-service beneficiaries, about a third of the Medicare ESRD population, received payment adjustments tied to rates of home dialysis and transplantation (Centers for Medicare & Medicaid Services, 2025). The first-year evaluation reported about 546 additional patient-months of home-dialysis training, a 9 percent increase, and 225 additional transplants, a 10 percent increase, but no increase in the actual use of home dialysis (CMS releases first-year results of ESRD Treatment Choices model, 2023).

The third-year summary is harder to read. It says that through 2023 the model led to an increase in home-dialysis training and in overall transplant rates, driven by deceased-donor transplants. Elsewhere the same summary says the model did not increase home-dialysis rates, transplant waitlisting, or transplant rates, and that it had no effect on Medicare spending or quality (CMS, 2025). Those statements appear to conflict about transplants, and I could not reach the technical report that would resolve the tension. CMS also proposed in 2025 to end the model early, at the close of that year, and I could not confirm the final decision. What I can say is that financial nudges to facilities moved training more than they moved lives. That is a lesson about where the bottleneck lies. Training is a thing a clinic does. Living at home with a machine is a thing a family does.

## The machines that might change the conversation

The dream behind all of this is a device that treats kidney failure without tying a person to a schedule at all. Two efforts show how far the dream is from the kitchen. A wearable artificial kidney, a belt-worn device that filtered blood continuously, was tested in a small human trial. It was well tolerated and cleared waste effectively, but the trial was stopped after the seventh participant because of technical problems with the device, and the authors concluded it would need redesign (Source Notes S16). At the University of California, San Francisco, a team demonstrated in 2023 that kidney cells housed in an implantable bioreactor could survive in a pig, perform several kidney functions, and avoid provoking an immune attack (Artificial kidney breakthrough as bioreactor survives in pig model without immune response, n.d.). A university web page dated 2026 describes human trials as a realistic goal for 2026 or 2027, but I could not verify that claim and I found no evidence that human trials have begun (Source Notes S17).

I mention these not to raise hopes but to calibrate them. Between a promising pig and a person lies a long road, and honest advocacy should say so.

## The house as the hospital

There is a conservative argument, and a human one, for home dialysis, and they meet at the kitchen table. The conservative argument is that the household is the most trustworthy institution human beings have built, and that policy should support its competence and not replace it. The human argument is that dignity is largely the experience of controlling one's own day.

But the argument has a shadow, and I want to name it. Every hour the patient reclaims from the clinic is an hour handed to the person at the counter. The man in the kitchen has a wife who has learned to hear a change in the machine's sound, and she was never asked whether she wished to learn. A home program that does not count its care partners among its patients has simply moved the burden from the building to the family.

The wise reform, then, is not to push everyone home. It is to make the choice real, to provide the training and support that make home therapy safe, and to ask the whole household what it can carry. Frost's line has a double edge. Home is where they have to take you in. The question is whether we have built the kind of home that can do it.

# Chapter 6: The Distance Between Here and Care
EPIGRAPH: "There are only two or three human stories, and they go on repeating themselves as fiercely as if they had never happened before."
BY: Willa Cather, O Pioneers! (1913)

In April 2026, a news story from the Nebraska panhandle described a small hospital's dialysis unit that had closed. Seventeen patients had counted on it, among them a woman named Pieper. Some had moved. One was living in a rental in another city during the week, coming home on weekends. Another was driving more than four hours round trip for each treatment (They counted on a rural dialysis unit to keep them alive. Then it closed., 2026).

I want to stay with that story for a moment, because the distance in it is not a metaphor. A four-hour drive, three times a week, to sit for four hours, and then to drive home feeling washed out, is not a commute. It is a second job on top of the half-time one described in Chapter 1.

## How a town loses its machine

The National Kidney Foundation wrote in 2023 that roughly 170 dialysis facilities had closed within the previous year, pointing to staffing shortages, supply chain disruptions, and rising costs (Source Notes S18). Closure is not evenly distributed. A research brief from the South Carolina Rural Health Research Center found that rural dialysis facilities were more likely to be designated low-volume and to run negative margins, and that those at risk of closing offered fewer stations and fewer late shifts (ESRD facility characteristics by rurality and risk of closure, 2017†).

What happens to patients afterward has been studied in older data. A registry-based analysis of closures in small-town, rural, and micropolitan areas between 2001 and 2015 found that care transitions after closure were associated with more hospitalizations, more missed treatments, and a marginal increase in mortality, with the effects more likely among Black patients and those on Medicaid (Source Notes S19). The National Kidney Foundation cited a 2023 study finding that the median one-way increase in travel distance was 6.6 miles in small-town and rural areas, with a wide range, though it noted that the data are old (Source Notes S18). I did not find a current peer-reviewed measurement of closure-driven travel time and missed treatments, which is itself a finding worth stating. The only national picture of how many such closures there have been recently comes from advocacy letters and local news.

## The thinning of the people who staff the machines

Behind every dialysis unit stands a nephrologist, and that profession is changing. A commentary from the National Rural Health Association, published in September 2026, reports that the fill rate for adult nephrology fellowship positions fell from 94.1 percent in 2010 to 65.8 percent in 2024, while the number of applicants fell by more than 37 percent. It cites a federal projection of a 21 percent shortage of nephrologists by 2037, and notes that rural areas have about 30 physicians and specialists of all kinds per 100,000 residents against 263 in urban areas (The rural nephrology workforce shortage is already here, 2026). A trade-press report indicated that the 2025 match recovered partway, with a fill rate near 73 percent (Source Notes S20). The commentary is advocacy in tone, and the federal projection should be checked against its original, but the direction of the trend agrees across sources.

An analysis of national data found that the roughly 240,000 rural Americans with kidney failure have less access to nephrology care and higher mortality than urban patients (Source Notes S21). One tele-nephrology program at a rural critical-access hospital reported seeing over 100 patients in its first year, which suggests a possible answer but is a single case report.

## A civic institution

Here I want to make an argument that sounds old-fashioned. A town's dialysis unit is not only a business. It is a civic institution, like the school or the volunteer fire department, whose presence tells the people who live there that their lives will be held in place. When it closes, the loss exceeds the lost treatments. It is a message about who the country is willing to keep nearby.

Conservative thought has a name for the principle that serves here: subsidiarity, the idea that matters ought to be handled by the smallest, nearest competent authority, and that larger bodies should support the smaller and not displace them. Applied to kidney care, it would suggest that policy should ask what it takes to keep care close, and treat each closure as a failure to be investigated, not a market outcome to be shrugged at. I do not know the right instrument. A payment adjustment for isolated units, a way of sharing nephrologists across counties, a home-dialysis program designed for places where the nearest clinic is two hours away, each is plausible, and none has been tested at the scale needed. What I know is that the question is not being asked with the urgency of the loss.

Cather wrote of the plains that there are only two or three human stories, and they keep repeating. One of them is the story of people who build a place and then watch the services that made it livable move away. The kidney patient driving four hours is a sentence in that story. We could, if we chose, write a different ending.
