# Chapter 17: A Kidney Is Not Forever
EPIGRAPH: "Hope is itself a species of happiness, and, perhaps, the chief happiness which this world affords."
BY: Samuel Johnson, The Rambler, No. 2 (1750)

There is a sentence people say to one another at transplant celebrations, and it is mostly true and slightly dangerous. "You've got your life back." It is true that a working kidney returns something enormous. It is dangerous because it leaves out the next sentence, which is that the kidney has a lifespan, and so, in a sense, does the return.

Consider a thirty-four-year-old woman, two years past her transplant, who has never felt better. She runs on Saturdays now. She is planning a trip. And on some quiet nights, at the edge of sleep, she does a calculation she has told no one about. Her kidney came from a deceased donor. She knows, in the way you know a fact you have chosen not to look at, that it will not last her whole life.

## The arithmetic of a graft

An analysis of national registry data, published in the American Journal of Transplantation in 2021, estimated that median graft survival for deceased-donor kidneys rose from 8.2 years for transplants performed in 1995 to 1999 to about 11.7 years in the most recent era, and that for living-donor kidneys it rose from 12.1 years to about 19.2 years for transplants in 2014 to 2017 (Source Notes S49). Those are medians, which means half of grafts last less. A conference abstract using the same registry reported projections for living-donor kidneys of well over twenty years, and noted that five-year and ten-year survival had improved too (Source Notes S49). Both sources use projections for the newest cohorts, and they disagree somewhat with each other, so I treat the figures as approximate.

The progress is real, and it is a quiet triumph. But look at the implication for a young recipient. If a kidney lasts a dozen years and a person lives sixty more, she will need more than one. The transplant is not an ending. It is the first chapter of a long relationship with a kind of medicine that she will have to renew. A second transplant means a second wait, and the waiting list, as the registry reminds us, is long.

## What a transplant buys

It helps to see the larger comparison before looking at the limits. Among patients who began treatment in 2007, national registry data showed an adjusted five-year survival of about 40.4 percent for those on dialysis, 73.7 percent for those with a deceased-donor transplant, and 87.0 percent for those with a living-donor transplant (United States Renal Data System, 2014). Those are old cohorts, and they are not a fair experiment, since people who receive transplants are selected partly for being healthier. A landmark study from the late 1990s, which tried to correct for selection by comparing transplant recipients with others who were also on the waiting list, found that the long-term death rate was 3.8 per 100 patient-years among recipients, against 6.3 among those still waiting and 16.1 among all dialysis patients (Wolfe et al., 1999). The direction is not in doubt. A transplant buys years, and the best estimates say it buys a great many.

That is why the length of a graft matters so much. The years a transplant buys are bounded by the years the graft lasts, and a person who outlives her kidney returns to a waiting list that, in 2024, held 143,982 adults (Scientific Registry of Transplant Recipients & Organ Procurement and Transplantation Network, 2026b). It is a fact that sits uneasily beside the celebratory sentence, and I think it should be said at the celebration.
## The drugs that hold it in place

A transplant works because the recipient takes drugs that quiet the immune system's natural wish to destroy a foreign organ. They are lifesaving, and they are not free of cost. They raise the risk of infection and of certain cancers, they must be taken exactly and for life, and they must be paid for. The previous generation of recipients simply accepted that bargain. The next might not need to.

In a clinical trial of an experimental cell therapy called MDR-101, recipients of kidneys from HLA-matched living siblings received an infusion of cells from the donor along with a conditioning regimen, in an attempt to teach the immune system to accept the kidney as its own. In a phase 3 study with 20 treated patients and 10 controls, interim results in 2023 showed that 16 of 19 patients were off immunosuppression at two years. A report in the American Journal of Transplantation in July 2025 described about 95 percent of the treated patients stopping all immunosuppression around a year after transplant, and 75 percent remaining free of it for more than two years. Four patients resumed drugs, for reasons including recurrent kidney disease and rejection, and none developed graft-versus-host disease (Source Notes S50). The sample is small, restricted to matched siblings, and the figures come from different time points, and I found no 2026 news on regulatory approval.

A transplant physician interviewed in April 2026 described the broader field as moving toward cell therapies, with trials combining stem cells and regulatory T cells in living-donor recipients and a randomized study of whether patients can be safely moved to a single immunosuppressive drug (Transplant tolerance: The frontier of kidney care, 2026†). If it succeeds, the result would be something transplant medicine has chased for sixty years: a kidney that the body treats as its own, which is to say tolerance.

## What tolerance means

The word "tolerance" is a clinical term, and I want to take it back for a moment. To tolerate something is to live peacefully alongside it. A person with a tolerated kidney does not carry it as a visitor to be guarded against. She carries it as part of herself. There is a quiet existential difference between taking a drug every day to prevent a rejection and living without that daily reminder that the body is in a negotiation.

Samuel Johnson wrote that hope is itself a species of happiness. He also warned, in the sentence that follows the one most often quoted, that the excesses of hope must be paid for in pain. The woman running on Saturdays knows both halves. A transplant is a gift of years, not a gift of forever. The right response is not to dampen her hope but to meet it with honesty. Tell her the numbers. Tell her about the second chapter. Tell her what is being built for it. And let her go running.
