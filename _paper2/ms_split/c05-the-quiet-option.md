# Chapter 5: The Quiet Option
EPIGRAPH: "That to philosophize is to learn to die."
BY: Michel de Montaigne, essay title, Florio translation (1603)

Picture a woman of eighty-two, whom I will not name, sitting across from a nephrologist whose kindness is obvious and whose clock is ticking. Her kidney function has fallen to the point where dialysis is the next step. The doctor has laid out the plan with a good deal of skill: access surgery, a schedule, a chair. And the woman, who spent her working life as a seamstress and has outlived her husband and one of her children, asks a question that is not on the form. "What would it cost me," she says, "in afternoons?"

This scene is a composite, and I begin with it because the question she asks is one that American kidney care has barely learned to hear.

## An option almost nobody is offered

Conservative kidney management, sometimes called comprehensive conservative care, means choosing to treat kidney failure without dialysis or a transplant, with attention to symptoms, comfort, and the patient's priorities for the time that remains. It is not "doing nothing." It involves medication, diet, support, and often palliative care. It is also, in the United States, strikingly rare.

In a 2023 analysis of a large cohort of people with chronic kidney disease, only 7 percent of 1,018 participants were planning for conservative management. Among 407 who completed a survey, 18 percent reported ever having a conversation with a provider about forgoing dialysis. The providers, for their part, mostly said they felt comfortable raising the subject, and yet none worked in a setting with a dedicated conservative-management clinic or protocol (Source Notes S11). A qualitative study of twenty nephrologists at Veterans Affairs facilities found that national guidelines call for discussing conservative management with patients at advanced stages, that this rarely happens, and that clinicians feel real emotional strain when they care for older patients who are moving toward dialysis (Source Notes S12).

The reasons are not hard to guess. Dialysis is reimbursed, scheduled, and accompanied by an infrastructure. Conversation is none of those. And physicians trained to rescue find it hard to say that rescue may not be what this person wants.

## What the evidence suggests, and what it cannot

Observational studies of adults aged seventy-five and older who have significant illness or functional limitation suggest that survival and quality of life may not differ appreciably between those who start dialysis and those who choose a conservative path. Dialysis is associated with more hospital stays and a smaller chance of dying at home or in hospice. In studies of people approaching kidney failure, a substantial share said they would give up meaningful life expectancy to reduce the burden of treatment (Source Notes S13). A 2026 qualitative study in JAMA Network Open, drawing on two American randomized trials conducted between 2019 and 2024, examined why older adults choose this path. I could read only its abstract, which describes the approach as infrequently used despite the weight dialysis places on patients and caregivers (Older patients and conservative kidney management, 2026†).

Let me be careful here, because this is the most delicate subject in the book. Observational comparisons of this kind are vulnerable to bias, since the people who choose conservative care are, on average, frailer to begin with. I am not arguing that anyone should forgo dialysis, and I would be appalled if this chapter were read as an argument for fewer starts. I am arguing for something simpler and more demanding: that the choice be real. A person who has never been told there is a second path has not chosen the first.

## The quiet endings

The other side of this question is dialysis withdrawal, the decision to stop treatment after it has begun. It is more common than most people suppose. Withdrawal is reported as a cause of death in 20 to 30 percent of people with kidney failure in Western countries, and the authors of the reviews I read note that this is probably an underestimate because of inconsistent coding (Source Notes S14). At one Midwestern center that followed 1,226 patients from 2001 to 2015, 536 died, and 262 of those deaths, 49 percent, followed a decision to withdraw. The death-notification forms captured the withdrawal in only about 73 percent of sampled cases (Source Notes S15).

After withdrawal, the end usually comes quickly. In a three-year Italian program that joined nephrology and palliative care, most patients died within a week, and the program reported fewer hospital admissions and more deaths at home (Source Notes S16). An Australian abstract reported that people who stop dialysis typically survive about a week, and that most still die in acute-care hospitals, often against their wishes (Source Notes S17). A week is short. It is enough time to say what needs saying, if the setting allows, and not enough to repair a setting that does not.

I mention these figures not to be grim but because they describe a moment for which most families are wholly unprepared. They arrive at it in a hospital corridor, without a plan, because no one had the conversation months earlier when there was still time to have it well.

## What a good conversation sounds like

I have been describing a gap in the abstract, and the gap is easier to feel in a sentence. The conversation that rarely happens might begin something like this: "Before we talk about dialysis, I'd like to understand what matters most to you in the time you have. Some people with kidneys like yours choose dialysis, and some choose to focus on comfort and on staying home. Both are real choices, and I'll support whichever is yours. Can you tell me what a good week looks like?"

Nothing in that paragraph requires new technology or a new payment code. It requires about ten minutes, a physician who is willing to say that medicine has limits, and a system that does not punish her for taking the time. It also requires the humility to hear the answer. A patient who says that a good week is one in which she can still garden, and who is then told about a schedule that will take three afternoons, has been given information she can use. A patient who is told only that dialysis is next has not been consulted at all.
## The oldest question

Montaigne titled one of his essays with a sentence that has been quoted for four centuries: to philosophize is to learn to die. He did not mean that we should brood. He meant that a person who has looked squarely at the end is freer to choose what to do with the middle. That is the existential point beneath the clinical one. Dialysis is a method of buying time. The seamstress's question, what will it cost me in afternoons, is the right question to ask of any purchase. Some people, asking it, will buy the time gladly and use it well. Some will decide the price is too high. Both are exercising the same dignity.

A conservative reading adds a note of humility. Medicine's power tempts it toward the view that every decline is a problem to be solved. The older and wiser tradition holds that the person is the authority over her own life, that families are the proper witnesses of such decisions, and that the physician's role is to illuminate the choice and not to make it. A system that cannot say "there is another way, and here is how it goes" has not respected the person in front of it.
