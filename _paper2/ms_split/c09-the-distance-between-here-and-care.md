# Chapter 9: The Distance Between Here and Care
EPIGRAPH: "There are only two or three human stories, and they go on repeating themselves as fiercely as if they had never happened before."
BY: Willa Cather, O Pioneers! (1913)

In April 2026, a news story from the Nebraska panhandle described a small hospital's dialysis unit that had closed. Seventeen patients had counted on it, among them a woman named Pieper. Some had moved. One was living in a rental in another city during the week, coming home on weekends. Another was driving more than four hours round trip for each treatment (They counted on a rural dialysis unit to keep them alive. Then it closed., 2026).

I want to stay with that story for a moment, because the distance in it is not a metaphor. A four-hour drive, three times a week, to sit for four hours, and then to drive home feeling washed out, is not a commute. It is a second job on top of the half-time one described in Chapter 1.

## How a town loses its machine

The National Kidney Foundation wrote in 2023 that roughly 170 dialysis facilities had closed within the previous year, pointing to staffing shortages, supply chain disruptions, and rising costs (Source Notes S27). Closure is not evenly distributed. A research brief from the South Carolina Rural Health Research Center found that rural dialysis facilities were more likely to be designated low-volume and to run negative margins, and that those at risk of closing offered fewer stations and fewer late shifts (ESRD facility characteristics by rurality and risk of closure, 2017†).

What happens to patients afterward has been studied in older data. A registry-based analysis of closures in small-town, rural, and micropolitan areas between 2001 and 2015 found that care transitions after closure were associated with more hospitalizations, more missed treatments, and a marginal increase in mortality, with the effects more likely among Black patients and those on Medicaid (Source Notes S28). The National Kidney Foundation cited a 2023 study finding that the median one-way increase in travel distance was 6.6 miles in small-town and rural areas, with a wide range, though it noted that the data are old (Source Notes S27). I did not find a current peer-reviewed measurement of closure-driven travel time and missed treatments, which is itself a finding worth stating. The only national picture of how many such closures there have been recently comes from advocacy letters and local news.

## The thinning of the people who staff the machines

Behind every dialysis unit stands a nephrologist, and that profession is changing. A commentary from the National Rural Health Association, published in September 2026, reports that the fill rate for adult nephrology fellowship positions fell from 94.1 percent in 2010 to 65.8 percent in 2024, while the number of applicants fell by more than 37 percent. It cites a federal projection of a 21 percent shortage of nephrologists by 2037, and notes that rural areas have about 30 physicians and specialists of all kinds per 100,000 residents against 263 in urban areas (The rural nephrology workforce shortage is already here, 2026). A trade-press report indicated that the 2025 match recovered partway, with a fill rate near 73 percent (Source Notes S29). The commentary is advocacy in tone, and the federal projection should be checked against its original, but the direction of the trend agrees across sources.

An analysis of national data found that the roughly 240,000 rural Americans with kidney failure have less access to nephrology care and higher mortality than urban patients (Source Notes S30). One tele-nephrology program at a rural critical-access hospital reported seeing over 100 patients in its first year, which suggests a possible answer but is a single case report.

## The nurse who stays

A dialysis unit is not a machine room. It is a handful of people, a charge nurse, two technicians, a dietitian, a social worker who comes on Thursdays, and what keeps a rural unit open is often nothing more than the willingness of these few to keep working there. The National Kidney Foundation's letter named staffing shortages first among the causes of closure (Source Notes S27). When one nurse leaves a small unit, the unit does not lose one-fifth of its capacity. It may lose the ability to run its late shift, and the patients who work are the ones who needed the late shift. The South Carolina brief's observation that rural units at risk of closure had fewer stations and fewer late shifts shows how the thinning works: it narrows the hours a patient can choose before it takes the unit away altogether.

I mention it because the usual language of workforce policy speaks of numbers, positions, and pipelines. What a rural patient experiences is a face. She knows the technician who learned how her vein behaves. She knows who will call the clinic in the morning if she does not come in. When that person leaves, the loss is a relationship, and relationships are the part of care that dashboards cannot see.

## A doctor on a screen

One response to the shortage is telemedicine. A report from a rural critical-access hospital described a tele-nephrology program that saw more than 100 patients in its first twelve months, a single case report and not a trial (Source Notes S30). It is easy to be cynical about this, and also easy to be starry-eyed. A video visit cannot place a needle or place a hand on a swollen ankle. But it can reach a county that has no nephrologist at all, and for the patient who has been choosing between a four-hour drive and no visit, a screen is not a lesser option. It is the only door that is open. The honest approach, as with every intervention in this book, is to test it, and to ask patients whether it gives them back an afternoon.

## What I could not find

I looked for a federal count of how many dialysis facilities have closed in recent years, how far patients now travel, and how many missed treatments follow. I did not find one in a form I could cite. The picture comes from advocacy letters, an older registry study, and local news, and I think that gap is itself informative. A country that counts every transplant and every organ cannot say, in a form a reader can find, how many of its dialysis units have shut their doors.
## A civic institution

Here I want to make an argument that sounds old-fashioned. A town's dialysis unit is not only a business. It is a civic institution, like the school or the volunteer fire department, whose presence tells the people who live there that their lives will be held in place. When it closes, the loss exceeds the lost treatments. It is a message about who the country is willing to keep nearby.

Conservative thought has a name for the principle that serves here: subsidiarity, the idea that matters ought to be handled by the smallest, nearest competent authority, and that larger bodies should support the smaller and not displace them. Applied to kidney care, it would suggest that policy should ask what it takes to keep care close, and treat each closure as a failure to be investigated, not a market outcome to be shrugged at. I do not know the right instrument. A payment adjustment for isolated units, a way of sharing nephrologists across counties, a home-dialysis program designed for places where the nearest clinic is two hours away, each is plausible, and none has been tested at the scale needed. What I know is that the question is not being asked with the urgency of the loss.

Cather wrote of the plains that there are only two or three human stories, and they keep repeating. One of them is the story of people who build a place and then watch the services that made it livable move away. The kidney patient driving four hours is a sentence in that story. We could, if we chose, write a different ending.
