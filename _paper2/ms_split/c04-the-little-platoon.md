# Chapter 4: The Little Platoon
EPIGRAPH: "...the growing good of the world is partly dependent on unhistoric acts; and that things are not so ill with you and me as they might have been, is half owing to the number who lived faithfully a hidden life."
BY: George Eliot, Middlemarch (1872)

Somebody drives Ruth. We rarely ask who.

In the vocabulary of the field he is a care partner, which is the sort of phrase that sounds like it was invented by a committee and, for once, earned its place. He might be her husband. He might be a son who moved back into his old bedroom, or a neighbor, or a niece. Whoever he is, he keeps the calendar, remembers the medication names, watches for the swelling in her ankles, sits through the appointments, and does the thing nobody else will do, which is to say "I'm worried about her" to a doctor with eleven minutes.

Edmund Burke wrote, in 1790, that "to be attached to the subdivision, to love the little platoon we belong to in society, is the first principle (the germ as it were) of public affections." (Burke, 1790) He was not writing about dialysis. But I know no better description of the unit in which kidney care actually takes place. It is not a hospital. It is not a payer. It is two or three people at a kitchen table with a pill organizer.

## What the platoon pays

The research on caregivers of dialysis patients is mostly small, from many countries, and cross-sectional, so it shows association and not cause. It is consistent enough to take seriously. Across studies in several settings, the hours a person spends caring are linked to depression, anxiety, and strain, and the burden tends to rise with the length of the caregiving (Source Notes S9). I will not offer a single number for how many hours a week the average care partner gives, because the figures I found were reported in incompatible units and I do not trust them. I would ask instead that you notice the absence of the number. An enterprise that costs the public billions and rests on the unpaid labor of the patient's family has never, so far as I could find, made counting that labor a priority.

## The gift of asking

There is one place where the platoon has been put to deliberate use, and the results are instructive. Many people who need a living-donor kidney simply cannot bring themselves to ask. The request is intimate and awkward, and the person most in need of courage is the person with the least to spare. A program at Johns Hopkins, called the Live Donor Champion program, answered with a change of roles. The patient chooses a friend, relative, or community member to speak on her behalf, and the two attend six monthly sessions of about an hour each, learning how to talk about living donation and how to map the networks they belong to.

In an early pilot, 15 candidates with no prospective donor went through the program and were compared with 15 matched candidates who did not. Twenty-five potential donors contacted the center on behalf of the program group. Four participants went on to receive a living-donor transplant and three more had donors in evaluation. Among the matched controls the number was zero (Source Notes S10). A larger follow-up of 163 candidates reported 81 donor referrals, and participants had about five and a half times the odds of at least one referral compared with matched controls on the waiting list (Source Notes S10). That result was presented as a conference abstract and it is observational, and I found no randomized trial of the program. I treat it as promising, not proven.

What moves me is not the statistic but the design. The program did not hire a bigger institution. It trained a friend. It recognized that the strongest asset a patient has is the web of people who already love her, and that the web needs words, permission, and a little coaching to become useful.

A faith-based organization in Israel has taken the same insight a step further. Matnat Chaim, a nonprofit that arranges mostly altruistic donations between strangers, facilitated 494 living kidney donations from its founding in 2009 through the end of 2017, according to a retrospective review (Emergence of an Israel faith-based community organization facilitating live donor kidney transplantation, 2018†). A retrospective count shows scale and not cause, and nobody should read it as proof that a congregation can replace a hospital. It does suggest that a community with a shared moral language can do something a bureaucracy cannot, which is to make generosity feel ordinary.

## An institution built like a platoon

The most striking large-scale illustration of this principle may be Spain. In 2025 the country recorded 51.9 deceased donors per million people, one of the highest rates in the world, with 2,547 deceased donors and 408 living donors that year, a little below its own record of 52.6 per million set in 2024 (Ministerio de Sanidad, 2026; La Moncloa, 2025). I will not compare that rate with the American one, because definitions of a donor differ and the comparison invites more heat than light. What interests me is the structure. The Spanish system is organized in three tiers, national, regional, and hospital, and at the base of the pyramid sits a hospital coordinator, usually an anesthesiologist or intensive-care physician working part-time in the role, embedded in the hospital where donation happens (The Spanish model, 2025). The coordinator knows the unit, the staff, and often the family. This is an institution designed on the scale of a platoon.

Whether it can be transplanted to another country is unclear, and its admirers disagree about which feature does the work. But the lesson for a conservative reader seems plain. The nearer a decision sits to the people it affects, the better it tends to go. We have built a national apparatus of extraordinary technical power, and I suspect it would work better if more of its human contact were placed in the hands of people who can be recognized in a hallway.

## What we owe the platoon

If the little platoon is where care happens, then policy has a simple obligation, which is to stop treating it as free. That does not necessarily mean wages. It could mean respite, training, a named person to call, a plain-language map of what is coming, or a clinic that asks the care partner how she is. It would be a modest revolution in a field that has built an elaborate machinery of support for the patient and almost none for the person who stands behind her.

Eliot ended her great novel by honoring the hidden lives that keep the world from being worse than it is. That is the care partner, to the letter. The least we owe such people is to count them.
