# Chapter 6: The Disease No One Feels
EPIGRAPH: "For in much wisdom is much grief: and he that increaseth knowledge increaseth sorrow."
BY: Ecclesiastes 1:18 (King James Version, 1611)

Kidney disease has an unusual talent for silence. A failing heart announces itself with breathlessness, a failing lung with every flight of stairs. A failing kidney can go about its decline for years without producing a single symptom a person would think to mention. By the time it begins to hurt, in the sense of fatigue and swelling and a taste like metal in the mouth, a great deal has already been lost. Nothing hurts until nearly everything does.

I think of this as the cruelest feature of the disease, and also the most hopeful, because silence is a problem that information can address.

## How many, and how few know

The Centers for Disease Control and Prevention estimated, in a national page updated in March 2026, that 14 percent of American adults, about 37 million people, have chronic kidney disease (Centers for Disease Control and Prevention, 2026). The numbers that follow are older, and I give them as context only. An earlier CDC analysis, reported at the time in the trade press, found that 96 percent of people with early-stage disease did not know they had it, and that 48 percent of those with severely reduced function were unaware and not yet on dialysis (Kidney disease affects 1 in 7 Americans, says CDC, n.d.). The American Kidney Fund cites 35.5 million Americans and says nine in ten are unaware, a figure that is widely repeated, though I could not find the calculation behind it (Source Notes S18). A study from the 2020s of patients with moderately to severely reduced function found that roughly half were unaware, and the authors observed that the gap has persisted for nearly two decades (Source Notes S19).

These figures disagree at the edges, and that is the honest state of knowledge. What they agree about is the shape: a large population, most of it unaware, discovering the disease late. A test that costs little exists. Measuring albumin in the urine is one of the key ways to detect early damage, and yet a study cited in commentary on one major trial found that among adults with type 2 diabetes receiving primary care at 24 U.S. health organizations, the median rate of such testing was only 52.9 percent (Diabetic kidney disease therapies keep flowing, n.d.). The people most likely to be harmed by diabetes were being checked about half the time.

## The most hopeful paragraph in the book

If the first half of this chapter is about silence, the second half is about something that has begun to change it. The FLOW trial, a large randomized study, was the first dedicated kidney-outcomes trial of a once-weekly injectable drug of the GLP-1 class, semaglutide, in people with type 2 diabetes and chronic kidney disease. A later analysis found that the benefit held whether or not participants were also taking an SGLT2 inhibitor, a different class of drug that professional guidelines now recommend as first-line treatment for most adults with diabetes and kidney disease (Effects of semaglutide with and without concomitant SGLT2 inhibitor use in participants with type 2 diabetes and chronic kidney disease in the FLOW trial, 2024†).

I will resist the temptation to promise what these drugs will do for the population. I found no study that measured how many eligible patients are actually receiving them, and a promise built on prescriptions nobody has counted is a rumor. But I will say what the pattern implies. For most of the history of kidney medicine, the options on offer began at the point of failure. Now, for the first time, there are tools aimed at the long quiet stretch before it. The kidney that never fails is the most patient-centered innovation imaginable, since it spares a person the entire apparatus of what comes after.

## The chair you never sit in

The second way of avoiding the chair is to skip it. A preemptive transplant takes place before dialysis ever begins. It is the goal many clinicians would choose if they could, and in practice it remains uncommon. One analysis of transplants from 2003 to 2012 found that only 17 percent of recipients overall, and 31 percent of recipients of living-donor kidneys, received one before dialysis. Another reported that only 2.5 percent of people whose kidneys failed in 2015 received a preemptive transplant as their first treatment (Source Notes S20). A 2025 study using national transplant data reminded readers that the survival benefit of very early preemptive transplantation remains debated.

One quiet policy story shows how a single number can decide whether a person gets to skip the chair. For years, a formula used to estimate kidney function included an adjustment based on race, which made the kidneys of Black patients look healthier than they were. Because a transplant candidate's waiting time could begin only when the estimate fell below a threshold, the formula had the effect of starting the clock later for Black candidates. In 2023 the national transplant network required centers to correct this by backdating waiting time for affected candidates, those who would have crossed the threshold on a race-neutral calculation (Organ Procurement and Transplantation Network, n.d.). By July of that year, 6,103 modifications had been completed, with a median of 1.7 years of additional time awarded, and the share of Black candidates transplanted before dialysis had risen from 11.7 to 13.9 percent, a preliminary finding the authors cautioned might reflect broader trends (6 month waiting time modification and 1 year race-neutral eGFR monitoring report, n.d.). A later quasi-experimental analysis of more than 181,000 adults, reported in March 2026, found that over 21,000 candidates had received added time between January 2023 and June 2025 and that the policy was associated with 5.3 additional transplants per 1,000 listings among Black candidates, in both the pre-dialysis and the on-dialysis groups (Source Notes S21).

I find this story moving for a reason that has nothing to do with politics. It shows that a measuring stick can quietly steal years, that the theft can be undone once someone looks at the stick, and that undoing it gave real people real kidneys sooner. It also illustrates something conservatives understand well: that an institution's rules deserve respect, and that respect includes the willingness to repair them when they turn out to be wrong.

## What knowing buys

The book of Ecclesiastes warns that increasing knowledge increases sorrow, and a person told at fifty that her kidneys are failing may well agree. But she is also handed a choice. She can act on the diabetes, the blood pressure, the medications, the diet, the family conversation about donation, the question of whether she would ever want the chair. The silence of kidney disease is not a kindness. It is a theft of time during which choices are still wide. The most patient-centered thing a health system can do is give people the news while they can still do something about it, and give it gently.
