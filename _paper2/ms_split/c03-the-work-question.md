# Chapter 3: The Work Question
EPIGRAPH: "There is nothing better, than that a man should rejoice in his own works; for that is his portion."
BY: Ecclesiastes 3:22 (King James Version, 1611)

Picture a man of forty-four who has taught high-school shop for nineteen years. He is good at it. He knows which fifteen-year-old can be trusted with the table saw and which needs another week with the hand plane. Two years ago his kidneys failed, and his treatment, in the plain arithmetic of Chapter 1, now occupies a schedule that does not bend to the bell. He has used up his sick days. His principal is kind and the district is not. At a dinner party, a stranger asks the question Americans ask each other instead of "who are you," which is "what do you do?" He opens his mouth, and for the first time in his life he does not know what to say.

The man is invented. The question he cannot answer is one the kidney literature has measured with some care.

## The numbers on work

A large Danish registry study followed adults between 18 and 65 who began dialysis or received a transplant between 2005 and 2019, linking each person's treatment records to social-benefit records. At the time dialysis began, 11 percent were employed, against 61 percent of matched people from the general population. Among transplant recipients the employment rate was 20 percent, against 67 percent for their matched counterparts. The authors concluded that transplantation leads to only a small increase in employment (Source Notes S6).

Other studies find somewhat higher figures, because they count different things. A systematic review and meta-analysis that pooled studies from 1966 to 2020 reported an employment rate of 26.3 percent among dialysis patients, and rates of 36.9 percent before transplant and 38.2 percent after (Source Notes S7). A United States cohort of people starting dialysis between 1996 and 2013 found 23 to 24 percent working (Source Notes S7). A small 2025 study of 94 transplant recipients in a region of Poland found that 66 percent had returned to work and that 41.9 percent had done so within six months, with return associated with higher education, better finances, and more time since the transplant (Source Notes S8).

The range from 11 percent to 66 percent is wide enough to need explaining, and the explanation is that each study asks a slightly different question. The Danish study measures who has a job at fixed moments, compared with matched people. The Polish study asks whether a recipient ever went back. A meta-analysis blends eras and countries. If I had to carry one number out of this literature, it would be the Danish one, because it has a comparison group, and it says that kidney failure removes most of a person's working life and that a transplant returns only part of it.

## Why work matters beyond money

I will not pretend the economics are small. A person who stops working loses income, employer-sponsored insurance, and the contributions that fund a later life. But the conservative tradition has always held, rightly, that work is also a form of belonging. To have a job is to be needed by someone you are not related to. It is to have a place that would notice your absence. It supplies a structure to the week, and a reason to put on a clean shirt, and a way of being useful that does not depend on anyone's pity.

Chapter 1 described dialysis as a half-time job nobody pays for. This chapter asks what happens when it collides with the job somebody does. An hour of recovery that lasts three, an appointment that cannot move, a body that gives out at the moment the afternoon shift begins: each is a small incompatibility, and together they push people out of work not through any dramatic decision but through the slow arithmetic of a calendar that cannot hold both.

## What the literature does not say

I want to be careful about what I can claim. Nothing in the studies I found proves that a particular kind of dialysis, such as treatment at night or at home, allows more people to keep working. It is natural to suppose so, and a person who dialyzes while asleep has more of the day. But I did not find a study that tested it, and I would be reasoning from the structure of the schedule and not from evidence. What the studies do show is that return to work after transplant is incomplete, and that it varies with education, finances, and time since the operation, which suggests that the barriers are as much social as medical.

## What a good employer would ask

There are small, practical gestures that cost an employer very little. Ask what the treatment schedule is, and move the meeting. Let a person leave at noon on the three days that matter and make up the hours at home. Do not require a letter for every appointment. Treat the occasional bad Thursday as a bad Thursday and not as a character flaw. Several of these gestures are matters of ordinary decency that no law needs to compel, and the small institutions in which most people work, the family business, the school, the congregation's office, are exactly the places where they are most easily offered.

The Polish study I mentioned contains one more observation worth carrying forward: return to work was associated with higher education and good financial standing, which means that the people least likely to go back are those with the fewest resources to begin with. A transplant is a medical event, and the return to ordinary life after it is a social one, shaped by whether a person has savings, a skill that can be done part-time, and an employer who can wait. That suggests a place for a modest form of help that does not resemble medicine at all: a vocational conversation early in the course of kidney failure, before the job has been lost and the skill has gone stale.
## The question beneath the question

The man in the shop classroom is not asking for charity. He is asking, in effect, whether the people who run institutions can imagine a person like him continuing to belong. A school that offered him a lighter schedule, a hospital that offered dialysis at an hour that fit, an insurer that did not punish him for part-time work, a transplant program that asked in its first interview what the patient did for a living and what he feared losing: each would be an expression of the same basic respect.

The question "what do you do?" has an existential edge that I do not think we should smooth over. For many people, what they do is a large part of who they are, and the loss of it is a bereavement that goes unmarked because nobody died. A medical system that treats the kidneys and ignores the identity has treated half of the patient. Ecclesiastes says that there is nothing better than that a man should rejoice in his own works, for that is his portion. A portion is what is allotted. The least a decent system can do is leave a person's portion where he can reach it.
