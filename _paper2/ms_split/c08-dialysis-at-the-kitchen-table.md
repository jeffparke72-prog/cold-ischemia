# Chapter 8: Dialysis at the Kitchen Table
EPIGRAPH: "Home is the place where, when you have to go there, / They have to take you in."
BY: Robert Frost, "The Death of the Hired Man" (1914)

Picture a man in his late fifties, standing in his own kitchen at six in the morning, doing something he would have thought impossible a year earlier. He is washing his hands for the third time. Beside the counter sits a machine about the size of a carry-on bag, and he has just threaded a pair of needles into the vein access in his forearm, a task he was taught over four weeks by a nurse who never once made him feel stupid. His wife is making coffee behind him. She has learned to watch the readout, and to hear a small change in the sound.

No such man exists, and for a growing minority of people with kidney failure the scene is simply true.

## The slow rise of the house

Home dialysis, which includes peritoneal dialysis done with a catheter and a sterile fluid, and home hemodialysis done with a compact machine, gives people something the clinic cannot: control of the schedule. A person can dialyze at night while sleeping, or for shorter sessions more often, or in the afternoon after the grandchildren leave. Recall what patients named as most important in Chapter 1. Ability to travel, time free from dialysis, and not feeling washed out are the things a home schedule can sometimes give back.

The uptake has been slow. A study using national registry data found that the share of newly diagnosed patients starting on home dialysis rose from 6.8 percent in 2010 to 13.3 percent in 2020, a pace the authors judged too slow to reach the federal goal of having 80 percent of new patients on home dialysis or a transplant by 2025 (Source Notes S24). A 2025 market report counted about 433,400 patients treated in centers and about 78,400 treated at home as of March 31, 2025, which by my arithmetic puts the home share near 15 percent of that combined group, though that is not an official national figure (Matthews, 2025). For home hemodialysis specifically, a manufacturer announcement citing 2022 figures put the share at only 2.4 percent of roughly 550,000 people on dialysis (FDA clears Quanta home hemodialysis system, n.d.).

## What the incentives did, and did not do

The federal government tried to nudge the numbers. Under a payment model called ESRD Treatment Choices, dialysis facilities in randomly selected regions, 95 of them, covering 2,591 facilities and 141,890 Medicare fee-for-service beneficiaries, about a third of the Medicare ESRD population, received payment adjustments tied to rates of home dialysis and transplantation (Centers for Medicare & Medicaid Services, 2025). The first-year evaluation reported about 546 additional patient-months of home-dialysis training, a 9 percent increase, and 225 additional transplants, a 10 percent increase, but no increase in the actual use of home dialysis (CMS releases first-year results of ESRD Treatment Choices model, 2023).

The third-year summary is harder to read. It says that through 2023 the model led to an increase in home-dialysis training and in overall transplant rates, driven by deceased-donor transplants. Elsewhere the same summary says the model did not increase home-dialysis rates, transplant waitlisting, or transplant rates, and that it had no effect on Medicare spending or quality (CMS, 2025). Those statements appear to conflict about transplants, and I could not reach the technical report that would resolve the tension. CMS also proposed in 2025 to end the model early, at the close of that year, and I could not confirm the final decision. What I can say is that financial nudges to facilities moved training more than they moved lives. That is a lesson about where the bottleneck lies. Training is a thing a clinic does. Living at home with a machine is a thing a family does.

## The machines that might change the conversation

The dream behind all of this is a device that treats kidney failure without tying a person to a schedule at all. Two efforts show how far the dream is from the kitchen. A wearable artificial kidney, a belt-worn device that filtered blood continuously, was tested in a small human trial. It was well tolerated and cleared waste effectively, but the trial was stopped after the seventh participant because of technical problems with the device, and the authors concluded it would need redesign (Source Notes S25). At the University of California, San Francisco, a team demonstrated in 2023 that kidney cells housed in an implantable bioreactor could survive in a pig, perform several kidney functions, and avoid provoking an immune attack (Artificial kidney breakthrough as bioreactor survives in pig model without immune response, n.d.). A university web page dated 2026 describes human trials as a realistic goal for 2026 or 2027, but I could not verify that claim and I found no evidence that human trials have begun (Source Notes S26).

I mention these not to raise hopes but to calibrate them. Between a promising pig and a person lies a long road, and honest advocacy should say so.

## What a family should be able to ask

If home therapy is to be a real choice, a household needs to be able to put certain questions to the clinic, and to receive answers in plain language. How many weeks of training will there be, and who will be on the phone at three in the morning when an alarm sounds? What happens if the person doing the treatment is sick, or travels, or simply cannot do it one day? Can we try it for a month and go back to the center without penalty? What will change in our house, in water, in storage, in the electric bill, and who covers it? Who checks on the care partner and not only the patient?

None of these is exotic. Each is a question that a thoughtful family would ask before committing to a machine in the kitchen, and a good program would have the answers ready before they are asked. The programs I would trust most are those that treat the household as a partner in a plan and not as a site where treatment is delivered.
## The house as the hospital

There is a conservative argument, and a human one, for home dialysis, and they meet at the kitchen table. The conservative argument is that the household is the most trustworthy institution human beings have built, and that policy should support its competence and not replace it. The human argument is that dignity is largely the experience of controlling one's own day.

But the argument has a shadow, and I want to name it. Every hour the patient reclaims from the clinic is an hour handed to the person at the counter. The man in the kitchen has a wife who has learned to hear a change in the machine's sound, and she was never asked whether she wished to learn. A home program that does not count its care partners among its patients has simply moved the burden from the building to the family.

The wise reform, then, is not to push everyone home. It is to make the choice real, to provide the training and support that make home therapy safe, and to ask the whole household what it can carry. Frost's line has a double edge. Home is where they have to take you in. The question is whether we have built the kind of home that can do it.
