@@PART Four | The Machinery of Hope

# Chapter 15: Three in the Morning
EPIGRAPH: "The art of being wise is the art of knowing what to overlook."
BY: William James, The Principles of Psychology (1890)

Picture a surgeon asleep in her own bed when the telephone lights up the nightstand. It is 3:07 a.m. A coordinator from an organ procurement organization is on the line, speaking fast and clearly in the manner of people who make this call often. A kidney has become available. The donor is sixty-one. There was a biopsy, and the result is not wonderful and not terrible. The kidney is a few hours from her hospital by air. A patient on her list, a man she has known for three years, is sixth in line. Four centers ahead of her have already said no.

She has perhaps a few minutes to decide, and the decision has several layers. Is the kidney good enough for this man? Can it be here in time? Will his recovery be harder if it arrives late? If it works poorly, her program's outcome statistics will carry the mark. If she declines, the offer passes to the next center, and perhaps the kidney is used, or perhaps it is not.

The surgeon is invented. The dilemma is exactly the one the data describe.

## What goes unused

In 2024, 29.3 percent of the kidneys recovered for transplant in the United States were not transplanted, up from 18.2 percent in 2013 (Scientific Registry of Transplant Recipients & Organ Procurement and Transplantation Network, 2026a). The registry's analysts say "nonuse," not "discard," and the choice is telling, because it concedes that the fate of a recovered kidney is often a decision and not a destiny.

Two details in that report say something about how decisions behave. Kidneys from donors who had a procurement biopsy went unused 40.8 percent of the time, against 6.4 percent for kidneys not biopsied. Selection explains much of that, since surgeons biopsy the kidneys they already doubt. But a biopsy samples a small piece of an organ and is read by a pathologist who may have been woken for the purpose, and a rate that high invites the question of how consistently such readings turn into verdicts (Scientific Registry of Transplant Recipients & Organ Procurement and Transplantation Network, 2026a). The other detail concerns hepatitis C. Nonuse of kidneys from donors who had been exposed but had no active virus fell from 49.5 percent in 2016 to 32.2 percent in 2024, which the report attributes to the spread of drugs that cure the infection. A drug invented elsewhere changed what surgeons were willing to do, and about a third fewer of those kidneys were wasted. Much of nonuse, in other words, is not a property of the organ. It is a property of what people believe they can safely do.

## One kidney, many answers

The decision at three in the morning is a human one, and humans vary. A national study of offers made between 2021 and 2023 found that the 193 adult centers accepted a median of 12.5 percent of offers for ordinary kidneys and 7.2 percent for suboptimal ones, with the widest variation among kidneys that had already endured more than 36 hours of cold storage (Quantifying center-level aggressiveness in transplanting suboptimal, deceased donor kidneys in the United States, n.d.†). Earlier studies, which I could read only in summary, suggest that variation within a single center can exceed variation between centers, that kidneys are offered a median of seven times before acceptance, and that agreement among transplant nephrologists on whether to accept a given offer was poor, with a statistic of 0.13 where 1.0 means perfect agreement (Source Notes S45).

I want to be fair to the surgeon in the story. Variation among skilled people deciding under uncertainty is not a sign of incompetence. It is a sign that the problem is hard and that the information is thin. The old teaching, put memorably by William James, is that wisdom is largely the art of knowing what to overlook. But to know what to overlook, one has to see everything first, and at three in the morning, nobody does.

## The hour as a hidden variable

Every hour on ice has a cost, and the cost compounds. A French cohort of 3,839 deceased-donor recipients found that each added hour of cold storage raised the hazard of graft failure by a factor of 1.013 and of death by 1.018 (Debout et al., 2015†). Compounded across the twenty-four hours between six and thirty, my own arithmetic gives a 36 percent higher hazard of graft failure and 53 percent higher hazard of death. The authors, rounding differently, wrote that a thirty-hour kidney carried a risk near forty percent higher than a six-hour kidney. A study reported in June 2026 of 74 kidney pairs from older donors found that cold storage beyond twelve hours raised the odds of delayed graft function more than sixfold, though it was small and from a single center (Source Notes S46).

The point is not that long journeys doom a kidney. The harm is a slope, not a cliff, and the surgeon in the story is weighing a slope. The deeper point is that the hours are a product of logistics as much as medicine. In 2025 a federal working group delivered a final report with 20 recommendations on moving organs, and the Federal Aviation Administration launched a website to help those who carry them by air, with updated guidance on keeping organs in the cabin (Health Resources and Services Administration, n.d.). A 2024 industry briefing noted that the contract then in force could not require organs to be tracked, which made it hard to learn what happened to those that arrived late or damaged, and the organization long contracted to run the network says it built its own tracking tool, now used by 16 procurement organizations (Source Notes S47). When the national kidney-allocation system was changed in 2021 to share organs more broadly, a federal monitoring report found that kidneys traveled farther, mostly within about 250 nautical miles, and that nonuse rose from 21 to 26 percent (Source Notes S47). A proposal for a more flexible system of ranking candidates, called continuous distribution, was listed as paused as of July 2025, and I found no newer status (Source Notes S47).

## What would help her

I do not think the answer is to take the decision away from the surgeon. The answer is to give her a better map than the one she has. A short, standard summary of the donor and the kidney that all centers see in the same format. A projection of what the expected cold time will be, updated as the plane takes off. A way to ask for a second look at a kidney that has been declined repeatedly. A published, plain-language record of how often decisions like hers turn out well, so that she is not carrying the full weight of uncertainty alone.

Above all I would give her a friend, a colleague or a system that tells her at three in the morning what the evidence says about kidneys like this one, as clearly as a pilot is told what the instruments say. Judgment is a human quality, and it improves with good instruments. That is not an argument against the surgeon. It is an argument for her.
