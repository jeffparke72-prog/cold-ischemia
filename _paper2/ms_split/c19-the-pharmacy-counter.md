# Chapter 19: The Pharmacy Counter
EPIGRAPH: "The rich ruleth over the poor, and the borrower is servant to the lender."
BY: Proverbs 22:7 (King James Version, 1611)

Stand behind a man in a pharmacy line on the first of the month, holding a paper bag he has not yet been allowed to take home. The pharmacist is explaining, gently, that the price has changed. His coverage reset. He is not a careless man. He keeps a spreadsheet. He knows exactly how much he has left after rent, and he knows that the pills in the bag are the only thing standing between his transplanted kidney and the machine he left behind.

The man is invented. The gate he faces is one that almost no one thinks of as a gate: the price of the pills that keep a graft alive.

## The cliff that was repaired

For decades, a transplant recipient whose only qualifying condition was kidney failure lost Medicare coverage 36 months after surgery, unless disabled or over sixty-five. The anti-rejection drugs did not become cheaper on the thirty-seventh month. Since December 2022 a benefit called Medicare Part B-ID has covered FDA-approved immunosuppressive drugs indefinitely, for a premium that was $97.10 a month in 2023 (Source Notes S53). A federal analysis in 2019 had observed that, for patients who lost coverage and had no other source, the cost of continuing therapy could be prohibitive. Congressional testimony cited a report that nearly 70 percent of transplant programs had experienced a death or a lost transplant because a patient could not afford the drugs, a figure that reached me only through that testimony (Source Notes S53). A cost-effectiveness analysis found that extending coverage saved Medicare money while improving outcomes. It is one of those rare policies in which compassion and arithmetic agreed, and it took years to enact.

I could find no peer-reviewed study of how the new benefit has changed patients' out-of-pocket burden or their financial distress after the thirty-sixth month. A repair whose effect nobody has measured has not been finished.

## The new uncertainty

The next risk is not a cliff that has been built but one that has been forecast, and I want to describe it with restraint. The 2025 federal budget reconciliation law changed Medicaid in ways that advocacy organizations expect to matter to kidney patients. The National Kidney Foundation, citing the Congressional Budget Office, said that more than 11 million people would lose Medicaid coverage and that more than $700 billion in funding would be removed over the budget period. It reports that nearly half of dialysis patients rely on Medicaid (National Kidney Foundation, 2025). A nephrology society newsletter noted that a transplant can cost more than $400,000 and that immunosuppressive drugs afterward cost $10,000 to $14,000 a year (American Society of Nephrology, 2025). A fact brief in August 2026 said that work requirements of 80 hours a month are scheduled to begin in January 2027, with exceptions for some groups, and that eligibility must be rechecked every six months starting by December 2026 (Gigafact, 2026†).

What I do not know is how many dialysis or transplant patients will lose coverage, whether exceptions will protect them, or how immunosuppressant coverage will fare. These are forecasts from advocacy sources and one fact brief, and the enacted text should be read directly. I say so because it would be easy, and wrong, to write a sentence that sounds like a prophecy.

## A conversation to have at month thirty

Nothing in the literature I read describes it, but the structure of the problem suggests a practical rule. Every recipient, at about thirty months after surgery, should be asked what coverage she will have at thirty-six, and what the drugs will cost her then. Insurance changes are rarely a surprise to the insurer, and they should not be a surprise to the person whose graft depends on them. A transplant program that made that conversation a routine of the second year, with a social worker or a pharmacist, would be doing the unglamorous work that keeps a gift from being wasted.

The same logic applies at every transition: a move, a job loss, a divorce, a change in a state's rules. Each is a moment at which a person might quietly fall between two forms of coverage, and in a system as complicated as ours the people most likely to fall are those without anyone to help them read the forms. We spend extraordinary effort delivering the kidney. We spend almost none on delivering the second year.
## Independence is a medical outcome

There is a conservative principle in this chapter that has nothing to do with the budget. Proverbs observes that the borrower is servant to the lender. A person who must ask for permission, every month, to take the medicine that keeps her kidney alive is not fully free, and she knows it. The dignity of self-reliance, which conservatives rightly honor, is poorly served by a system that sets a person up to depend on institutions she cannot influence. Whatever one's view of the proper size of public programs, an arrangement that makes the continuity of a transplant depend on a bureaucratic reset date is not a conservative arrangement. It is merely fragile.

The remedies are not exotic. Measure the problem, by asking recipients each year whether they have missed doses for cost. Offer a free navigation call before the thirty-sixth month and before any change in coverage. And design coverage rules with an explicit exception for people whose survival depends on a daily drug, because the cost of that exception is trivial beside the cost of a lost graft.
