Professional Assessment · 20 Questions

Is Your Organization a
True Advocacy Organization?

A frank, evidence-based assessment designed for healthcare advocacy organizations, patient groups, and disease foundations. Answer honestly. The results may be uncomfortable. That is the point.

This assessment measures structural independence, not intention. Good intentions are not a substitute for structural integrity.

The advocacy landscape in chronic illness and transplant medicine is dominated by organizations that receive substantial funding from the pharmaceutical companies, dialysis corporations, and insurance carriers whose practices they are supposed to hold accountable. This is not a conspiracy — it is a documented structural reality.

This assessment asks the questions that most patient advocacy organizations never ask themselves. Each question is drawn from peer-reviewed literature, federal oversight reports, and documented conflicts of interest in the advocacy sector. There are no "right answers" if your organization is structurally compromised. There are only honest ones.

Answer all 20 questions to receive your full Advocacy Integrity Score and a personalized analysis. Your responses are confidential. Results are sent directly to you via email. Jeff Parke, Co-Founder of the Cold Ischemia Foundation, reviews all submissions personally.

Section I — Funding Sources & Independence (Q1–5)

Scenario: A Senate Finance Committee report identifies that three of your top sponsors collectively paid $4.1 billion in federal settlements for off-label promotion and data suppression over the past decade — including drugs prescribed to your patient community.
✓ Only True Advocacy Response
C is the only structurally defensible answer. Responses A, B, and D all prioritize donor relationship preservation over patient constituency protection. Response A actively validates a documented wrongdoer. Response B treats the patient community's safety as secondary to organizational revenue. Response D is structural theater — an ethics committee with industry ties cannot produce independent ethical judgments.

The documented pattern in major patient advocacy organizations is overwhelmingly Response A or B. The National Kidney Foundation, American Kidney Fund, and others have issued statements defending pharmaceutical partners facing federal scrutiny rather than protecting their patient constituency.

Cold Ischemia Foundation accepts zero pharmaceutical funding as a founding constitutional constraint — not a policy that can be reversed by a future board. This is the only position that eliminates the conflict entirely. Suspending funding after an investigation has already occurred is not advocacy independence. It is crisis management.

Section II — Governance, Leadership & Constituency Representation (Q6–10)
06

The executive director's compensation is set by this board. This governance structure:
Scenario: The board is voting on whether to publicly oppose a proposed CMS rule that would reduce payments to health systems for outpatient procedures your patient community depends on. Three board members are employed by institutions that would be financially affected by the rule.
✗ B is the only accurate structural assessment
Response B correctly identifies the structural compromise. Conflict-of-interest disclosures (Response C) do not eliminate conflicts — they acknowledge them while allowing compromised votes to proceed. An industry executive who discloses their conflict and then votes in the industry's favor has disclosed but not resolved their conflict. Appointed patient representatives (Response D) serve at the pleasure of the appointing body — they cannot represent constituency interests that conflict with those of their appointing board without risking removal.

A 2022 analysis of 100 major patient advocacy organizations found that fewer than 12% had majority-patient governance structures with independently elected patient representatives.
The CIF Standard

CIF's Care Partner Advisory Council is drawn from the constituency being served — not appointed by an industry-adjacent board. Structural representation is not performative: it means care partners have a vote, not a seat.

Response B is co-advocacy with an industry funder against peer-reviewed science — this is pharmaceutical capture made visible. The CIF Standard

When a clinical researcher asks why these variables are absent, your response is:
Scenario: The excluded variables include immunosuppressant-related metabolic complications, care partner burden scores, and medication non-adherence events — all of which are directly relevant to transplant patient outcomes and all of which appear negatively in the pharmaceutical company's own clinical trial data for their flagship product.

Response C is the only answer that acknowledges the structural problem and commits to remediation. Responses A and D are content-free deflections. Response B uses the medical advisory board as a legitimacy proxy for what is documented funder influence — if the medical advisory board approved a funder-designed instrument without independently evaluating variable inclusion, the advisory board itself is not functioning independently.

Registry instrument design that systematically excludes adverse event variables beneficial to a funder is a form of research infrastructure capture. The data produced by such a registry is not a neutral dataset — it is a curated dataset that serves the funder's interests while wearing the credibility of patient-generated evidence.
The CIF Standard

CIF's care partner registry and data collection instruments are designed to capture what matters to care partners — including burden, financial damage, employment impact, and mental health outcomes. These variables appear unfavorably for every institution in the transplant system. That is precisely why they must be measured by an organization with no financial relationship to those institutions.

14

Scenario: The foundation is considering a $500,000 grant and requires outcome data aligned with their evaluation framework, which specifically requires pre/post clinical measures rather than activity metrics.
△ A is honest; D partially honest but instrumentalized
Response A is structurally honest. Response D uses the grant to justify a capability gap rather than treating the gap as an accountability problem. Responses B and C are impact measurement fraud — substituting activity metrics and satisfaction surveys for outcome data is not measurement, it is reporting theater.

The overwhelming majority of patient advocacy organizations cannot demonstrate outcome impact because they are measuring the wrong things. The CIF Standard

CIF's DMAIC outcome framework explicitly targets clinical metrics: care partner burden scores (ZARIT-22), recipient depression screening (PHQ-9), medication adherence (tacrolimus trough variability index), and 30-day unplanned utilization rates. Activity metrics are internal operational measures. Outcome metrics are the accountability standard.

"Working through established channels" that serve institutional interests is not advocacy. It is institutional participation.

✓ Only Accountable Answer: B

Responses A, C, and D are the language of institutional self-protection in the face of documented advocacy failure. "Stakeholder awareness," "momentum," and "long-term investment" are metrics invented by advocacy organizations to make inaction sound like strategy.
