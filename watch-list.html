About This Ledger

Why it was built

People living with kidney disease depend on a small number of companies for nearly every part of their care: the clinics they visit, the machines and supplies used on them, the drugs they are prescribed, the labs that read their blood, and the funds and groups that speak for them in Washington. Those companies are among the most heavily regulated in American health care, and yet the record of what goes wrong inside them is scattered across dozens of places that almost no patient ever sees.

A federal settlement is announced on a Justice Department press page. A failed state inspection sits in a PDF on a health department server. A recall is posted to an FDA database. A wrongful death suit is filed in a county courthouse. A pattern of complaints lives in patient forums. Each piece is public. Together they are close to invisible, and that is how a labyrinthine system stays comfortable for the people who profit from it.

The Watch List pulls those pieces into one place, scores them the same way every week, and publishes the result where anyone can read it. It is not built to sell anything or to tell anyone where to receive care. It exists so that patients, families, care partners, clinicians, reporters and lawmakers can see the public record for themselves and ask better questions of the companies that shape their lives.

The record was always public. It just was never in one place, and it was never written for the people it affects most.

Each edition is researched from scratch. Every company is searched on its own, across eight kinds of sources, from federal enforcement down to repeated patient reports. Nothing reaches the page without a public link that a reader can open and check. Proven findings carry more weight than open cases, and open cases carry more weight than allegations. Older problems fade over time so a company is judged on its recent record, while the Consistent flag makes sure a long history of trouble is never forgotten.

How to read an edition

  1. Make your callOn release day the front page asks which company you think tops the ledger. Pick one to break the seal, or skip straight to the results. Your record of calls is kept only on your own device.
  2. Scan the ledgerSection A ranks all fifty companies. The change column shows whether a score rose or fell since the last edition. A red Consistent label means problems were documented in at least three of the last five years.
  3. Compare fairlyThe largest chains run thousands of clinics and will naturally collect more records. Switch to Per 100 clinics to compare kidney care providers of every size on equal footing.
  4. Open a company fileSelect any company to see every item behind its score, each with its source, date, type and status, plus a chart of its score across editions.
  5. Read the briefs and patternsSection B lists what is new this week. Section C shows problems shared across companies. Select a pattern to filter the ledger.
  6. Go back, not aheadUse the edition archive under the nameplate to revisit any past week. The next edition stays sealed until Monday at 9:00 AM Eastern.

Section E

Questions and Answers

Is this a list of bad companies?

No. It is a record of documented public complaints and actions. A higher score means more items that are recent, serious and well documented. It is not a verdict on any company, and it does not judge the quality of care at any single location.

Why can't I open next week's edition?

Each edition is researched over the weekend and published Monday at 9:00 AM Eastern, so everyone sees it at the same moment. Until then the data is not on this website at all, so there is nothing to open early. Every past edition stays available in the archive.

Why do the largest companies score higher?

Companies with thousands of locations are inspected and sued more often simply because of their size. That is why kidney care providers can also be compared per 100 clinics, which puts a 50-clinic chain and a 2,500-clinic chain on the same scale.

What is the difference between final, pending and alleged?

Final means a court, regulator or company settlement has resolved the matter, or a recall or citation was issued. Pending means a lawsuit or investigation is open and unresolved. Alleged means a claim has been reported but is not yet backed by a filing or regulator. Pending and alleged items are always labeled as unproven and carry less weight.

Do old problems count forever?

No. Points shrink as an item ages: full weight and a half for the last 90 days, full weight up to a year, half weight from one to five years, and a small amount after that. The Consistent flag separately marks companies with documented problems in at least three of the last five years.

Can a company respond or correct the record?

Yes. Companies and readers can send corrections through the contact page with a link to supporting records. Verified corrections are made in the next edition, and past editions are only changed to fix a verified error.

Is my guess stored anywhere?

Your predictions and your record of calls are saved only in your own browser on your own device. Nothing about your visit is sent to the Cold Ischemia Foundation.

Who decides what is included?

The same written method is applied to every company every week. The weights, rules and sources are published on this page so anyone can check the work. Companies cannot pay, sponsor or ask their way off the list.

The Watch List is published by the Cold Ischemia Foundation, Ellenton, Florida. Every item links to its public source. Corrections: contact page.
