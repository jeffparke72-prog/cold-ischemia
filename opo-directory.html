Each part of the country is served by one OPO, the nonprofit that coordinates deceased organ donation in its area. Here is every one: who they are, where they are, how to reach them, and which transplant programs they work with, from the federal government’s own records.

Thinking about living donation?Living donation is arranged by transplant centers with a living donor program. Use the transplant programs listed under each OPO, and the national resources below, to find one. Our Living Donor Journey walks you through the process.
Want to be a donor after death?Your local OPO coordinates deceased donation and can answer questions about registering. You can also register nationally at registerme.org or through your state’s registry.

About This Data

Addresses, phone numbers, and affiliated transplant programs come from the Health Resources and Services Administration’s data downloads (OPO and transplant center facts file), retrieved September 28, 2026. Current public names, states served, and CMS performance tiers (2023 data cycle) were compiled from opodata.org and CMS’s QCOR system by the open-source opo-scraper project. Where an OPO now uses a different public name than its registered name, both are shown.

CMS tiers come from the 2020 OPO Conditions for Coverage rule, which ranks OPOs on donation and transplantation rates: Tier 1 meets the top standard, and Tier 3 OPOs are at risk of decertification. Tiers are recalculated as new data is released. OPOs may serve only part of a listed state.
