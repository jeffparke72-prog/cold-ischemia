Our Story

Before there was a foundation, there was a kitchen table.

The Cold Ischemia Foundation did not begin with a strategic plan, a seed round, or a ribbon cutting. It began the way most important things begin — with exhaustion, with anger, and with the quiet refusal to accept that this was simply how things were.

Jeff Parke spent decades inside the American healthcare machine — first as a clinical research professional navigating how medicine gets tested and deployed, then as something far more personal: a care partner. Marie Parke understood it from the other side of the same table, living with kidney disease inside a system extraordinarily good at treating the organ and extraordinarily indifferent to the human being attached to it. Together, they saw the same failure from two angles. Neither angle was comfortable. Both were clarifying.

"We did not choose this fight because it was convenient. We chose it because we could not find anyone else who would."

Jeff & Marie Parke · Founders, Cold Ischemia Foundation · Ellenton, Florida

"Cold ischemia" is the medical term for the window of time between when a donor organ is removed from its source and restored to circulation in a recipient's body — the period of maximum vulnerability, where everything can be lost. That's the name they chose, deliberately, because care partners live inside that window every single day: doing critical work the system provides no training for, no compensation for, no recognition for, and no path back from.

Care Partner Advocacy

The person keeping a patient alive at home deserves more than a signature line.

Transplant programs will not list a candidate without a designated care partner, then offer that same care partner no training, no legal standing, and no pay for years of medically essential labor. Roughly 500,000 people are performing mandatory, clinical-grade care labor as a condition of transplant listing right now — labor that's invisible in cost accounting and absent from every outcome measurement system.

Compensation for care partner labor

Recovery-phase caregiving is skilled, sustained, and unpaid. Marie's Lifeline Compensation Act would create a real path to pay — not just a tax credit nobody can access in time.

Job & income protection

Care partners are routinely forced to choose between the patient's recovery and their own paycheck. 61% work while providing care; 69% of those make significant workplace sacrifices as a result.

A formal voice in the medical decision

Zero care partners sit on the UNOS Board of Directors. Every allocation policy affecting 500,000+ care partners is designed without a care partner voice at the table.

Mental health as clinical infrastructure

53% of transplant care partners meet clinical criteria for depression or anxiety — and the overwhelming majority are never referred for treatment. Untreated depression in recipients raises mortality risk by 65%. This isn't a support issue. It's a clinical one.

Emerging Technology

BHOC: the organ is only as good as the clock it beat.

Biologic Hemoglobin Oxygen Carrier (BHOC) technology is one of the more promising attempts to slow the cold ischemia clock — and almost no patient or care partner has ever heard of it.

Not a Transfusion. Not a Drug.
A way to keep tissue oxygenated when there's no circulation to do it.

Cold storage slows the damage an organ accumulates in the window without blood flow. BHOC technology is one of the few approaches trying to interrupt that damage entirely, by delivering oxygen to tissue independent of a beating heart. This lives almost exclusively in surgical journals and industry panels right now — never in the room with the patient who's waiting for an organ. CIF is working to change that: plain-language explanation instead of a surgical abstract, and outreach to the researchers and regulators deciding whether this technology reaches the people who need it.

Patient Education — Not in the Welcome Packet

What nobody hands you at intake.

Transplant conferences talk about access and allocation. They don't spend much time on what living with a transplant actually feels like week to week.

BK Virus, CMV & the Biopsy
It's already inside you — that's the part nobody explains.

Most adults carry BK virus and cytomegalovirus (CMV) quietly, kept in check by a normal immune system. Immunosuppression — the entire point of a transplant working — is also what lets them reactivate. Nobody hands you a pamphlet on this at your pre-transplant workup; you find out the way most transplant patients find out about most things, after it's already happening. And the biopsy that monitors for it is rarely the hard part physically — it's the waiting on results that nobody prepares you for emotionally, especially the second, third, and tenth time around.

This is manageable, monitored, and anticipated by good transplant centers. What's missing isn't the medicine — it's someone saying this out loud before you're staring at a lab result you don't understand.
