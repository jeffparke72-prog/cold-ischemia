# Chapter 5: The Body Nobody Mentions
EPIGRAPH: "After great pain, a formal feeling comes."
BY: Emily Dickinson, poem 341 (c. 1862; first published 1929)

There are subjects that a clinic's waiting room will discuss and subjects it will not. Everyone will talk about the fluid limit, the potassium, the long drive. Almost no one will talk about what happens in the bedroom, in the mind, or in the space between two people at a kitchen table who have gone quiet.

This chapter is about those subjects. I have written it carefully, because the people it concerns have been silent for good reasons, and because I do not want to treat their silence as a gap in the data and nothing more. It is also the chapter in which I most want you to feel something, because the facts here are not in dispute and have changed very little in the way care is delivered.

## A marriage, in outline

Consider a composite. I will call him Daniel. He is forty-four and has been on dialysis for three years. He and his wife have been married for eighteen. They have two children and a mortgage and a dog. Before his kidneys failed they were, like most couples, an ordinary mixture of affection and friction. Now there is a third presence in the marriage, which sits in a chair at the clinic three days a week and comes home in the evening, exhausted, with a bandage on its arm.

Daniel does not talk to his doctor about the fact that he and his wife have not been intimate in more than a year. He does not know that the problem has a name, or that the other men in the unit are living with it, or that it is among the best-documented consequences of his illness. His wife has not mentioned it either. She has decided that it is her job to be kind. He has decided that it is his job to be grateful. The two decisions, each made in love, have built a wall.

The research on this is not subtle. A recent meta-analysis, reported in a trade summary, found that sexual dysfunction affected about 71 percent of people with chronic kidney disease across studies, with higher rates among those on hemodialysis, at 77.6 percent, and those before dialysis, at 76.7 percent, and a lower rate among transplant recipients, at 56.9 percent. The condition was linked to poorer physical and mental quality of life and to more severe depression (Source Notes S68). An earlier meta-analysis in 2010 reached the same conclusion. In other words, in the most common forms of treatment, most people are affected.

Even so, the evidence is thinner than the problem. One review noted that there were twenty-one meta-analyses of sexual dysfunction in men with kidney disease and only two in women, and the figures that exist for women on hemodialysis range widely, from about a quarter to more than three quarters across small studies using different questionnaires (Source Notes S68). A woman on dialysis is, on this subject, less studied than a man. That alone should make us uneasy.

I want to be clear about what I am not saying. I am not saying that the research has found a cure that clinicians are withholding. I am saying that a condition affecting most patients is, in practice, hardly ever raised, and that the first thing a clinic can do is the cheapest: to ask. A nurse who says, "Many people on dialysis notice changes in intimacy, and there are things we can try. Would you like to talk about it?" has given a patient permission to be a whole person.

## The question of children

Now picture a different room. A woman of twenty-nine, whom I will call Aisha, learns she is pregnant, three years into dialysis, and a week later is told by a clinician, with real kindness, that she should consider ending the pregnancy.

For decades that was the standard advice, because the odds were bad. A pregnancy on conventional dialysis was dangerous for the mother and rarely ended in a living child. What changed that picture, at least in part, was a finding from Toronto. A research group led by Michelle Hladunewich compared pregnancies in women receiving intensive hemodialysis, long and frequent sessions, with pregnancies in a large American registry of women on conventional dialysis. In the Toronto group, 86.4 percent of pregnancies ended with a live birth, against 61.4 percent in the American group, and the median pregnancy lasted 36 weeks against 27. The relationship appeared graded: live birth occurred in 48 percent of women dialyzing 20 hours or fewer a week and in 85 percent of those dialyzing more than 36 hours (Hladunewich et al., 2014).

The caution here is large. The Toronto cohort numbered only 22 pregnancies and the American 70. The comparison was retrospective, the women were not randomly assigned, and the places and eras differed. The authors themselves concluded only that pregnancy on intensive dialysis appeared feasible and safe. I would not tell any woman that the question is closed.

But think about what the study says, even with all its limits. In the right hands, with enough hours, a woman who was once told she should not have a child could be told that she might. That is not a statistic. That is a person on the other side of a door. The question for the field is why this finding, published in 2014, is not better known among the young women who most need to know it, and why intensive treatment for pregnancy is still treated as a boutique option and not a standard of care to be built into the health system.

## The weight of the mind

Neither Daniel nor Aisha is likely to be asked, at their next visit, how they are feeling in the way a friend would ask. And the feelings are not minor.

A 2025 meta-analysis in *Seminars in Dialysis*, which pooled 137 cross-sectional studies covering 21,608 people on hemodialysis, estimated that 45.8 percent had depression (95 percent confidence interval 42.0 to 49.6), with a lower, but still striking, 38.9 percent in high-income countries (Source Notes S61). Other reviews report ranges from about 5 percent to more than 75 percent, depending on the instrument and the population. The spread reflects the difficulty of measuring a mood. The center of the evidence says that something like four in ten, give or take, are struggling.

Worse, they are struggling in the same room, day after day, with a group of people who might help and who have, in most clinics, no structure to do so. The social isolation that often accompanies chronic illness has its own cost. A 2026 population-based cohort study of people with chronic kidney disease found that those who were the most socially isolated had a mortality risk about 33 percent higher than others, and those who were lonely had a risk about 10 percent higher. The authors estimated that the most isolated lost around two to three years of life expectancy (Source Notes S62).

I would not call loneliness a disease. The authors of such studies are careful to say that association is not causation, and I share the caution. A person may be isolated because she is sicker, and not the reverse. But the finding fits what every dialysis nurse already knows. The patients who have someone, a husband, a sister, a friend from church who drives them on Friday, do better in ways that no lab test captures. And the ones who do not have someone are the ones I worry about at night.

## The remedy that is not a pill

What would help? The honest answer is that no one knows with certainty, and the best trials have been modest. Peer mentoring, in which a person who has lived through kidney failure supports a newly diagnosed one, has been tested in a 200-patient hemodialysis trial reported in the *Journal of the American Society of Nephrology* in April 2025. A clinical event rate was lower in the mentored group, with an incidence rate ratio of 0.60 against 0.85, but the overall result did not reach statistical significance (Source Notes S48). A mentoring program for transplant candidates found no difference in time to listing or in quality of life (Source Notes S48). Not a triumph.

Yet I would resist the conclusion that mentoring does not work. A trial that fails to show a benefit has told us that this version, delivered this way, to these people, did not. It has not told us that a bad afternoon is no better with a veteran in the next chair. The lesson I draw is that we should invest in better-designed tests and keep the programs that people say they value. A person who has survived the first year can tell a newcomer in five minutes what the pamphlet takes forty pages to leave out.

## The grief that has no funeral

There is a name for what many of these people are carrying, and it is not depression, though it keeps company with it. It is grief. They have lost something, a good many things, and nobody died.

They have lost the body they used to trust. A man learns that he can no longer drink what he likes, eat what he likes, or travel as far as he likes, and that a scar now runs along the inside of his arm where a vein was made to do a job it was not born for. A woman learns that her skin has changed color and her energy has changed hours. They have lost the long, careless sense of the future that healthy people carry and do not notice. And they have lost the right to be simply tired, because tired has become a symptom, and symptoms have to be reported.

Our culture has rituals for the death of a person and almost none for the death of a self. A widow gets casseroles. A man who loses his old life gets a pamphlet. The existential thinkers I have read tell us that the confrontation with finitude is the beginning of a more honest way of living, and I believe them. But it is a beginning that needs company. I have watched people go through it alone, in the vinyl chairs, with the television on, and I have watched people go through it beside someone who simply said, *Yes. This is hard. I am here.* The difference was not a medication.

A clinic cannot supply a funeral for a lost life, but it can supply a witness. A social worker who has ten minutes to sit with a patient, rather than two, is not a luxury. She is the nearest thing the system has to a chaplain, and in most units she is stretched across far more patients than she can ever sit with. When we talk about the cost of the promise, we should remember that the expensive part is the machine, and the part that matters most is a person with time.

## What we call people

There is one more thing that belongs in a chapter about the body, and it is the smallest and most pervasive. It is the words.

In a clinic, a patient who misses treatments is often described in the chart as "noncompliant." A person who is "a dialysis patient" is defined by the machine. A condition is called "end-stage," as if the story had ended. These are not trivial labels. Guidelines from health systems and advocacy groups have long urged person-first language and the avoidance of terms like "noncompliant," which attribute a failure to the patient's character and not to the circumstances that made adherence difficult (Source Notes S70). One experiment, though not in nephrology, found that clinicians who read stigmatizing wording in a chart held more negative attitudes toward the patient and were more likely to blame the patient for failing to follow treatment (Source Notes S70).

I am not naive about this. Changing a word will not mend a marriage. A commentary in the nephrology literature warned that swapping one label for another would not fix the underlying inequity, and I accept the warning (Source Notes S70). But words are the first thing a patient meets. They tell her whether the person across the desk sees a human being or a problem. A clinic that stops writing "noncompliant" and starts writing, "had difficulty getting to treatment because the bus was late," has given itself something harder to dismiss: a reason.

## What you can do

If you are a patient, you have permission to ask the questions nobody asks. At your next visit, say one of these sentences aloud: *I'd like to talk about intimacy.* Or *I think I may be depressed.* Or *I'm thinking about having a child.* If the first clinician cannot help, ask for the social worker or for a referral. You will not be the first.

If you are a partner, say the thing. "I miss you" is a complete sentence. It will not solve the problem. It will end the silence, and silence is where marriages and spirits break down.

If you work in a clinic, add three questions to your standard assessment this month: one about intimacy, one about mood, one about whom the patient could call at two in the morning. Write down the answers. If you run a unit, look at your charts for the word "noncompliant" and ask what happens if you change it.

If you are a researcher, study women on dialysis. The literature owes them more than two meta-analyses.

And if you are none of these, remember that Dickinson's formal feeling, the stiff politeness that settles over a house after great pain, is not peace. It is the sound of people trying not to cry. A kinder world would know how to say so.
