# Chapter 4: The Afternoons
EPIGRAPH: "So teach us to number our days, that we may apply our hearts unto wisdom."
BY: Psalm 90:12 (King James Version, 1611)

Meet a woman I will call Marisol. She is not a real person, and I say so plainly, because what follows is a composite assembled from what patients, nurses and researchers have described, and I would not want anyone to mistake an illustration for a case. But she is built from true parts, and if you have spent time in a dialysis unit you will have met her.

Marisol is thirty-eight. She rents a chair in a salon, which means she is, in effect, a small business: she sets her own hours, and no one pays her when she is not there. Her clients are people she has known for years. They tell her about their divorces and their mothers. Two years ago her kidneys began to fail, quickly and then all at once, and she learned a new vocabulary and a new calendar. Monday, Wednesday, Friday, one o'clock, four hours in the chair.

She has done the sum. Everybody does, eventually, in the car in the parking lot with the engine off. But she did not do the sum we are about to do. She did the one that counts only the hours on the machine.

## The sum nobody does

Let us do it properly, because slowness is the point.

A common schedule for treatment in a dialysis center is three sessions a week, each about four hours. Over a year that is 156 sessions, and 624 hours in the chair. Now add the part nobody schedules. In an online survey of 359 people who dialyze in centers, the median time to recover from a session was three hours, and four in ten said it took four hours or longer. The most common complaint was feeling washed out, reported by 62 percent, followed by cramps at 44 percent and symptoms of low blood pressure at 42 percent (Source Notes S1). A separate review put the typical recovery at two to four hours, with about a quarter of patients reporting more than six, and a French cohort of 536 patients found that 15 percent needed more than twelve hours (Source Notes S40).

Take the survey's median. Three hours of recovery after each of 156 sessions is another 468 hours.

[[FIG:time|Figure 4.1. A year of in-center hemodialysis in hours, compared with a full-time work year. Illustrative arithmetic from a common three-session schedule and a published median recovery time; travel excluded.]]

Together, 1,092 hours. A full-time job occupies about 2,080 hours in a year. Kidney failure treated in a center takes something like half of a full-time job, and the person who holds it is not paid, cannot quit, and is often too tired to do any other job at all. I must be honest about the limits of that figure. The schedule is common but not universal, the recovery number comes from one modest survey, and recovery overlaps with sleep and rest in ways no questionnaire can untangle. Travel to and from the clinic is not counted. The point is not that the sum is exact. The point is that it is the right kind of sum, and that nearly no one does it.

Here is what the sum looks like from inside. It is Wednesday. Marisol has a cut and color booked for eleven, which will run to twelve-thirty. She has to leave by twelve-fifteen. She will sit in the chair until five, and she will walk out at ten past, and by the time she reaches her car the bottom of the day has dropped out of her. She will drive home slowly. She will lie on the couch with the television on, not watching it. At seven her daughter will ask whether there is anything for dinner. At eight she will wake up wondering where the evening went.

You will notice that I have not said she is in pain. She is not, particularly. What she is, is *absent*. For a stretch of each day she is physically somewhere, but the person who lives in her body has stepped out. Multiply that by three days a week, and by the years, and you begin to see the shape of what this treatment takes. It does not take her life. It takes her afternoons.

## What the doctors count, and what Marisol counts

Here is a fact that has haunted me since I first came across it. In an international exercise to decide which outcomes should matter most in dialysis research, hundreds of patients, caregivers and clinicians from dozens of countries were asked to rate a list of possible outcomes. The patients and caregivers placed far greater weight than the professionals on the ability to travel, on time free from dialysis, and, to a lesser degree, on feeling washed out afterward (Source Notes S60).

[[FIG:song|Figure 4.2. Outcomes that patients and caregivers rated higher than clinicians did in an international consensus exercise, in points on the rating scale.]]

Look at that again. The largest gaps were not about chemistry. They were about the ability to leave town for a wedding, and about hours that belong to the patient. I suspect the explanation is simple, and cruel in its simplicity. A physician sees a patient for a few minutes at the machine, and the training and the incentives point toward whether the blood is clean. A patient lives in the other twenty hours, and is judged by nothing, and so must make the judgments herself.

I do not blame the clinicians. I have known many nephrologists, nurses and technicians who give more of themselves than any employer could ask. But the system they work in measures what it can measure. It measures the adequacy of the treatment. It does not measure how long you were gone afterward, whether you made the recital, whether you had to turn down the job. And what a system does not measure it cannot manage.

## Small changes with big afternoons in them

This is where the story should turn hopeful, and I think it can, because the evidence on recovery is more interesting than most people in the field have been told.

Consider something as plain as the temperature of the fluid. Standard dialysis fluid is warmed to about body temperature. Several small studies have tested cooling it a degree or two. In a 2024 quasi-experimental study in Pakistan involving 92 patients, researchers compared fluid at 36 degrees Celsius with fluid at 37. In an older crossover trial of 50 patients, comparing 35 degrees to 37, 76 percent of patients reported feeling more energetic after cooler treatments (Source Notes S41). These are small studies from varied settings, and none is the definitive trial. Larger registered trials in Iran and Turkey had not, in the sources I could reach, published results. So I will not tell you that cooler dialysis cures the washed-out feeling. I will tell you that a fix this cheap, one that requires no new machine and no new drug, deserves a proper test, and that the patients in those studies were telling us something.

Or consider a report on a change in prescription. In one study described in the trade press, the median recovery time fell from 210 minutes to 60 minutes at six months, and was 105 at twelve (Source Notes S42). Again I hold this loosely. It is a report, not a randomized trial, and the changes involved were of the kind a unit makes patient by patient. But think about what it means: three and a half hours reduced to an hour. If that held for even a fraction of patients, the arithmetic of my sum above would change by hundreds of hours a year.

And consider the large trials that tested giving patients more dialysis, more often. The Frequent Hemodialysis Network trials randomly assigned 245 patients to six sessions a week or to the usual three. The daily arm reduced the mass of the heart's main pumping chamber by 13.1 grams, a meaningful improvement in a part of the body that kidney failure damages. The nocturnal trial, which tested long sessions overnight at home, did not reach statistical significance on its main outcome, though it showed better control of blood phosphate and blood pressure (Source Notes S43). The trials took time away from the day and gave some of it back at night, and in doing so they pointed to a possibility: that the shape of the treatment is not fixed.

What strikes me is that all of these ideas begin with the patient's complaint and not the lab value. Nobody had to invent a new molecule. They only had to take seriously that three hours of washed-out is a harm.

## The question of work

The deeper cost of the missing afternoons is not leisure. It is livelihood.

A large Danish registry study followed adults between 18 and 65 who began dialysis or received a transplant between 2005 and 2019. At the time dialysis started, 11 percent were employed, against 61 percent of matched people from the general population. Among transplant recipients the employment rate was 20 percent, against 67 percent of their matched counterparts. The authors concluded that transplantation produced only a small increase in employment (Source Notes S44).

Other studies give different numbers, because they ask different questions. A meta-analysis that pooled studies from 1966 to 2020 put the employment rate among dialysis patients at 26.3 percent, and at 36.9 percent before and 38.2 percent after transplant (Source Notes S45). A small 2025 study of 94 transplant recipients in one region of Poland found that 66 percent had gone back to work, 41.9 percent within six months, and that return was associated with higher education, better finances and more time since the surgery (Source Notes S46). If I had to carry one figure out of this literature, it would be the Danish one, because it compares like with like. It says that kidney failure removes most of a person's working life, and that a transplant returns only part of it.

Marisol knows this without a registry. She has watched clients drift to other stylists on Mondays, Wednesdays and Fridays. She has watched her savings drop. She now takes new clients only on Tuesdays and Thursdays, and has begun to suspect the salon will not need her for long. The work she did, in the way she did it, was part of who she was. The people in the Danish study did not leave a job. They left a self.

I want to avoid being sentimental about this. A job is also rent, and insurance, and a pension, and the end of a particular kind of worry. But the conservative tradition I admire has always insisted that work is more than a wage. It is a form of belonging, a way of being useful that does not depend on anyone's pity. A policy that rescues a person's body and strands her identity has done half the job.

## The people at the edge of the room

There is another person in this story, who rarely gets a chair of her own. Marisol's mother, who lives forty minutes away, comes on Fridays to take the girl to school and to sit on the couch. Small studies of caregivers of hemodialysis patients, from places as far apart as Indonesia, Greece and India, have linked the hours spent in care with depression and strain (Source Notes S2). The samples are small and the instruments vary. But the direction is clear, and the people in them would not be surprised.

We have a habit, in this field, of counting the patient's hours and ignoring the family's. There are no "washed out" questionnaires for mothers.

## What a better arrangement would look like

The sum I opened with is not a law of nature. Some of the 1,092 hours could be given back by tuning the treatment so that recovery is shorter. Some could be given back by moving treatment to evenings, or to nights, or home, where the patient is in control of the clock. Some could be preserved by employers who are willing to move a Wednesday meeting. And some could be given back only by a transplant, which is why the questions in the previous chapter matter so much.

What none of it requires is the invention of a miracle. It requires a change in what we measure. If the next national quality program counted, alongside the adequacy of the treatment, a single question asked monthly of every patient, *how long did it take you to feel like yourself after your last treatment?*, the field would begin to improve on this front within a year. People manage what they measure. We have not been measuring the right thing.

## What you can do

If you are on dialysis, start a log. For one month, after each session, write down the time you left the unit and the time you felt like yourself again. Bring the log to your next care-plan meeting and ask your nephrologist what could be changed, including the temperature of the dialysate, the length or timing of the sessions, and whether a home option might fit your life. Ask to be taken seriously.

If you are a unit manager, add the recovery question to your monthly patient survey, and read the results aloud at your staff meeting. If you run a quality committee, bring one patient to every meeting and let her speak first.

If you employ someone on dialysis, ask them what their schedule is and then fit your schedule to theirs, rather than the other way around. It costs less than you fear.

And if you are one of the many who has never had to count your afternoons, I ask only that you take the psalmist's verse seriously. We are all asked to number our days. A few of us have been handed a calculator.
