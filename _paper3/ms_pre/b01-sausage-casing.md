@@PART One | The Promise
# Chapter 1: Sausage Casing and Orange Juice Cans
EPIGRAPH: "Is there no balm in Gilead; is there no physician there? why then is not the health of the daughter of my people recovered?"
BY: Jeremiah 8:22 (King James Version, 1611)

Imagine the Netherlands in the worst years of the occupation. It is 1943. A young physician named Willem Kolff has a ward in a small hospital in the town of Kampen, and on the ward there are people dying of a thing that, in those days, no one in the world could stop. Their kidneys have failed. Poisons that a healthy body quietly sweeps out each hour are building in their blood, and the doctors can do nothing but make them comfortable and watch the confusion come, and then the stupor, and then the end.

Kolff was not content to watch. He had read about earlier attempts to clean blood through a membrane, attempts that had worked in animals and failed in people, and he had a theory about why: the membranes were wrong, the anticoagulant was wrong, the machines were clumsy. So in a country where nearly everything was being taken by someone else, he began to build with what could be found. The account that has come down to us, repeated in nearly every history of the field, is of a rotating wooden drum wrapped in cellophane tubing, the sort used for sausage casings, turning through a bath of fluid, with parts borrowed from wherever parts could be borrowed, including the water pump of an automobile (Source Notes S63). The details have taken on a folklore sheen, and I would not stake much on every can and bolt. But the essential picture is secure. A doctor in an occupied country made a kidney out of scraps.

The first patients he treated died. This is the part of the story that gets trimmed in the retelling. He treated, by the standard account, more than a dozen people over those first years, and none of them survived. They died, one after another, of the very disease that the machine was meant to answer (Source Notes S63). Think of what it is to continue under those conditions. To tell the next family that the machine might help, having seen the last family bury someone. To keep building anyway.

In September 1945 a sixty-seven-year-old woman, her kidneys shut down by an acute illness, was connected to the machine for about eleven hours. She woke, as the story goes, and she lived for several more years (Source Notes S63). She was the first patient to be rescued by an artificial kidney, and with her the idea moved from a hope to a fact.

I begin the story of the American promise with this scene on purpose. The promise did not begin in Washington. It began with a man who was willing to be wrong in public, again and again, among the dying, until he was not.

## The machine that could be used only once

There was a catch, and it is the one that shaped the next fifteen years of kidney medicine and the lives of everyone who came after.

Kolff's machine could rescue a person whose kidneys had stopped suddenly and might recover, someone crushed in an accident, or poisoned, or run down by a severe infection. For such a patient, a few treatments could carry them across a gap until their own kidneys woke up. What the machine could not do was keep alive a person whose kidneys had failed for good. Dialysis required a way into the bloodstream. In those years that meant a surgeon cutting down to a vein and an artery, placing needles or tubes, and, when the treatment ended, tying them off. Each time, a piece of blood vessel was used up. After a handful of treatments there was nowhere left to cut.

So the people with permanent kidney failure, which is to say the people in whom the failure would come every week of the rest of their lives, were outside the promise of the machine. The device could clean their blood. It could not be plugged in more than a few times. A treatment that works twelve times is a treatment for a bad month. It is not a treatment for a life.

Many of us who live with chronic illnesses of our own have met a version of this problem. The technology is dazzling, and then you notice that it fails at the doorway, at the thing so ordinary that no one wrote a grant to fix it. The cure is there. The way in is missing.

## The tube that made a life

In Seattle, in the winter and early spring of 1960, a physician named Belding Scribner and a young engineer named Wayne Quinton solved the doorway problem with a piece of plumbing. They took a tube of the new slippery plastic Teflon, shaped it into a small U, and placed one arm into an artery and the other into a vein in the patient's forearm, leaving the loop outside the skin, where it could be opened and attached to the machine and then closed again so that blood simply flowed through the loop between treatments (Source Notes S63). A shunt, it was called. It was not elegant. It was a permanent opening in the body that had to be kept clean and covered and watched. But it did the single thing no one had been able to do. It made the same arm available again and again.

The first person to be treated with it was a machinist at Boeing, a man in his late thirties named Clyde Shields. He was dying of kidney failure. He had a wife and children. In March of 1960, he was connected to the new apparatus, and he did not die. He went on, on dialysis, for more than eleven years (Source Notes S63).

Let that sentence sit for a moment. For the first time in the history of medicine, a person whose kidneys had failed forever lived for more than a decade on a machine. Eleven years of Saturday mornings and school plays and arguments about money, of a wife who learned to flush the tubing, of children who grew up knowing their father by the odd way he held his forearm at the dinner table. That did not exist before. The gift of Scribner and Quinton, and of the man who dared to be first, was time. Not an abstraction, not a statistic. The ordinary stuff of a human life.

I think about Clyde Shields's wife more than I think about the machine. The accounts are thin on her, and I will not invent her speech. But there is a particular loneliness in being the person who learns the apparatus, who stands at the edge of the room with the clamps in her hands, who understands that if she makes a mistake, the thing keeping her husband alive could fail. Families in this field have always been unpaid members of the clinical team, and the promise we are about to examine has never fully counted them.

## What the tube taught us

The history of dialysis could be told as a tale of ever-better machines, and in a narrow way it is. But when I look at the turning points, what I see is a pattern that the field has been slow to learn.

First, the decisive advance was not the glamorous one. Kolff's machine was the headline. Scribner's shunt, a bit of plastic tubing a plumber might have devised, was the breakthrough that turned a rescue into a way of life. Today the same pattern recurs. The access to the bloodstream, whether by a surgically created connection in the arm or a catheter placed through the skin, remains the place where much of the trouble in dialysis begins. I did not find, in the sources open to me, a clean current figure for how often it fails, and I will not manufacture one. But anyone who has spent a year in a dialysis unit knows the sentence: the problem is the access.

Second, the people who made the leap were outsiders to the established wisdom. Kolff was a young doctor in a small town in an occupied country. Quinton was an engineer rather than a physician. Shields was a machinist who agreed to be the first. Progress came from people who were close enough to the problem to feel it and unburdened enough by the way things were done to try something else.

Third, and this is the one I want to stay on: each advance, when it arrived, immediately produced a new and harder question, one that no machine could answer. If you can keep a person alive on a device, and the device and the staff to run it are scarce, whom do you keep alive?

## The first kitchen tables

The next chapter in the story is the one I find most affecting, because it is the one we nearly forgot. By the middle of the 1960s, in Seattle and in London, physicians had begun to train patients and their families to run the machines in their own homes (Source Notes S63). The reason was practical: there were too few chairs, too few nurses, and too many people who would die waiting. The consequence was extraordinary. Ordinary people, a machinist's wife, a schoolteacher's husband, learned to mix the fluids, prime the lines, insert the needles, watch the pressures, and respond to the alarms. They did it in spare rooms and basements and, as the later telling has it, at kitchen tables.

Imagine what it asked. A teenager, home from school, watching her father sleep with a machine beside the bed. A mother learning at forty to read a pressure gauge. A household reorganized around a schedule, with a spare bedroom becoming a clinic and a dinner hour becoming a window. These were not heroes in the usual sense. They were people who took on, without training, without pay, and mostly without applause, a duty that would be considered professional today.

Then something changed. When the national benefit arrived in 1973, it brought money, and money brought a new model: the dialysis facility, paid by the treatment, staffed by professionals, built to take patients in and put them in chairs. For many people that model was a mercy. It also, by most accounts, pushed home dialysis to the margins for decades. In the registry data for people starting dialysis, the share beginning on a home therapy was only 6.8 percent in 2010 and had reached 13.3 percent by 2020 (Source Notes S15). In other words, the most common way to get dialysis in the early 1960s became, within a lifetime, the uncommon way, and its return is still slow.

I raise this because it shows how a promise can reshape what it promises. The country offered to pay for dialysis. It did so in a way that made dialysis something done to people in a building rather than something done by them in a life. Neither choice was wrong. But a nation that pays for a thing is also deciding what that thing will look like, and we are still living inside that decision.

## An honest accounting of what we know

Before the next chapter takes up that question, I owe you a note on the sources for this one. The story of Kolff and Scribner is among the most retold in medicine, and it appears in textbooks, memorial essays, hospital histories and the recollections of the people who worked beside them. I have drawn the outline from that body of accounts and have not been able, in this setting, to consult the original papers and obituaries for every detail. The broad facts are not in dispute: the wartime machine, the first failures, the first survivor in 1945, the Teflon shunt of 1960, Shields as its first patient and his long survival. The specifics, such as the exact number of Kolff's early patients, the exact parts, and the precise ages, vary from source to source, and where they vary I have kept to the version that is both widely told and hedged. If you are citing this chapter for any purpose other than understanding, go to the primary record.

## What I would have you do with this

It is easy to read a story like this one with a pleasant sense of distance. The inventors are dead, the machines are in museums, and the progress is complete. It is not. Every part of the dialysis experience I describe in the pages ahead, the hours, the access, the recovery, the isolation, exists in the shadow of design decisions made by people who were solving the problem they could see. The question for us is who is solving the problem we cannot see, and whether anyone who actually lives with it is in the room.

If you are a patient, or someone who loves one, the most useful thing you can do after reading this chapter is small. Next time you are at a unit, ask the technician or the nurse what, in their experience, is the most annoying part of the equipment, and what they would change if they could. Write down what they say. The best ideas in this field have always started that way, with a person at the bedside noticing what everyone else had stopped noticing.

If you work in a clinic, ask your patients the same question and keep a list on the wall. If you are a student, an engineer, a nurse, a person with an odd skill and an idle weekend, know that the most important invention in the history of chronic kidney failure was a piece of plastic tubing designed by an engineer who asked what the doctors needed.

And if you are none of those things, remember Clyde Shields. A machinist said yes, when the safest thing would have been to say no. The history we inherit is made of people like him, who stepped forward when stepping forward had no guarantee. We owe them a better afterward than the one we have so far written.
