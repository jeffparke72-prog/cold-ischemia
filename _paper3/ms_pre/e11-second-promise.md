# Chapter 11: The Second Promise
EPIGRAPH: "And they that shall be of thee shall build the old waste places: thou shalt raise up the foundations of many generations."
BY: Isaiah 58:12 (King James Version, 1611)

In 1971, a man let a machine take his blood in front of a committee of Congress, and a year later the country made a promise. It was a good promise. It was kept, in part, with a seriousness that deserves our gratitude.

Now it is our turn.

I do not mean that every reader must become an activist, though some of you should. I mean something simpler and harder. A promise made by a previous generation is not discharged by admiring it. It is discharged by finishing it. Glazer and the lawmakers and the nurses and the families who organized in the 1960s did the first half. The second half belongs to people who are alive now, who have read what this book has laid out, and who can no longer say they did not know.

This chapter is the plainest in the book. It sets out ten commitments, each drawn from the evidence in the earlier chapters, each phrased so that it can be acted on by someone in the next week. None of them requires a miracle. Some require a decision. A few require courage. All of them are, I believe, within reach.

## The arithmetic of urgency

Let me begin with the one calculation that should move even the hardest ledger-keeper.

In 2021, the per-person annual cost to traditional Medicare was about $99,325 for a patient on in-center hemodialysis and about $43,913 for a patient with a functioning transplant (Source Notes S64). The difference is $55,412. For every person who moves from the machine to a working kidney, the program saves, on this arithmetic, more than fifty-five thousand dollars a year, and the person gains the afternoons that Chapter 4 described. I do not claim that every patient can be transplanted, or that the saving is exact for any individual. I claim that we are paying a very large premium to keep people on the more expensive and less humane option, and that closing even part of the gap would pay for a good deal of what follows.

That is the conservative case, and I believe in it. The human case is the one that moves me, and it needs no arithmetic.

## 1. Count the hours

Every dialysis unit in the country should ask every patient, once a month, how long it took them to feel like themselves after their last treatment, and should publish the answer. The international consensus exercise described in Chapter 4 found that patients and caregivers valued time and travel above what clinicians did (Source Notes S60). Then measure what you value. A national quality program that adds this single question will do more for the quality of life of dialysis patients than most of the programs now in place.

*Who can act:* unit managers, the dialysis providers, the federal agency that designs the quality programs.

## 2. Ask everyone about a transplant, every year

Only 12.3 percent of prevalent dialysis patients were on the transplant list in 2021 (Source Notes S64). Every patient should be asked, annually and in writing, whether they have been evaluated, and if not, why not. A patient who is not a candidate should hear the reason, and should be able to ask again when circumstances change.

*Who can act:* nephrologists, social workers, patients.

## 3. Put a navigator in every unit

The Emory trial and the Georgia facility trial in Chapter 2 point in one direction: people are listed when someone is paid to walk beside them (Source Notes S47). The navigator need not be a physician. She needs time, a telephone and a clear mandate.

*Who can act:* providers, state health departments, insurers.

## 4. Make the gift cost nothing

A living donor should be no worse off financially than before. The Honor Our Living Donors Act is a start, and the federal rulemaking that implements it should be watched closely (Source Notes S27). Every transplant center should publish a plain-language list of what donors will and will not be reimbursed for, and every employer should have a written donor-leave policy.

*Who can act:* Congress, the agency writing the rules, transplant centers, employers.

## 5. End the chair you earn by nearly dying

No state should fund care for kidney failure only at the point of crisis. The evidence in Chapter 7, from patients in three cities and from Colorado's experience, points to scheduled care as safer and cheaper per patient (Source Notes S50; Source Notes S52). Whatever one believes about immigration, a policy that appears to cost more and cost lives has no obvious defender.

*Who can act:* state legislators and Medicaid agencies.

## 6. Give every unit a witness

Intimacy, mood, loneliness and grief are in the room whether or not the chart asks about them. Every unit should have a social worker with time to sit down, a peer who has walked the road, and a routine of asking three questions: about intimacy, about mood, and about whom the patient could call at two in the morning (Source Notes S68; Source Notes S61). The peer-mentoring trials have been mixed (Source Notes S48), which tells us to improve the design and not abandon the idea.

*Who can act:* clinic leaders, patient groups, researchers.

## 7. Keep the roads open

A unit that is about to close should be required to publish a plan for its patients before it closes. Every unit should hold a tested disaster plan, move treatments up before forecast storms, and be ready for heat (Source Notes S49; Source Notes S71). The cost is small. The Sandy data show that early dialysis helped, and also that about four in ten patients did not receive it.

*Who can act:* providers, state and local emergency managers, the federal agency that certifies facilities.

## 8. Tell every donor family what happened to the gift

Within thirty days of a donation, every family should hear in writing what became of each organ, and every organ procurement organization and transplant center should publish its own record of kidneys recovered and not used. Nonuse reached 29.3 percent in 2024 (Source Notes S81). A figure that high should never be a surprise to the families.

*Who can act:* procurement organizations, transplant centers, the oversight agencies.

## 9. Open the trials to the people usually left out

As xenotransplantation and tolerance trials expand, their enrollment should be reported by race, geography, income and sex, in public, as they go. A technology that reaches only the well connected will deepen the divide it was meant to heal.

*Who can act:* trial sponsors, regulators, institutional review boards, journalists.

## 10. Change the words

Stop writing "noncompliant" in the chart. Stop calling a person a dialysis patient when she is a teacher who is on dialysis. Put the barrier, not the blame, in the record (Source Notes S70). A word costs nothing, and the people who read it are the ones who decide how much care to give.

*Who can act:* everyone who writes a note, a letter, a policy, a headline.

## A Wednesday in a unit that kept the second promise

I want you to be able to see it, so let me describe a place that does not yet exist. It is a Wednesday, a little before one in the afternoon, and the chairs are filling.

A man named Andre, a patient who has been here four years, arrives and finds a clipboard, not the sort that asks him to sign for anything, but a single question printed large: *How long did it take you to feel like yourself after Monday?* He writes "two hours," which is better than last month, when he wrote four. The nurse who collects it does not file it. She reads it aloud at the morning huddle, and the unit's staff talk for five minutes about what changed, because they have been trying cooler fluid for the people who said they felt washed out, and for Andre it seems to have helped.

A younger woman in the next chair has a navigator, a quiet person with a laminated list, who stops by to tell her that her transplant evaluation is scheduled for the twelfth and that a ride has been arranged. On the wall hangs a sheet showing the unit's plan if the river rises, with the telephone number of the shelter and the sequence of extra treatments. In the corner, a social worker with a calm face sits with a man who has not spoken in a month. She has an hour. She has been given an hour.

None of this is sentimental. It is a matter of who is employed to do what, and what the unit decides to count. Nothing in it needs a technology that does not exist. A unit like it could be built next year by a provider willing to treat these things as part of the job. I have seen pieces of it in the best clinics. I have not seen all of it in one.

## The objections, answered

A book that asks for this much should answer the objections.

*It costs too much.* The arithmetic at the head of this chapter is a start. A navigator, a social worker with time, a disaster plan and a monthly question are not expensive set beside the cost of a hospital stay or the difference between dialysis and a working graft. Nobody should claim every dollar will return, but the burden of proof falls on the arrangement that costs $99,325 a year per person to keep people on a machine, not on the one that tries to move them off it.

*The staff are already stretched.* Yes. And a workforce that is asked to do more with less will leave, which is what the data on nephrology fellowships suggest is already happening (Source Notes S20). That is an argument for hiring the right people, not for abandoning the task. A social worker or navigator is a force multiplier for the nurses, not a burden on them.

*Patients do not want to talk about these things.* Some do not. Many do, and have never been asked. The correct response to the uncertainty is to offer, gently, and to allow refusal. A question asked kindly and left unanswered is better than silence.

*It is not the system's job.* This is the objection I understand best and respect least. A system that took on the job of paying for these lives in 1972 took on, at the same time, the job of making them livable. The people who wrote that law did not mean the promise to stop at the door of the clinic. They did not imagine the whole of it.

## What I fear

I should say what worries me. I fear that these ten commitments will be read by people who agree, and nodded at, and filed. I fear that the number of lines in the system where something can be done is exceeded only by the number of reasons each line has for waiting for another. The regulators will wait for the Congress. The Congress will wait for the evidence. The evidence will wait for the funding. The funders will wait for the regulators. And in the meantime the woman in the vinyl chair will go on counting her afternoons.

I also fear a slower thing, which is that we will do just enough. We will fix what a camera can see. We will leave alone the quiet cruelties that cost nothing to maintain and a good deal to remove: the silence about the body, the gap between a person and the list, the distances that no one counts.

## What I believe

I also believe something, and I will say it plainly because this is the last chapter that carries an argument.

I believe that the kidney community is better than its system. I have spent years among nurses who stay late, technicians who learn a patient's name and the name of her dog, surgeons who return to the hospital at three in the morning, coordinators who sit in hallways, donors who give without being asked twice. These are not people who need to be persuaded to care. They need a structure that lets them.

I believe that a person on dialysis is not a patient first. She is a mother, a mechanic, a teacher, a poet. The afternoons she lives through are hers. And I believe that a country that once decided, in a few paragraphs and almost without debate, that no one should die for want of a machine can decide, with more debate and more care, that no one should be left alone with the afternoons.

Isaiah's line speaks of those who rebuild the old waste places and raise the foundations of many generations. It is a promise to the builders, not to the bystanders. The foundations here were laid by Kolff and Scribner and Shields, by the Herrick brothers, by the strangers in Seattle, by Glazer in a hearing room. What we build on them will be seen by people not yet born, some of whom will be sitting, someday, in a chair with a window nobody looks out of.

Let us give them a better view.

## What you can do

Pick one of the ten. Choose the one that is closest to where you stand. Write it on a card. Put it where you will see it.

Then do one thing about it this week. Make one call. Ask one question. Send one letter. Give one hour. And then, because the work is slow, do it again next week.
