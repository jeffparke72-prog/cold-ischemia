# Chapter 6: The Road, the Storm, and the Heat
EPIGRAPH: "Every valley shall be exalted, and every hill and mountain shall be made low: and the crooked shall be made straight, and the rough places plain."
BY: Isaiah 40:4 (King James Version, 1611)

A dialysis chair is not a place. It is a distance.

Everyone who lives with kidney failure learns this early, though they learn it in different ways. For some it is the eleven minutes to the unit that feel like an hour on the days when the body is heavy. For some it is the pickup truck on a county road in winter. For some it is the bus that does not run on holidays, and for some it is a weekday rental room in a town an hour from home, because driving three times a week is no longer possible. The treatment itself is portable, in principle, to anywhere with electricity, water and a trained hand. But the people who need it are not portable at all. They live where they live.

This chapter follows three kinds of distance: the long road, the weather that closes it, and the heat that makes the body unable to bear it.

## The long road

In April 2026, a reporter for KFF Health News described the closing of a small dialysis unit in rural Nebraska. Seventeen patients depended on it. One of them now faced a round trip of more than four hours for a treatment that lasts four. Another had rented a place near the new clinic, living away from home on weekdays and returning on weekends (Source Notes S31). I do not offer the story as proof of a national trend. It is one account. But it is a precise portrait of what happens, in human terms, when an administrative decision to consolidate a unit lands on a person who has already lost everything else he could give up.

The wider data say the story is not isolated. In 2023, the National Kidney Foundation wrote that roughly 170 dialysis facilities had closed within a year, citing staffing, supply chain pressures and costs, and noted a study finding that, in small-town and rural areas, the median one-way distance to a facility rose by about 6.6 miles when a nearby one closed (Source Notes S31). That figure rests on older data, and I mention it with that qualification. A registry-based study of closures in remote areas, covering the years 2001 to 2015, found that when a facility closed, patients nearby had more hospitalizations and more missed treatments, with a marginal rise in mortality, and that the burden fell more heavily on Black and Medicaid-enrolled patients (Source Notes S32).

The underlying economics are simple and unforgiving. Rural facilities more often have low patient volume and negative margins, with fewer stations and fewer late shifts (Source Notes S33). A unit with a dozen patients struggles to cover a nurse, a technician, a social worker and a water-treatment system. And behind the units stands a shortage of the physicians who oversee them. The fill rate for adult nephrology fellowship positions fell from 94.1 percent in 2010 to 65.8 percent in 2024, and, by one trade report, recovered to about 73 percent in the 2025 match (Source Notes S34). A rural health commentary from September 2026 made the same point with a stark contrast between the number of kidney specialists in rural and urban areas, and a federal projection of a 21 percent nephrologist shortage by 2037 (Source Notes S33).

I once thought of the rural nephrologist as a lonely figure, and in a sense she is. But when I try to imagine the system from the patient's side of the windshield, I see a slow erosion of the one thing a chronic illness cannot survive: the nearness of help.

## Harvest

Let me give the road a face, and let me say again that he is invented. Call him Walt. He is sixty-one, a wheat farmer in a county where the nearest dialysis unit is seventy miles away. He has gone to that unit three times a week for five years. In the winter it is merely tiring. In the fall, at harvest, it is a kind of grief, because the window to bring in a crop is short and the combine does not run itself, and the Tuesday, Thursday, Saturday schedule falls across the very days that the weather turns fair.

Last year he hired a neighbor's son for the cutting. The year before he did half himself and lost part of the field. This year he is asking his nephrologist whether a home therapy could fit, which in his case would require a clean room, a reliable power line, a trained helper, and a supply truck that comes down a gravel road in all weathers. The answer will not be simple. But he is asking. And the question he asks is not really a medical one. It is whether the life he has built, on land his grandfather broke, can survive the treatment that keeps him alive.

I did not find a national count of how many patients travel an hour or more for dialysis, and I will not manufacture one. The figures I could verify measure what changes when a unit closes, which is the telling part. When the nearest chair moves away, people do not simply drive farther. They miss treatments, they land in hospitals, and the mortality figures drift upward. A distance is a dose.

## The people at the end of a long road

No account of distance in American health care is honest if it leaves out the land that sits at the end of the longest roads. American Indian and Alaska Native communities have borne a heavier burden of kidney failure than almost any other group in the country. The American Kidney Fund reports that rates of kidney failure among American Indian and Alaska Native people run about double those of white Americans, though I could not locate the year and source underlying that comparison and offer it with that caution (Source Notes S35). The same advocacy source ties the disparity to health education, poverty, lack of insurance and, importantly, long travel distances to dialysis.

What interests me is the part of the story that has an unexpected ending. The Centers for Disease Control and Prevention reported in 2017 that the incidence of diabetes-related kidney failure among American Indians and Alaska Natives fell by 54 percent between 1996 and 2013, and by 2013 had reached the same level as among white adults with diabetes. Diabetes caused 69 percent of new kidney failure in this population against 44 percent overall. The agency credited a population-based approach to diabetes care pioneered by the Indian Health Service beginning in the mid-1980s (Centers for Disease Control and Prevention, 2017).

I want to underline what that means. A federal health program, working with tribal communities, applied unglamorous, sustained, upstream care to the disease that was driving kidney failure, and it worked. Not a miracle drug. Not a new machine. Decades of screening, follow-up, nutrition support and community health workers, delivered to people where they lived. If any single result in this book could be called a vindication of the conservative faith in patient institutions over grand schemes, this is it. And it did not end the disparity. The gap that remains is a measure of how much farther the same work would have to go.

## When the road closes

Now put weather on the road.

A dialysis machine needs three things the way a lung needs air: electricity, clean water in large volumes, and a road that a person can drive on. Remove any one and the schedule of treatment turns into a countdown. A 2019 systematic review of studies in the Americas found that the loss of power, drinkable water and transportation, along with facility closures, can disrupt care and lead to missed sessions and more hospitalizations and emergency visits (Source Notes S36).

The studies are specific. After Hurricane Katrina, a survey of patients from nine New Orleans dialysis units found that 16.8 percent had missed three or more treatments and 23 percent were hospitalized in the following month (Source Notes S36). After Hurricane Sandy in 2012, researchers followed 13,264 in-center patients and compared them with others. In the storm zone, 4.1 percent visited an emergency department in the following week against 2.6 and 1.7 percent in the two comparison regions, 4.5 percent were hospitalized against 3.2 and 3.8, and 1.83 percent died within 30 days against 1.47 and 1.60 (Source Notes S36). These are modest differences in absolute terms and large in a vulnerable population.

What helped was almost embarrassingly simple. Fifty-nine percent of patients received an early dialysis session before the storm hit, and those who did were less likely to go to an emergency room or be hospitalized in the following week, with odds ratios of 0.75 and 0.77. The commentary on the study noted that about 40 percent did not get the early session, meaning the preparedness was good and incomplete (Source Notes S36).

Puerto Rico after Hurricane Maria is the example I take comfort in. A study of mortality there found that deaths among people on dialysis were not higher than expected afterward, and the authors credited adherence to disaster-preparedness guidelines and an early government response (Source Notes S36). On an island whose infrastructure had been shattered, a population dependent on electricity and water came through without excess death. Preparation is not glamorous. Nobody wins an award for a disaster that did not happen. But a unit that holds a plan, tells its patients where to go, and moves treatments up when the forecast turns is spending a few hours so that no one loses days.

Imagine the woman on the Gulf Coast, at her kitchen table, with a storm named and swerving and a recorded voice from the clinic saying it will close if the power goes. She counts forward. Her next session is Thursday. The storm will arrive Wednesday night. She has a folder, a bag by the door, a daughter two states away who has told her, firmly, to come. She is among the lucky ones. A daughter two states away is a plan. Many people do not have one.

## The heat

There is a danger that has crept closer in recent years, and the research on it is young. In 2024, a nationwide analysis of 945,251 dialysis patients in 245 American cities found that deaths rose by 15 to 20 percent during extreme humid-heat events, with the largest relative increases in the urban Southeast (Source Notes S37). I treat this as an association and not as proof of cause, since many things differ between hot days and others. But the study's message is plain enough. Bodies that have lost the ability to shed fluid and salt are poorly equipped for weather that attacks them from the outside.

I searched for evidence on whether heat also causes people to miss their treatments, and found, in the studies I could reach, that the question had not been fully answered. A 2023 analysis of weather and missed hemodialysis noted that high temperatures had not been examined at all (Source Notes S37). That is a gap, and a significant one, in a world where the average summer is growing hotter. If I had to name a research question in this field that could be answered quickly and cheaply, with the data already collected by the large dialysis providers, it would be this one.

## An argument from stewardship

The conservative tradition, at its best, is a theology of stewardship: the belief that we hold things in trust, and that what we have been given should be handed on in better order than we found it. A road is held in trust. So is a clinic, a power line, a water main, a county's only nephrologist. We tend to notice these things only when they fail.

The citizens who live at the far end of the system do not want special treatment. They want what the 1972 promise implied, which is that where you were born would not decide whether you could live. A promise that is kept only within an hour's drive of a city is a different promise.

## What you can do

If you are on dialysis, make a one-page disaster plan this week and give a copy to your unit. Write down three things: where you would go if the road closed, whom you would call, and what you would eat. Ask your unit whether it moves treatments up before a forecast storm, and ask in what circumstances. If the answer is vague, ask again.

If you live in a rural area and your unit is under threat, ask your county health officials and your state's congressional delegation what plan exists for the patients. Ask in writing. Ask in public. A closed unit that is noticed in advance is often a unit that is rescued.

If you run a clinic, test your generator and your water supply this month and tell your patients the results. If you manage a transit service, ask the local dialysis units what time their patients need to travel, and ask yourself whether your schedule matches.

If you are a researcher, answer the heat question. The data are already sitting in someone's server.

And if you are someone who simply drives past a dialysis unit on your way to somewhere else, I ask only that you think of it as a place with a long road behind it. Isaiah's prophecy of a straightened path was not addressed to engineers. It was addressed to a people. But I like to think a grader and a surveyor would have understood him.
