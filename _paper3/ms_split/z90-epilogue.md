# Epilogue: The Window Nobody Looks Out Of
EPIGRAPH: "Cast thy bread upon the waters: for thou shalt find it after many days."
BY: Ecclesiastes 11:1 (King James Version, 1611)

Return with me, once more, to the room.

A row of recliners. A window that nobody looks out of. A television bolted high in a corner, tuned to a program no one chose. Machines that hum at slightly different pitches, so that the room carries a kind of chord. It is one o'clock on a Wednesday. People are reading, dozing, answering email, watching their own blood travel through clear tubing and come back clean.

You have been in this room many times now. You have seen it as a clock, as a body, as a road. You have seen it from the hallway outside an intensive care unit and from the other end of a long chain of strangers. And I would like you to see it a last time as a room in which a promise is being kept, today, by every person in it, in the plain act of staying.

Because that is what these people do. They stay. They have been taught patience whether they wanted the lesson or not. The man in the third chair is learning to read the language of his own pulse. The woman by the window, who has not looked out of it in three years, has a daughter in college and has told no one at work. They are not waiting for rescue. They are, in the quiet, steady manner of people who have no alternative, living.

I think often of the man in the hearing room in 1971. I do not know what he looked at while the machine ran. I imagine he looked at the faces of the men across from him, one after another, trying to see which of them had understood. I imagine he saw some who had, and some who had not, and that he kept his voice level anyway.

What I take from him is not his courage, which was great, but his method. He did not argue with the committee about the theory of insurance. He made them watch. The most effective advocacy in the history of this disease has always had that shape: someone shows a roomful of people what a life looks like, and refuses to look away.

If this book has done its work, you have been shown something. I hope you leave it with an itch you cannot scratch, a sense that many small injustices are being committed in the course of a promise that mostly works. I hope you do not leave it with despair. The facts here support a kind of impatient hope. A gene-edited kidney sat in a man for 271 days. A chain of strangers carried a gift across eleven states. A state discovered that scheduled care costs less than crisis care. A federal program, applied patiently to a community's diabetes, cut the rate of diabetes-related kidney failure there by more than half. These things happened. They are not fantasies. They are the work of people who decided that something was possible and went and did it.

Ecclesiastes tells us to cast our bread upon the waters, and promises that we shall find it after many days. I have always liked the verse for its refusal to promise quick returns. Reform in this field has always been slow. The seed that Kolff planted in a ruined country in 1943 flowered in a Seattle shunt in 1960 and in a hearing room in 1971 and in a federal law in 1972, and its fruit is still being gathered, and its roots are still unfinished. Whatever you do after reading this will probably bear fruit long after you have forgotten you did it. That is how it should be.

There is one small thing I would ask you to do before you close this book, because it is the thing I wish someone had done for me.

Write a letter to the next person. Not a grand one. Half a page, to a stranger who will sit in that chair next week, who is frightened and has not yet learned the vocabulary. Tell her the three things you wish you had known on the first day. Tell her which nurse to ask, and which question to ask the doctor, and what the first month will feel like, and that the washed-out feeling does not last forever, or that it does, and what helps. Tell her she is allowed to be angry. Sign it with your first name.

Hand it to the social worker. Ask that it be given to new patients. A hundred such letters, in a folder at the front desk, will do more for a frightened person than a pamphlet written by a committee. They are the oldest technology in medicine: one person who has been there, telling another where the stones are. And they are a way of keeping faith with the promise, in the smallest and most human unit in which it can be kept.

Someone will sit in that room next Wednesday who has never been there before. She will be frightened. She will look at the vinyl chair and the machine and the window, and she will wonder what it all will cost her. It is within our power to make sure that when she asks, someone is there with an honest answer, and a plan, and a hand.

That is the whole of it. A machine, a payment, and a promise. We have the first two. The third is ours to keep.
