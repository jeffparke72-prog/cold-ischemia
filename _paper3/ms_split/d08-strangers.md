@@PART Three | The Gift and Its Cost
# Chapter 8: Strangers, Chains, and the Receipt
EPIGRAPH: "No man is an island, entire of itself; every man is a piece of the continent, a part of the main."
BY: John Donne, Meditation XVII (1624)

Think of a woman whose husband needs a kidney. She is willing to give him one. She goes through the evaluation, the blood tests, the questions, and is told, with great gentleness, that she cannot. Her blood type will not do. Her tissue is a wrong match. The thing she is most willing to give is the one thing she cannot give him.

For most of the history of transplantation, that was the end of the story. A willing donor and a needy recipient, sitting side by side in a room, each ready, the match refusing. They went home, and he went back on the list, and she carried the knowledge of what she would have done.

In the years after the turn of the century, a small group of physicians and coordinators saw a way out, and it began with a very simple observation. Somewhere in the country, there was another couple in exactly the same predicament, whose wife's kidney was incompatible with her husband but would work for the first woman's husband. If the pairs could find each other, they could trade.

## The trade, and then the chain

That is the idea of paired kidney exchange, and it is one of the most beautiful pieces of applied mathematics in medicine. Two incompatible pairs swap donors, and two people receive kidneys who otherwise would not. Three pairs can swap in a cycle. As databases grew and matching software improved, the cycles grew longer, and then someone saw that the logic could be pushed further.

Suppose a person with no recipient in mind, an altruistic donor, walks in and offers a kidney to whoever needs it most. The kidney goes to the recipient of one pair. That pair's willing donor, no longer needed by their own loved one, then gives to the next pair, whose donor gives to the next. The chain ends when the last donor's kidney goes to a person on the deceased-donor waiting list. One act of pure generosity ripples down a line of people who would otherwise have been stranded.

In 2009 a group of researchers published a paper in the *New England Journal of Medicine* describing the first such chain that was not performed all at once but unfolded over months: a nonsimultaneous, extended, altruistic-donor chain, in which one anonymous donor set in motion a series of transplants (Rees et al., 2009). The surgeons knew the risk: a donor in the middle might back out after their own loved one had already been transplanted, and the chain would break. In that first chain, the pledge held. People kept their word.

The National Kidney Registry later reported a chain, begun in 2008, that included 30 transplants across 17 hospitals in 11 states over four months (Source Notes S41). The University of Alabama at Birmingham announced a single-center chain in which 74 donors and 74 recipients took part, with later reports of 100 or more recipients, though I could not confirm the current total (Source Notes S42). And the Registry has reported facilitating its ten-thousandth living-donor transplant, with 1,744 transplants in 2024 alone, a share of roughly 30 percent of all living-donor kidney transplants in the United States, according to its own figures (Source Notes S43). Those figures come from the organization itself and should be treated with that in mind. They are, nonetheless, a record of what strangers can do when a system invites them to.

## The person who gives to no one

I have wondered about the first donor in each chain. The person who walks in with no name attached to the gift. In the language of the field, a non-directed or altruistic donor. They are not saints and they are not strange, as far as the studies of donors can tell. They have often described, when asked, a feeling that is hard to translate: that they had two of something and someone needed one.

Imagine a man of fifty-two, a postal worker, whom I will call Ray. He read an article one afternoon about a stranger who had given a kidney, put it down, and kept thinking about it for a year. He told no one. He called a transplant center. He sat through the psychological screening, which includes the question every candidate dreads, *are you sure?*, and went home to think again. When the surgery was done, he woke in a recovery room to a nurse who told him that his kidney had been flown to a city two states away, that it had started working at once, and that the woman who received it was a mother of three.

He never met her. He may never. But what interests me is what happened next. Somewhere in the country, because of that kidney, a husband had a reason to give to a stranger, because his wife's match had just been made. And somewhere else the cycle continued. The gift was not a single event. It was a spark in a dry field.

I find this the most hopeful thing in the entire kidney ecosystem. It shows that we are not condemned to scarcity by fate. We are condemned by our failure to build the structures through which generosity can travel. Chains are such a structure. They convert a unique act of courage into a sequence of transplants, and they turn individuals who might have been turned away at the door into links.

## The receipt

Now I must tell you something less inspiring, which is what the gift costs the person who gives it.

A living donor does not pay for the surgery. By custom and by law, the recipient's insurance covers the donor's evaluation and operation. What the donor does pay for, and what no insurer covers, is everything around it. Time off work, unpaid in many jobs. Travel, to a center that may be hundreds of miles away. Lodging for the donor and a companion. Child care. Lost income during the six weeks, give or take, of recovery. The donor who is self-employed, or paid by the hour, or caring for an elderly parent, has the highest bills and the least cushion.

Researchers who studied this decades ago, including a team publishing in the *Clinical Journal of the American Society of Nephrology* in 2015, argued that financial barriers to donation were real and could be reduced (Tushla et al., 2015)†. The evidence from the years since is that the barriers have not vanished. Living donation in the United States peaked in 2004, and then fell, most of all among men, Black adults, and the young and the poor (Source Notes S11). A system that asks for generosity and sends the generous a bill is a system that shifts its own failure onto the people it needs.

Congress has started, belatedly, to address it. The Honor Our Living Donors Act, enacted in the Consolidated Appropriations Act of 2026 on the third of February, 2026, directs federal action to expand reimbursement for living donors. The responsible agency published notices in the summer of that year seeking comment, and a physicians' group letter I found put the proposed cap on one measure at about 21 percent, a figure I could not verify against the final notice (Source Notes S13). Private programs, including those described by the National Kidney Registry and the American Kidney Fund, now offer wage reimbursement and life-insurance protections to some donors (Source Notes S14). These help. They also depend on the charity of nonprofits and not the obligation of a government, and I think that matters.

A bill called the End Kidney Deaths Act, introduced in the 119th Congress, would go further, with a tax credit for living donors. Its supporters argue that it would increase donation and reduce the cost of dialysis. Its critics worry about the ethics of financial incentives. I could confirm its existence and its sponsors from a legislative tracker but could not verify either the odds of passage or the evidence supporting its projections, which come from campaign materials (Source Notes S44). I am neither endorsing nor condemning it. I raise it because it expresses a conviction I share: that the present arrangement, in which a donor's generosity is rewarded with a bill, cannot be right.

## A principle for the gift

I believe a donor should be able to give a kidney and be no worse off, financially, than the day before. Not better off. That would be a different thing, with different dangers. But no worse. I think that is a principle on which a conservative and a progressive can agree. The conservative sees in it the idea that you do not charge people to do a good deed. The progressive sees in it the idea that the poor must not be shut out of generosity. Both are right.

What would a gift without a receipt look like? It would be a donor who, from the first telephone call, is told in writing which costs will be covered, and who is reimbursed promptly. It would be a transplant center with a staff member whose sole job is the donor's financial navigation. It would be an employer who has been told, by law, that donor leave cannot be punished. These are not fantasies. In some systems abroad they are routine. They require only that a country that says it values its donors acts as though it does.

## The other receipt: the patient's

There is a second bill I must set beside the first, and it is the one the recipient pays, or the family of the one still waiting.

When insurance does not cover everything, and when a transplant requires moving, taking leave, paying for drugs, and surviving a year of reduced income, families turn to the one resource that costs nothing to try. They open an online fundraising page. A study of transplant fundraising campaigns in Canada, published in 2019 in *PLoS ONE*, examined 258 kidney campaigns and found that they raised only about 11.5 percent of the amount requested, against nearly half for liver campaigns. The authors concluded that crowdfunding eases some financial burden but does not do so equitably (Source Notes S45). An American analysis of a smaller set of transplant campaigns found an average raised of about $10,700 against an average goal of about $30,400 (Source Notes S45). A summary of a larger study of nearly 20,000 American organ transplant campaigns reported that Black and Hispanic campaigners raised less on average and were less likely to meet their goals, though I could not reach the primary paper and offer that with caution (Source Notes S46).

Think about what this means. The family with the largest circle of friends, the best photograph, the most followers, and the most time to craft a story gets the most help. The family with none of those gets less. A sick person's worth is being weighed, in effect, by her social network's generosity. We have, without deciding to, built a second selection committee, and it is worse than the first. The committee in Seattle at least tried to be fair. The crowd does not try.

## What you can do

If you are considering donating a kidney, ask the transplant center for a written list of what will and will not be covered, and ask for the name of the financial coordinator. Do not accept "it depends." Ask what depends on what.

If you are a patient with an incompatible donor, ask your center whether it participates in a paired-exchange program and which one. Ask how long the typical match takes. If the center does not participate, ask why.

If you run a transplant program, publish your donor-cost policy on your website in plain language. If you are an employer, write a donor-leave policy now, before an employee needs it.

If you are asked to share a fundraising page, share it. But also ask the harder question of yourself and your community: why are we making sick people do this?

And if you are a stranger, like Ray, who has been turning over a thought for a year, call a center. The paperwork is long. The chain is waiting.
