# Chapter 2: Who Shall Live
EPIGRAPH: "I have set before you life and death, blessing and cursing: therefore choose life, that both thou and thy seed may live."
BY: Deuteronomy 30:19 (King James Version, 1611)

Picture a conference room in Seattle in the early 1960s. It is evening. A handful of people, almost none of them doctors, sit around a table with folders in front of them. One is a minister, one a lawyer, one a banker. They have jobs and families, and they have been asked to do something that no committee of citizens had ever been asked to do, which is to decide which of several dying people will be given a place on the only dialysis machines in the region (Source Notes S3).

The center was new, and its machines were few. Scribner's shunt had opened the door to long-term treatment, and within months the doctors discovered that they had more candidates than chairs. A doctor can refuse to treat a patient only with great difficulty and great pain. So the physicians made an extraordinary decision, and I find it, even now, both admirable and terrible. They stepped back from the choice. They gave it to a small panel of laypeople, who would see the candidates only on paper, anonymously, stripped of their names, and decide.

The accounts say the panel was reported to include a minister, a lawyer, a banker, a homemaker, a labor leader, a state official, and one physician, though the sources differ on the exact makeup (Source Notes S3). What I am sure of is that they met, that they read, and that for each place on the machine they turned someone away.

## The article that named it

In November 1962, *Life* magazine published a long piece by a journalist named Shana Alexander under a title that would follow the committee for the rest of its existence: "They Decide Who Lives, Who Dies" (Source Notes S3). The nation had been told, in the idiom of the day, that medicine had performed a miracle, and now it was told what the miracle cost. A committee of citizens would assess a father's worth against a mother's, an engineer's against a clerk's. People began calling it the "God Committee."

I have read how the members themselves felt about the work, in the accounts that quote them, and it is not what the title suggests. They were not enjoying the power. At least some of them wrestled with it for the rest of their lives. They tried to be fair. They also, being human, made judgments that today would make us wince. The criteria that were reported included things like a candidate's dependents, income, education, and what was called "social worth," which, in the bluntest reading, meant the committee preferred patients whose lives looked most like the committee's own (Source Notes S3).

I do not say this to condemn them. They were handed an impossible task and did it in good faith. I say it because the history gives us a hard lesson about decisions that must be made when resources are scarce: someone will always make them. The question is whether it is done openly or in the dark, by people who know they are doing it or by people who have persuaded themselves that they are not.

## The answer the country gave

The committee's work eventually became unnecessary, and it became unnecessary in an unusual way: the public decided that nobody should be asked to do it. By the end of the 1960s, the idea that a nation of this wealth could let people die for lack of a machine, and let a committee choose among them, had become intolerable. Patients and their families organized. They testified. Some did it with their own blood, as Shep Glazer had.

The Social Security Amendments of 1972, signed on the thirtieth of October that year, contained a short provision that extended Medicare coverage to people of any age who needed dialysis or a transplant because their kidneys had failed (Source Notes S1). It is an oddity of American law. Medicare was designed for the old, and later extended to the permanently disabled. This was the first time the country had said that a single organ's failure was enough to qualify a person of any age. By most accounts it passed with remarkably little debate, as a rider to a much larger bill, because few in Congress thought the stakes would be large. The projection, as it has been handed down, was something in the neighborhood of ten thousand patients and a bill of about $135 million (Source Notes S1). The accounts differ as to whether that figure was annual.

The benefit took effect on the first of July, 1973.

## What the promise covered, and what it did not

To grasp the American promise to the kidney patient you have to read it the way a lawyer would, which is to say, for the sentences it leaves out.

It covered dialysis. It covered the transplant operation. It put the country's money behind the thing Seattle's committee had been unable to supply, which was chairs. And it did so quickly: in the decade after 1973 the number of dialysis patients in the United States grew many times over.

What it did not do, at the start, is worth noticing. It did not cover the cost of the drugs that keep a transplanted kidney alive for long. For many years, a person who qualified for Medicare only because of kidney failure lost that coverage thirty-six months after a successful transplant, and with it, the coverage for the immunosuppressive drugs without which the new kidney would be rejected. A person could be handed a gift of life by a donor and then lose the means of protecting it. Only in recent years has Congress created a narrower benefit, known as Part B-ID, to cover those drugs for people who would otherwise lose them, available since December of 2022 (Source Notes S5). The pharmacy counter is a place I return to later in this book. For now I want only to point out that the promise was written with the dialysis chair at its center, and it has taken half a century to fill in the rest of the picture.

It did not do anything about the shape of life around the chair. It did not say anything about work, or the body, or the distance between a house and the nearest unit. Those omissions were not malice. They were the natural limits of what a committee of lawmakers could imagine.

## The arithmetic that nobody wants to say out loud

Let me do something now that makes some of my colleagues uncomfortable, which is to look at the cost without flinching.

The program has grown very large. In 2023 the Medicare program paid $55.3 billion for people with kidney failure (Source Notes S2). Beneficiaries with kidney failure are about one percent of all Medicare beneficiaries, and by one advocacy estimate their care accounts for about seven percent of spending in traditional Medicare (Source Notes S6). Anyone who tells you the cost is trivial is not being straight with you. A fiscal conservative has a right to ask whether the money is being well spent, and I would add that those of us who care about patients have a duty to ask it first.

Here is the figure that I wish more people carried in their heads. The per-person cost to traditional Medicare in 2021 was about $99,325 a year for a patient on in-center hemodialysis, about $86,976 for a patient on peritoneal dialysis, and about $43,913 for a patient with a functioning transplant (Source Notes S7). Put plainly, a functioning kidney costs the program less than half of what keeping the same person on a machine costs, and the person is, by almost every measure, better off.

Now set that beside this one: only 12.3 percent of prevalent dialysis patients were on the transplant waiting list in 2021 (Source Notes S7). Not 12.3 percent of those who could benefit. Of everyone on dialysis.

I do not claim that all the others could safely receive a kidney. Some are too sick, some are too old, some do not want it, and some cannot be matched. The wait, as those who live in it know, is measured in years. But the gap between the chance of a transplant and the reality is so wide that it should trouble anyone, whatever their politics. We have a program that pays more for the worse outcome, and then waits for patients to find the better one.

## The committee no one sees

I said that a committee decides who lives. I want to tell you where the committee sits now.

It is not a room in Seattle. It is the series of small decisions that determine whether a given person with kidney failure is ever told about a transplant, ever referred to a transplant center, ever completes the evaluation, ever reaches the list. At each of those stages some people drop away, and not at random. A randomized trial at Emory University enrolled 401 patients and found that those who were given a patient navigator were more likely to be waitlisted, 32 percent against 26 percent, though they took longer to get there, by an average of 126 days (Source Notes S8). A larger trial run in dialysis facilities in Georgia, covering more than 9,000 patients in 134 facilities, found that a structured intervention improved referral to transplant centers and narrowed the racial gap (Source Notes S8).

These are the trials that deserve our attention, because they tell us that the invisible committee can be changed. A person who is hustled to the list by a navigator is not a person who deserved it more. She is a person who, absent the navigator, was not told. The historical committee at least looked the candidates in the eye on paper. The modern one often does not look at all.

Even the rules written to correct past injustice show how hard this is. For years, the formula used to estimate kidney function included a race adjustment that made Black patients appear healthier than they were, delaying their qualification for the waiting list. In 2023 the national transplant network adopted a policy to backdate waiting time for Black candidates whose listing had been delayed by that formula. One analysis found that the change was associated with more transplants, 5.3 more per 1,000 listings (Source Notes S9). It was a correction of a wrong long after the fact, and it is worth noticing how recent it is.

## Choosing life

The verse at the head of this chapter comes at the end of a long speech in which Moses sets before a people the consequences of their choices and tells them to pick the harder, better path. The commandment is not abstract. "Choose life, that both thou and thy seed may live." Two generations, in the one sentence.

I read it as a description of what the founders of the American kidney program were trying to do in 1972, and as a challenge to the rest of us. They chose life, and in the process of choosing it they created a machine of enormous scale, whose workings they could not have predicted. The challenge to us is not to un-choose it. The challenge is to finish choosing. A nation that decides, as ours did, that no one should die for want of a dialysis machine has committed itself to the next question: what kind of life, for how long, with whose help, and at whose elbow?

## What you can do

If you are a patient or a family member and you are on dialysis, ask your care team this week, in writing if you can, one plain question: *Am I a candidate for a transplant, and if not, why not?* Ask for the answer in terms you can understand. If the answer is that you have never been evaluated, ask who can start the referral. The invisible committee is made of silences, and silences end when someone asks.

If you work in a dialysis unit, ask yourself what share of your patients have been asked about a transplant in the past year, and who in your building keeps that list. If no one does, that is the first fix.

If you are a legislator, a staffer, or a citizen who writes to one, ask the question that the 1972 bill's authors did not: not how much we pay for dialysis, but how many of the people we pay for are being offered the cheaper, better thing.

And if you are simply someone who has read this far, I would ask that you remember the strangers at the table in Seattle, anonymous even now, who tried to be fair under an impossible weight. The system we have built should make their burden unnecessary. We do not yet fully deserve that.
