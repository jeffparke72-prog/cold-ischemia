# Chapter 7: The Chair You Earn by Nearly Dying
EPIGRAPH: "I was a stranger, and ye took me in."
BY: Matthew 25:35 (King James Version, 1611)

It is one in the morning in a hospital emergency department in a city I will not name, and a man is sitting upright on a gurney because he cannot lie down. He is thirty-eight. His breathing is shallow and quick. His ankles are swollen to the shape of loaves. A monitor over his shoulder shows a heart rhythm that makes the resident look twice at the screen.

He is, in this account, a composite, drawn from the situations described in the research literature. He has lived in the United States for most of his adult life. He works, when he can, in jobs that do not ask for papers and do not offer insurance. A year ago his kidneys began to fail, and he did not know, because he did not go to a doctor, because doctors cost money he did not have and attention he could not afford to draw. Tonight the poison has built to the point where his body can no longer hide it.

They will dialyze him. They will do it urgently, and well, because the people in this room are good at their jobs. In a day or two he will go home, feeling better. And he will not be offered another chair until he gets sick enough to be rushed back.

This arrangement has a name: emergency-only hemodialysis. In some states, people who lack legal immigration status and have kidney failure can receive dialysis only when they arrive at an emergency department in a crisis. Between crises there is no schedule, no clinic, no nurse who knows his name.

## A narrow question, asked plainly

I want to approach this subject with care, because it is politically charged and I do not intend to settle any argument about immigration in these pages. My question is narrower, and, I think, one on which people of very different convictions can agree. How well does this arrangement work as medicine? And what does it cost?

The most careful comparison I found followed 211 undocumented patients who started dialysis between 2007 and 2014 at three hospitals. Two of them, in Denver and Houston, offered emergency-only dialysis. The third, in San Francisco, offered standard, scheduled dialysis. After adjustment, the patients receiving emergency-only treatment had a higher risk of death: a three-year hazard ratio of 4.96, though its interval was wide enough to include no difference (0.93 to 26.45), and a five-year hazard ratio of 14.13, with an interval wider still (1.24 to 161). They also spent about ten times as many days in acute care as would be expected (Source Notes S38).

Read that with the care it deserves. This was an observational study, and patients differed in ways that adjustment could not fully remove: those who received standard care were more likely to start with a fistula or graft and to have better blood counts. The enormous intervals show how little the data can say about the size of the difference. They do not erase its direction.

A second study of 181 adults compared scheduled with emergency-only dialysis. One-year mortality was 3 percent for the scheduled group and 17 percent for the emergency-only group. The emergency-only patients made six more emergency-department visits per month, had 1.5 more hospitalizations and spent ten more days in the hospital over six months, and their care cost $5,768 more per month (Source Notes S39).

## The costliest door

I have heard the argument made, in good faith, that offering scheduled dialysis to people who are not lawfully present is a benefit the country cannot afford. I will not rule on that. I will say only what the numbers imply. The emergency-only model is not cheap. It is expensive in the dollars spent on repeated crises, it is expensive in lives, and it is expensive in the quieter coin of hospital beds and clinicians' conscience. A system that waits until a person is close to death before giving him the treatment that would have kept him well has chosen the costliest door.

Colorado offers a natural experiment, and the best one I could find. In 2019, the state decided to count kidney failure as a qualifying condition for emergency Medicaid, which extended coverage to scheduled outpatient dialysis three times a week for those who qualify. A published account reports that before the change, the state's emergency Medicaid dialysis spending for this population came to close to $20,000 per patient per month in 2017 and 2018, and that after the change, the average monthly cost per patient was $5,574 even as the number of patients served rose to about 135 a month (Source Notes S40). The account relies on state data reported in a secondary publication, and the totals are not easy to reconcile with one another, so I lean on the direction and not the decimals. Scheduled care cost less per patient than emergency care.

A fiscal conservative should find this interesting. A humanitarian should find it interesting for a different reason. For once the arithmetic and the conscience point in the same direction.

## The nurse's side of the gurney

There is another witness to this arrangement, rarely quoted. In the qualitative literature I read, some nephrologists describe the moral distress of providing emergency-only care as among the hardest parts of their work. They know the patient will return, sicker. They know that the intervention they provide tonight, however skilled, is a repair they will have to make again. They are doing the right thing in a structure that makes it hard to do the right thing for long.

The oath that physicians have sworn for twenty-five centuries binds them to use treatment for the benefit of the sick. In the version translated by Jones in 1923, a physician swears to "use treatment to help the sick according to my ability and judgment, but never with a view to injury and wrong-doing." The oath does not say which sick people. A profession that sends a man home after a rescue, knowing that he will return in worse condition, has not quite honored its oath, though it may have obeyed its rules. I have no quarrel with the people who work in this system. I have a quarrel with the system that puts them there.

## The week of a man living on a threshold

Let me ask you to follow our composite home. He rents a room with two other men. His employer, who pays in cash, does not know he is ill. He learns, as people in his position do, to take the measure of his own body the way a sailor reads the sky. He knows what fluid in his legs means, and he knows the taste at the back of his throat when the poison is rising. He drinks as little as he can bear. He avoids the foods he has been told to avoid, which are most of the foods he can afford. He works as long as the weakness lets him, because a missed day is a missed payment, and then he makes a calculation no one should have to make: how bad can I let it get before I must go in?

This is the true cruelty of the arrangement. The system makes the patient his own triage nurse. It rewards the patient who waits and holds on, because only the sickest are served, and it punishes the one who comes early, because early is not an emergency. A man who presents too soon is told, politely, that he does not yet qualify. A man who waits too long may not reach the door.

I do not know how many people live this way at any moment, and I have not found a reliable national count. Estimates exist and they differ. What I know is the shape of the life, which I have heard from those who study it and from those who have lived near it, and it is this: a week arranged around a threshold, and a family that learns not to ask how he is.

A child in that home learns something, too. She learns that her father's illness is a thing that comes in the night and leaves in the morning, that it is not to be discussed with teachers, and that the hospital is a place where he is helped and then, somehow, sent away. What she makes of a country that treats her father like this I cannot say. I suspect she will remember it for the rest of her life.

## What I do not know

I should be exact about the limits of the evidence. I found no new study of emergency-only dialysis from 2025 or 2026. The principal cohort studies date from 2018 and rely on data that are now a decade old. I found no outcome data from Texas beyond the early cohort. Policies have shifted in some states since, and some of the changes may have altered the picture. The estimates of the difference vary, and the largest carry wide uncertainty.

But the shape is consistent, and it fits what the previous chapters have shown. People who cannot reach a chair on a schedule are not saved money. They are charged later, in worse form, at a higher price. The distance between a man and his treatment is not only measured in miles. It is measured in paperwork, in fear, and in the unwritten rule that you must be dying before you are served.

## The stranger

The verse at the head of this chapter is from the passage in Matthew in which the king separates the righteous from the unrighteous by a single test: what did you do for the least of these? I was hungry, and ye gave me meat. I was thirsty, and ye gave me drink. I was a stranger, and ye took me in. The passage does not ask the stranger's papers. It asks only whether the door opened.

You need not share my faith to find the point useful. The principle is old and widely held across traditions, and it is not partisan. A decent society is partly measured by what it does for the person it has no obligation to help. The emergency-only chair is a test of that principle, and it is a test the country has been failing slowly and expensively.

I think of the man on the gurney. He does not know about hazard ratios. He knows that the last time he was breathless, they helped, and that he will be told to come back when it gets bad again. He is waiting in a different kind of line, one whose only way forward is to become sicker. If there is a principle of kidney care in this book, it is this one: no one should have to earn a chair by nearly dying.

## What you can do

If you live in a state that provides emergency-only dialysis, find out what your state's rules are, in writing, from your health department. Ask your state representative one question: "What does it cost us per patient, per month, to treat people only in crises, compared with scheduled care?" Colorado has an answer. Ask why yours does not.

If you work in a hospital that provides emergency dialysis, keep a simple count, even if only on a whiteboard in the staff room: how many patients return, how sick they are when they do, and how many days they stay. Numbers on a whiteboard have changed more policies than speeches.

If you are a lawmaker, read the Colorado experience and ask your staff to repeat the analysis for your state.

If you are someone whose political convictions make you hesitant about this chapter, I ask you to set aside the larger argument for a moment and consider the narrow one. Whatever you believe about who should be here, a policy that appears to cost more and to cost lives, compared with the alternative, has a hard case to make for itself.
