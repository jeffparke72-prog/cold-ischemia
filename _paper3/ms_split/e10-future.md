# Chapter 10: The Pig, the Patient, and the Quiet Revolutions
EPIGRAPH: "Nature, to be commanded, must be obeyed."
BY: Francis Bacon, Novum Organum (1620)

In the autumn of 2025, a man in Massachusetts went back to dialysis.

His name was Tim Andrews. For nearly nine months he had lived without it, with a kidney from a gene-edited pig sewn into his body at Massachusetts General Hospital in January of that year. By the reports available to me, he was off dialysis for 271 days, a record for a patient living with a pig kidney, before the organ began to fail and was taken out (Source Notes S55). That is a little over thirty-eight weeks of Saturdays on which he did not sit in a chair.

Let me stay with that for a moment, because the news coverage tended to treat it as a failure, and I do not read it that way. For nine months a man had a life that his illness had denied him. He could plan a week without a machine in it. He could say yes to a weekend. And when the organ gave out, he returned to the chair, and some months later, in January of 2026, received a human kidney, becoming, by Harvard Medical School's account, the first patient to progress from a pig kidney to a human one (Source Notes S55).

Everything I have written so far in this book describes a promise made in 1972 and imperfectly kept. This chapter asks what a promise would look like if the biology changed. The honest answer is that no one knows, and that anyone who tells you otherwise is selling something.

## What has actually happened

The record is short and still being written. In March of 2024 surgeons at Massachusetts General Hospital transplanted a gene-edited pig kidney into Richard Slayman, a man with kidney failure; he died in May, and the hospital said at the time that there was no indication that his death was the result of the transplant (Source Notes S56). Later in 2024, a team at NYU Langone transplanted a pig kidney into Lisa Pisano, who also had severe heart disease. Then, in November, a pig kidney was placed in Towana Looney, who lived for roughly four months with it before it was removed in the spring of 2025 because of rejection (Source Notes S56). These accounts come from widely reported news coverage, and I was not able to re-confirm every number in this session. I offer them as the outline of what is known.

By the fall of 2025, the regulators had moved. The Food and Drug Administration cleared formal clinical trials. In September of that year, the pig producer eGenesis received approval for a trial of its gene-edited kidney, with a Massachusetts General nephrologist helping to lead it (Source Notes S57). In November 2025, United Therapeutics announced the first transplant in its EXPAND trial of the UKidney, in patients with end-stage kidney disease (United Therapeutics Corporation, 2025). The National Kidney Foundation, in an April 2026 explainer, described the trials as having started that fall (Source Notes S57). A journal review published in 2026 in the *American Journal of Transplantation* surveyed where the field stood, and one study in *Nature Medicine* in 2025 profiled the immune response in a living recipient of a gene-edited pig kidney (Source Notes S55).

Notice what the sentence structure of the whole story is. First, one patient. Then, a handful. Then, a trial. This is how medicine has always moved, and the pace is neither slow nor fast. It is the pace of evidence. I think this is the most important thing a patient can understand about the news: the headlines are a narrative of triumph and disaster, but the underlying reality is a long series of small, careful steps, and the decisive evidence has not yet come.

## What a spare part would not fix

I want to be careful here, because the enthusiasm around xenotransplantation can distort the planning for everything else.

Suppose it works. Suppose that within a decade, pig kidneys can be offered to thousands of people each year, with the survival of the graft approaching that of a human organ. Even so, a pig kidney will not mend a marriage, or bring back a job, or lengthen a road, or pay the tab for the immunosuppressants, or reach a man in a county without a transplant center. It will reach, first, the people with the best access to a handful of academic hospitals. It will reach the people whom the navigator trials in Chapter 2 showed to be already reaching the list.

Every technology in this history has followed that law. The Scribner shunt made long-term dialysis possible, and then there were not enough chairs. The 1972 law made the chairs, and then the chairs did not reach the countryside. If the pig kidney arrives, it will be a magnificent solution with an old shadow: the question of who is first.

This is why I think we should treat the arrival of any new organ source as a deadline, and not a rescue. We have, at most, a few years to fix the parts of the system that new technology will exploit: referral, evaluation, listing, and the systems of follow-up. If we do not, then the future will be the present, only more expensive.

## Quieter revolutions

The pig kidney will make the news. The revolutions I find most hopeful are the ones that do not.

**Tolerance.** The reason transplant recipients take drugs for life is that the immune system sees the new kidney as foreign. A long-sought goal has been to teach the body to accept it. A phase 3 trial of a cell-based therapy called MDR-101, from Medeor Therapeutics, treated 20 patients, compared with 10 controls, and was reported in the *American Journal of Transplantation* in July 2025. About 95 percent of treated patients had been able to stop all immunosuppression about a year after treatment, and about 75 percent remained off the drugs for more than two years, though four had to resume them (Source Notes S58). The numbers are small. The treatment applies to a specific group, recipients of kidneys from certain living donors. But think of what it implies. A person who has been taking drugs every day, with the infections, the cancers and the cost, could someday be free of them. A nephrologist described the field in an April 2026 interview as a frontier (Transplant tolerance, 2026)†.

**Preservation.** Chapter 9 described how much of a kidney's fate is decided in the cooler. New technologies pump fluid through the organ while it waits, or oxygenate it. A trial that compared hypothermic machine perfusion with ordinary cold storage published in the *New England Journal of Medicine* in 2009 and was followed up over ten years, a result I know only through the device maker's announcement (Moers et al., 2009; Organ Recovery Systems, 2025). A newer randomized trial of warm perfusion in kidneys from donors after circulatory death appeared in *Nature Medicine* in 2023 (Hosgood et al., 2023)†. Another approach adds an oxygen-carrying molecule derived from the hemoglobin of a marine worm to the preservation fluid; a French study of 60 grafts reported strong survival at 48 months, but the numbers come from the manufacturer and a small trial, and I grade them cautiously (Source Notes S59). The Cold Ischemia Foundation, of which I am the founder, has published its own explainers on this technology (Cold Ischemia Foundation, n.d.-a), and I note that as a disclosure and not as evidence.

**The home and the wearable.** A device to put the kidney on a person's body has long been an engineer's dream. A wearable artificial kidney trial reported in *JCI Insight* in 2016 found it well tolerated and effective at clearing waste, but was stopped after the seventh participant because of technical problems with the device (Source Notes S60). A research project in California reported a bioreactor that survived in a pig without an immune attack, and a university page suggested human trials might come in 2026 or 2027, which I could not verify (Source Notes S61). In the meantime, the Food and Drug Administration cleared a new home hemodialysis system in November 2024, according to a trade report (Source Notes S62). The incident share of home dialysis, as I noted in Chapter 1, was 13.3 percent in 2020, up from 6.8 percent a decade earlier.

**Upstream.** The most valuable kidney is the one that never fails. The Centers for Disease Control and Prevention estimated in March 2026 that 14 percent of American adults, about 37 million people, have chronic kidney disease (Centers for Disease Control and Prevention, 2026). An advocacy source puts the number at 35.5 million and says that nine in ten are unaware (Source Notes S63). An earlier federal analysis, reported in the trade press, found that 96 percent of people with early-stage disease were unaware of it, and 48 percent of those with severely reduced function and not yet on dialysis (Source Notes S64). Testing for the protein in urine that signals kidney damage, a cheap and simple test, was done in only 52.9 percent of adults with type 2 diabetes in one primary care analysis (Source Notes S65).

Meanwhile the treatments have improved. The FLOW trial, reported in 2024, was the first large trial dedicated to kidney outcomes of a drug in the semaglutide class in people with type 2 diabetes and chronic kidney disease, and found a benefit regardless of whether patients also took another class of drug, an SGLT2 inhibitor (Source Notes S66). The tools exist. What does not yet exist is the habit of using them.

## A thought experiment for 2031

Let me describe a day that has not happened. I offer it as an exercise and not a forecast, and I ask you to read it with the suspicion it deserves.

It is a Tuesday in a clinic in a mid-sized city. A nephrologist is meeting with two patients. The first is a retired executive, sixty-eight, who has been on dialysis for two years. He has a good insurance plan and a daughter who is a lawyer. By the time he sits down she has read three papers and has a list of questions. He is offered a place in a pig-kidney trial in a month. The second patient is a home health aide, sixty-two, who has been on dialysis for six years and who drove ninety minutes to be here. She has heard of pig kidneys on the television. No one has told her she might qualify. She has never been evaluated for a transplant of any kind, because years ago someone said she was too old, and nobody asked again.

Which of the two is the better candidate? In a strict medical sense, perhaps neither, perhaps both. The point of the thought experiment is the gap in the room. The executive is surrounded by people who will carry him to a new technology. The aide is surrounded by people who are too busy to stop. The kidney, whatever its origin, will reach the first before the second, unless somebody builds a bridge.

You can build that bridge now. It is made of plain things: a referral for every patient who has not had one in two years; a navigator in every unit; a standard of reevaluation when guidelines change; a recruiting rule for trials that says, among other things, that the people who are hardest to reach must be reached first. None of it requires a laboratory. All of it requires a decision.

## The humbling part

I have written enthusiastically, and I should now be honest about how often this field has been wrong. The artificial kidney was announced in 1945 and the wearable version was still being tested seventy years later. The first pig-to-human transplants in the modern era failed in months. The enthusiasm that surrounds any kidney breakthrough has a way of outrunning the evidence, and patients are the ones who bear the cost of disappointment.

So I will make a modest claim. I do not claim that these technologies will arrive. I claim that the possibility of their arrival changes our obligations in the meantime. A system that is about to change should be preparing for it, in the way a prudent household prepares for a birth. It should be building the registries to follow the first thousand recipients carefully. It should be training the nurses. It should be ensuring that the early trials enroll the people who have been left out of every earlier one, the rural, the poor, the old, the Black and Native, and the women. And it should write down now, before the first commercial offer, who gets the first kidney.

## What you can do

If you have kidney disease, ask your nephrologist whether you are a candidate for any clinical trial, and ask where trials are recruiting. Ask whether you would qualify for a xenotransplant or tolerance trial. Do not be discouraged by the first no; eligibility rules change.

If you have diabetes or high blood pressure or a family history of kidney disease, ask your primary care clinician for two simple tests, one for kidney function in the blood and one for protein in the urine. Ask what the results mean. Ask again next year.

If you work in transplantation, ask your institution to describe in public how it will choose participants for early trials of new technologies, and whether people from communities that are usually left out will be invited.

If you are a journalist, remember that every first patient is also the last patient of a story that began with someone else.

And if you are a person who simply hopes, remember that hope is not the same as a plan. The plan is the part that needs you.
