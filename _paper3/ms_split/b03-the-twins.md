# Chapter 3: The Gift Nobody Had to Give
EPIGRAPH: "Greater love hath no man than this, that a man lay down his life for his friends."
BY: John 15:13 (King James Version, 1611)

On the twenty-third of December, 1954, two young men lay in adjoining operating rooms in a Boston hospital, one of them healthy and the other close to death. They were identical twins. They were named Ronald and Richard Herrick, and by the time the day was out, one of them would be missing a kidney and the other would have it (Source Notes S3).

Imagine the night before. It is the week of Christmas, and the hospital is quiet in the way hospitals are quiet at the end of a year. Richard is twenty-three, a veteran, thin, yellowed by the poisons his failing kidneys are no longer clearing. He has been told that nothing can save him. He knows that the doctors have been talking about an idea so unusual that most of the profession thinks it improper: that a kidney might be taken from his brother and sewn into him.

Now imagine Ronald. He is, as far as anyone can tell, entirely well. If he does nothing, his life will go on unchanged. If he agrees, a surgeon will open him, remove a working organ that his body needs and that cannot be returned, and he will leave the hospital with one kidney, a scar, and a debt that his brother can never repay.

I have thought a great deal about what that decision costs, and I should say that I am not describing it from the outside only. There is a kind of knowledge that comes to the people who stand at the edge of a gift of this kind, and I will leave it at that. But I can tell you what the history records: that the surgical team, led by a young surgeon named Joseph Murray, worked for months to be sure the operation was right, that the physicians fretted over the ethics of operating on a healthy person for another's sake, and that the brothers' identical genetic makeup had been checked, by some accounts using fingerprints and skin grafts, before anyone dared to proceed (Source Notes S3).

## What the twins proved

The operation worked. The kidney began to make urine on the table. Richard, within days, was clear-headed and hungry. The immune system, which rejects almost any foreign tissue, saw nothing foreign, because the tissue was his own in every way that mattered. He went on to marry, to have children, and to live for roughly eight more years before his original disease returned and took the kidney (Source Notes S3). Ronald lived on for decades, a man with one kidney and a clean conscience. Murray, in time, was awarded the Nobel Prize in Physiology or Medicine, in 1990, for what the twins had begun (Source Notes S3).

The scientific lesson was narrow and immense. A transplanted organ could function in a human being. The obstacle was not surgical. It was immunological. Almost every other patient with kidney failure had no identical twin, and the surgeons would spend the next decades learning to suppress the immune system enough to stop it from destroying the new kidney without letting the patient die of infection.

But there was a second lesson, and it is the one I want to dwell on, because it has been lost under the first.

The kidney did not come from a laboratory. It came from a person. The whole structure of transplant medicine, which now includes tens of thousands of operations a year, regulation, registries, hospitals, and a vast apparatus of coordinators, begins with a man in his twenties who had a spare organ and a brother he loved. The foundational act of transplantation was not a technical triumph. It was a gift.

## The strange moral shape of the gift

We take it for granted now, but the principle at work in that operating room is unlike anything else in medicine. In nearly every other field, a physician treats the patient on the table. Here the physician harms one person in order to help another, and does it with the first person's full agreement. The justification is not a medical benefit to the donor. It is the donor's own decision, which the law and the profession agree to treat as sacred.

Later generations, wanting more organs than the gifts of family could supply, built an elaborate structure around that sacred decision. In 1984 Congress passed the National Organ Transplant Act, which, among other provisions, made it a federal crime to buy or sell a human organ (National Organ Transplant Act, 1984). The reasoning was and remains persuasive to many: that a market in organs would turn the poor into a supply, that the rich would buy the kidneys of the desperate, and that the whole practice would corrupt the thing that made it beautiful. Today the law stands, and a donor in the United States may be reimbursed for certain expenses but cannot be paid.

I say all this without taking a side in the argument about markets, which I will leave to the philosophers. What I want to draw out is the consequence for everyone else. If we insist that kidneys be given and not sold, we have taken on an obligation that the market would have discharged automatically, which is to make the gift as easy, as safe and as free of cost to the giver as we can. A system that depends on generosity cannot also be stingy with the generous.

## Why a living kidney is different

A kidney from a living donor tends to last longer than one from a deceased donor, and the difference is not small. In a registry analysis, the median survival of a transplanted kidney from a deceased donor rose from about 8.2 years for transplants in the late 1990s to roughly 11.7 years for more recent ones. For living-donor kidneys the corresponding figures were 12.1 years and about 19.2 years (Source Notes S10).

That second number is the one to sit with. A living-donor kidney, on average, now lasts nearly two decades. For a young recipient, that can be a childhood and a career. For an older recipient, it can be the rest of life. It also arrives on a day of the surgeon's choosing, with the donor and recipient prepared, the cold ischemia time, the hours an organ spends without blood flow, held to the shortest it can be. The deceased-donor kidney arrives at three in the morning, from a stranger, after a journey in a cooler. The living-donor kidney arrives at nine, from someone who says, I will do this for you.

The trouble is that the supply of such gifts is not growing the way we need it to. Living-donor kidney donation in the United States peaked in 2004 and then declined, with the drop concentrated among men, Black adults, and younger and lower-income adults (Source Notes S11). The problem is not confined to America. In Australia, a 2026 report in the *Medical Journal of Australia* found 253 living-donor kidney transplants in 2024, compared with 354 in 2008, a fall of roughly 29 percent (Source Notes S12). I did not find an equally clean figure for the United States as a whole for the most recent year, and I will not guess one. But a decline in the face of rising need is not what a healthy system looks like.

## What stops a person from saying yes

Ask a hundred people whether they would give a kidney to someone they love, and a startling number will say yes. Ask them again after the first conversation with a transplant coordinator, and the number shrinks. The reasons are rarely hidden. Some are medical. Many are logistical. Lost wages. Travel and lodging. Child care during recovery. Fear of what the donation might do to future insurance. A donor can be a hero and also be unable to afford the weeks of recovery that follow.

Congress has begun to respond. The Honor Our Living Donors Act, enacted as part of the Consolidated Appropriations Act of 2026 on the third of February, 2026, directs federal action to broaden the reimbursement of living donors' costs, and the agency responsible published notices that summer seeking comment on how it would be done (Source Notes S13). I will have more to say about how far these measures go in Part Three. Meanwhile private programs have grown up to cover some of the gap, offering wage reimbursement and life-insurance protection to certain donors (Source Notes S14). They are welcome. They are also a reminder of how much of the weight of this gift has been left to charity and chance.

There is a further factor that does not show up in any statute. Many people who would give a kidney do not offer, because they do not know that they are being asked. The patient is often the last person to say it. A woman on the waiting list will go years without asking anyone for a kidney, because it is among the hardest requests a person can make. She does not want to be a burden. She has watched her children's faces. She hopes someone will volunteer.

A program called Live Donor Champion, developed at Johns Hopkins, was built around that silence. Instead of asking the patient to ask, it trains a friend or relative to speak for her, across six monthly sessions, to be her advocate in the community. In a pilot with fifteen candidates and fifteen matched controls, and a follow-up with 163 candidates, those who took part had substantially higher odds of a referral from a potential donor, about 5.5 times higher in the larger analysis (Source Notes S15). The evidence is preliminary, drawn from conference abstracts and a registered study, and not from a randomized trial. But the idea behind it is old and plain: a person is sometimes best helped by someone willing to ask on her behalf.

## The debt in the gift

I return, before I end, to Ronald Herrick.

I do not know what the brothers said to each other, and I will not guess. The record is silent on that, as it usually is on the most important things. But I know what gifts of this size do. They bind. A person who has received a kidney from a brother, a friend, a stranger, carries it as a part of himself and as a fact about his life, and the person who gave it carries a scar and a quiet knowledge. The best of the transplant community understands this. The worst of it forgets that gratitude, like the kidney, can be damaged by neglect.

John's gospel speaks of laying down a life for one's friends. A kidney is not a life. But in a modern world where donors survive and flourish, it is as close as most of us will come to the offer. We should not make it harder than it must be.

## What you can do

If you are waiting for a kidney, do not wait quietly. Tell one person this week that you need one. If you cannot say it, ask someone you trust to say it for you. Programs exist that train friends to do just that. Ask your transplant center whether it offers one.

If you are a potential donor, ask your recipient's center for the donor advocate, the independent person whose job is to protect you. Ask what will be reimbursed and what will not. Write down the answers.

If you are a coordinator or a social worker, make a one-page sheet of the real costs of donation, in dollars, and give it to every person who asks about it, before they ask for it.

If you write laws, remember the first rule of any system built on gifts: it must never punish the givers.
