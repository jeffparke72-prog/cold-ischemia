# Chapter 9: The Hallway
EPIGRAPH: "'Tis better to have loved and lost than never to have loved at all."
BY: Alfred, Lord Tennyson, In Memoriam A.H.H. (1850)

Picture a corridor outside an intensive care unit at two in the morning. The lights are low and the vending machine hums. A woman in a cardigan sits with her hands between her knees. Her son is nineteen. He was in a car, and then he was in this building, and now a neurologist has told her, as gently as the words allow, that he will not wake up.

She has been awake for twenty hours. She has not eaten. She has been told things she has understood and things she has not. And now a second person approaches, a coordinator from the organ procurement organization, wearing a lanyard and a face arranged for this moment, to ask if she has thought about donation.

I have stood, in my life, in more than one corridor like it, and I will leave it at that. What I can tell you is that no room in the American health system carries more weight per square foot than this one. In it, a family's worst hour meets the generosity on which the whole transplant enterprise depends. It is the hinge of the system. If the family says yes, a stranger may live. If the family says yes and then learns that the kidney was never used, a trust has been broken that no press release can mend.

## What families say afterward

The question that matters most is whether people who say yes regret it. The literature on this is modest but consistent. An Australian interview study of 49 potential donor families found that no family that had consented expressed regret about the decision, while some who had declined did. The process was described as stressful, but not stressful enough to undo satisfaction with the choice (Source Notes S47). A review of the wider literature found that dissatisfaction with how hospital staff behaved was more likely to trouble a family than the donation itself (Source Notes S47).

Read that second finding twice. What hurts families, in the long run, is less often the decision than the treatment. The coordinator who hurried them. The nurse who could not look at them. The call that never came afterward. A donor family has given the most extraordinary gift one human being can give, and it is common, in many places, for them to hear nothing at all.

A survey of aftercare for donor families in fifteen European countries found great variation in what is offered, and an analysis of 1,613 letters exchanged between donor families and recipients, presented as a conference abstract, suggested how much both sides value the contact (Source Notes S48). I would not overread a conference abstract. But the pattern fits what every coordinator will tell you: grief that is acknowledged turns into something endurable, and grief that is ignored hardens.

The letters are the part I think about. Somewhere there are shoeboxes of them. *Dear family, I do not know how to begin.* A man who has been given a kidney, trying to write to a mother he will never meet. A mother, years later, reading that her son's kidney has lasted. Whatever else may be said about the system that connects them, those pages are among the most moving documents produced by American medicine, and almost no one outside the field has seen them.

## When trust cracks

Trust is not a sentiment. It is infrastructure, and it can be damaged.

In late October of 2024 the Associated Press reported that people were opting out of organ donor registries after stories about a man in Kentucky who had been mistakenly believed dead. The story was alarming, and the fear it set loose was entirely predictable (Source Notes S49). In the months that followed, the federal agency that oversees the transplant system opened its own inquiry into the organization involved, and in July of 2025 the Department of Health and Human Services released a summary of a review of cases at one procurement organization, reporting concerns in 103 of 351 cases (Source Notes S49). I report the number as the agency reported it. I do not know what proportion of those concerns would be borne out on full examination, and I would counsel patience before drawing grand conclusions. But I am certain about the direction of the harm. When people stop trusting that a donor is truly dead, the supply of organs dries up, and with it, hope for tens of thousands of people.

What the episode showed is that the trust at the center of the system is brittle. It rests on the assumption that the family in the corridor will be told the truth, that the doctors who care for the patient are separate from those who care for the organs, and that the process is watched by someone with the power to stop it. Where that assumption fails, it fails for everyone.

The response, as I read it, has been an attempt to bring the system closer to daylight: federal actions on transparency and accountability for organ procurement organizations, reorganization of the national network, new guidance from the Centers for Medicare & Medicaid Services on the donation process in 2026 (Source Notes S50). Whether these changes hold, and whether they reach the corridor, will not be known for a while. I would say only that a reform that never changes what the coordinator says to the mother in the cardigan is not yet a reform.

## The gift that goes unused

Now I must describe the part of this chapter that I find hardest to write, because it is the part that the families do not know.

Suppose the woman in the corridor says yes. The kidneys are recovered. They are placed on ice, labelled, and offered through the national system to the transplant centers on a ranked list. And then, with startling frequency, someone says no.

Nationally, the share of recovered kidneys that were not transplanted has climbed. According to the national registry, it was 18.2 percent in 2013, and then 26.6 percent in 2022, 27.9 percent in 2023 and 29.3 percent in 2024 (Source Notes S51).

[[FIG:nonuse|Figure 9.1. Share of recovered deceased-donor kidneys not transplanted, United States: 18.2 percent in 2013, 26.6 in 2022, 27.9 in 2023 and 29.3 in 2024.]]

Think about it. Nearly three out of every ten kidneys that families gave, at the worst moment of their lives, were not used. A federal monitoring report on the 2021 change to the allocation system noted that nonuse had risen from about 21 percent to 26 percent in the period around the change (Source Notes S50). Some of the discarded kidneys are in poor condition, and I am not suggesting that every recovered organ is fit for every recipient. Donors are older and sicker than they used to be, as the population is. But the pattern raises the question of what is being lost, and why.

Part of the answer is the behavior of the offering process itself. A literature on offer decisions reports that the median kidney was offered seven times before it found a taker, that a study of 68 physicians found greater variation in decisions within a transplant center than between centers, and that a survey of the agreement among reviewers produced a statistic, a kappa of 0.13, that indicates only slight agreement (Source Notes S52). A kappa that low means that two experienced doctors, shown the same offer, would often disagree. Which is another way of saying that whether a kidney is used can depend on who is on call.

Part of the answer is time. A kidney is a living organ held in a cooler, and the hours matter. A 2026 report in *Transplant International*, studying 74 pairs of kidneys from older donors, found that when the cold ischemia time exceeded twelve hours, the odds of delayed graft function were 6.3 times those when it was under eight (Source Notes S53). Earlier work by Debout and colleagues found that each additional hour in cold storage increased the risk of graft failure and death (Debout et al., 2015)†.

[[FIG:hazard|Figure 9.2. Relative hazard of graft failure and death rising with hours in cold storage, from about 1.0 at six hours to about 1.36 and 1.53 at thirty hours.]]

And the hours depend on logistics. The federal transport page now acknowledges a working group's final report in 2025 with twenty recommendations, and an air-transport website for organs launched in 2025; an industry briefing in 2024 observed that the contract in force could not require organs to be tracked (Source Notes S50). A kidney is a gift. It is also a parcel, and parcels get lost.

## The person with the lanyard

I should speak for a moment about the coordinator in the corridor, because she is usually the villain of the stories families tell, and she is more often a casualty of the same system.

Imagine her night. The call came at midnight, from a hospital three counties away. She drove there in the dark, rehearsing. She has done this many times, and it has not become easier. She reads the chart, finds the nurse, learns the family's name, and waits, sometimes for hours, for the right moment, which does not exist. The professional guidance is that the conversation about donation should be separated from the news of death, so that no family feels pressed. She knows this. She also knows that the window is short and that the person in the bed will not be a donor if she waits too long. She is holding both of these truths while a mother weeps.

Then there is the rest of her night. If the family says yes, she coordinates the surgical teams, the flights, the lab tests, the allocation calls, the paperwork, perhaps for thirty hours without sleep. At some point she will stand in an operating room and watch an organ come out of a boy of nineteen. She will do it again in a week.

I have never met a coordinator who was not marked by the work. Few systems measure what it costs them. And fewer still measure what happens when a good coordinator is replaced by a tired one, in the middle of the night, in the hallway, with a mother. We speak of the supply of organs as though it were a matter of logistics and law. It is also a matter of the nerves of a few thousand people.

If we want families to say yes, and to be glad they did, we must invest in the people who ask. A coordinator who has had training in grief, supervision when she needs it, and the time to do the follow-up she knows she should do, is the best infrastructure the system can buy.

## What a better system has done elsewhere

I do not wish to give the impression that every country does this badly. Spain's approach has been studied for decades: a national organization, and in each hospital a physician coordinator whose job is to identify potential donors and talk with families. In 2025 Spain reported 51.9 deceased donors per million population, close to the record of 52.6 it had set the year before, and roughly 6,300 transplants in all (Source Notes S54). The comparison with the American system should be handled with care, because laws, cultures and counting methods differ. But what the Spanish model shares with the programs that work elsewhere is not technology. It is people, placed where the families are, with the time and the training to treat them like human beings.

It reminds me, as so much of this book does, that the most scalable intervention in medicine is often a person with time.

## What I would ask of the system

I would ask for three things. I would ask for a promise to every donor family that they will hear, in writing, within a month, what happened to every organ: used, and where, or not used, and why. It is a small administrative act and a very large moral one. I would ask for honest, public reporting by every center of how many kidneys it declined and what happened to them. And I would ask that offers come with a clock, so that no kidney is lost for want of a decision.

The woman in the cardigan did not know to ask. Her son's kidneys are, I hope, working somewhere. If they are not, someone owes her an explanation.

## What you can do

If you are a donor family, you are entitled to ask the organ procurement organization what became of the organs. Ask in writing. Ask again if you are not answered. If you wish to write to a recipient, ask whether your organization will forward the letter.

If you are a registered donor, talk to your family. Say your decision aloud, at a dinner, in plain words, so that the woman in the corridor, if she is ever your mother or your wife, does not have to guess.

If you work for an organ procurement organization, send every donor family a follow-up within thirty days. If you work in a transplant center, post your offer acceptance data in a form a family could read.

If you write regulations or oversee them, treat nonuse as an emergency, because for every kidney discarded there is a person on a list.

And if you are someone who has put a heart on a driver's license and moved on, thank you. Do one more thing: make sure someone knows.
