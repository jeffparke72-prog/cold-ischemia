# Prologue: The Man Who Would Not Leave the Room
EPIGRAPH: "To care for him who shall have borne the battle, and for his widow, and his orphan."
BY: Abraham Lincoln, Second Inaugural Address (1865)

In the fall of 1971, in a hearing room on Capitol Hill, a man in a business suit sat down beside a machine and let it take his blood.

His name was Shep Glazer. By the accounts that survive he was in his forties, a salesman, a husband, and the officer of a small organization of patients who depended on dialysis (Source Notes S1). The members of the House Ways and Means Committee had heard a great deal that season about the cost of keeping people like him alive. They had heard it from actuaries and from agency staff, in the dry vocabulary of appropriations. What they had not done, most of them, was watch it. Glazer arranged for them to watch.

I have tried many times to picture the room. The accounts differ in their details, and I will not pretend to a precision they do not support. But the outline is the same in all of them. A man sat at a witness table. Tubing ran from his arm to a machine. His blood left him, passed through a filter, and returned, while he spoke to the men who held the purse. He was not performing a trick. He was making an argument without raising his voice: that this was what staying alive looked like for people with failed kidneys, that it could be done, that it was being done in a few places for a few people who happened to be lucky, and that the luck was the thing he had come to talk about.

Think about what it takes to do that. Think about sitting in front of the most powerful committee in the House, with a needle in your arm, and asking strangers to decide whether you and people like you are worth the trouble. There is no way to do it without a certain terrible calm. He could not leave for the next four hours. He could not argue, and then stalk out, and then think of the thing he should have said. He had to stay, and let them look, and keep his voice even.

A year later, in October of 1972, President Nixon signed a large bill that touched Social Security in many ways, and one provision of it, tucked in among hundreds, did something that no nation had done. It extended Medicare, a program built for the old, to anyone of any age whose kidneys had failed (Source Notes S1). On the first of July, 1973, the promise took effect. The country had decided, in a few paragraphs and with remarkably little argument, that a person should not die for want of a machine.

I want you to hold on to the word *promise*, because the rest of this book is an audit of it.

The people who wrote that provision believed they were solving a problem with a boundary. The best estimates of the day, as the story is usually told, held that perhaps ten thousand Americans would need the benefit, at a cost to the government of something over a hundred million dollars (Source Notes S1). By 2012 the Medicare program was paying for more than 525,000 people with kidney failure, at a cost above $28 billion a year (Source Notes S1). By 2023 the figure had grown to $55.3 billion in a single year, and the share of the population living with kidney failure, adjusted for age and sex, stood at 2,327 for every million Americans (Source Notes S2). The line between those two numbers is the story of a promise that proved far larger than the people who made it.

You might expect a book that begins this way to be a celebration. Part of it is. The promise worked in the plainest sense. People who would have died in the early 1970s lived, in numbers that no one at that hearing table could have counted, and they raised children, and buried their parents, and grew old. Anyone who dismisses that has forgotten, or never learned, what the alternative looked like.

But a promise is not what it says on paper. A promise is what the people on the receiving end can actually count on. And when you set out to measure the American promise to the kidney patient by what the patient lives through, rather than what the statute provides, the picture changes. A machine and a payment are not a life. A life has afternoons in it, and a body, and a front door a certain distance from the nearest chair, and a person on the other side of the dinner table who does not know what to say.

This book walks through the places where the promise and the person come apart. It begins, in Part One, with where the promise came from, because I have found that nobody improves a thing they do not understand, and because the history is more moving and more strange than most people who work inside it have been told. It goes on, in Part Two, to the people the promise has been hardest on: those whose afternoons it took, whose bodies it changed, whose roads it lengthened. In Part Three it turns to the gift at the center of transplantation, the strangers who give a kidney and the families who give an organ in their worst hour, and to what we do with those gifts once they are given. And in Part Four it looks ahead, to the machines and the biology that could make much of this obsolete, and to the smaller, more human obligations that no machine will remove.

I should tell you what I am and what I am not. I am not a clinician. I am not a politician. I have sat in the rooms this book describes, at more than one side of the table, over more than one stretch of years. I will not tell you my own story, because it belongs to me and to the few people who carried me through it, and because the people who fill these pages deserve the room. What I can offer is the habit of attention that those years teach. You learn to notice who is waiting. You learn what a waiting room sounds like at night. You learn that the thing nobody says out loud is usually the thing that matters.

Where I have invented a figure to stand for many, I will say so. The people in this book who are named are named because their stories are in the public record. The rest are composites built from what patients, families, nurses and researchers have described, and I mark them plainly.

Three promises of my own. I will not use a number to frighten you when a sentence will do. I will not hide the limits of what is known, and in this field there are many. And I will end every chapter by asking what you, or a clinic, or a community, or a lawmaker, can do next, because a book that makes you feel something and then gives you nowhere to put it has done only half its work.

Glazer finished his treatment that day and, so far as I know, went home. The committee did what it did. The history books say that the room was moved. I have never read an account that says how he felt, driving home with a pressure bandage on his arm, having shown a roomful of strangers the thing he would otherwise have shown no one. I like to think he felt the particular tiredness of a man who has said what he came to say.

He was asking, as we all are, to be counted. We will see whether the country kept its word.
